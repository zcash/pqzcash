Ragu PCS Pareto

Ragu as a PCD scheme, priced with its Pedersen-IPA decider today, with the lattice scheme Akita under five field and modulus choices, and with the hash-based schemes: WHIR over Pasta, over Goldilocks, and with Pasta claims in 64‑bit limbs; a Plonky3-style FRI over Goldilocks; the Flock/Binius-style binary-field family; and BitZ, each with ARC accumulation and with its own recursion, a draft of BitZ's arithmetization over Akita, and Flock-style Boolean circuits over Akita's binary groups, experimental. Each point is one configuration; its coordinates are compressed proof bytes, prover work, and a third measure: the merge circuit's gates or the atoms that it absorbs, the decider's verification work, or the prover's work for a whole transaction. The hash-based rows' rate and grinding, the security floor, zero knowledge, and the hashes allowed in circuits (conventional only, or Poseidon 1.1 too) are selectable. Each class, pre-quantum (Pedersen-IPA) and post‑quantum (the rest), has its own frontier: the configurations that nothing else in the class beats on all three at once, where native support for the Pasta base field counts as a requirement rather than a cost.

Frontiers

The table lists the configurations of each class that are not dominated within that class on bytes and the selected second and third measures together, among the selection above, and also each family's own frontier, marked "family" in the last column, so that every scheme's best configurations appear even where its class dominates it. Header size only adds cost; the post‑quantum rows are at 2 elements, and for Pedersen-IPA select one to compare at a fixed shape.

Pasta as a requirement. Native support for the Pasta base field, which the Ironwood recovery circuit needs, enters the dominance test as a requirement rather than a cost. A configuration that has it (Pedersen-IPA, the Pasta variants of Akita, WHIR over Pasta and the limb hybrid, BitZ and its draft frontend over Akita) is dominated only by another that has it, while a 128‑bit, Goldilocks, 𝔽₂ or binary-groups configuration can be dominated by any. So the post‑quantum frontier always keeps the best Pasta-native points beside the cheaper other-field ones, and the table's Pasta column says which is which.

Which measures. The second and third measures are chosen independently from the same seven, the accumulation prover by default for the second and merge gates for the third, and the frontier is taken on bytes and both. The accumulation prover is what one fuse costs the prover beyond its own step: the merge circuits' commitments plus the fold's sum-checks over the accumulator; prover work is the commitments alone, the Prover work knob below. Merge gates is the default third measure since every family has an estimate of them. Accumulation atoms is kept because it is the more robust proxy for the lattice merge, whose gate count is estimated. For the hash-based families atoms say nothing, since their merge cost is the Merkle hashing. Decider work is the decider's verification cost, milliseconds for the lattice and binary-field families and for the hash-based deciders with BLAKE3 trees, tens for Pedersen-IPA and, with Poseidon trees, for WHIR over Goldilocks, and tens to over a hundred for WHIR over Pasta, so it rarely decides a frontier, but it is there to check. Transaction prover is the expected work to prove the application chosen below, the Ironwood recovery circuit, a transaction with four recovery inputs, a number of Merkle path checks, or a Tachyon transaction of 2 to 16 tachyactions in Tachyon's own proof tree, costed in each family's own arithmetization: its steps at the family's per-step commitment cost, as many as the application takes, so more where Pallas arithmetic must be emulated; one merge per step at the row's accumulation prover; and, for the zero-knowledge rows, the masking work at the handoff to the public aggregator. The Transaction prover paragraph below gives each application's cost in each arithmetization.

Parameter changes against design changes. When configurations that need code or design changes are included, each class's frontier is the union of its frontier over all of them and its frontier over the parameter-only ones, so that what the changes buy stays visible. The table's last column says which frontier a row is on. The per-family box collapses each family to its own frontier instead, so that a dominated family still shows its best configurations.

Rank. The circuit budget below limits the endoscalings per step and the binders at each rank, which makes rank a real trade-off for Pedersen-IPA. A larger circuit holds more endoscalings and fewer binders, which removes committed components and with them bytes and atoms, while the IPA grows by 128 bytes per rank and every remaining polynomial doubles. Rank 14 with the fixed circuits packed beats rank 13 on all three measures; ranks 15 and 16 buy bytes and atoms with prover work. For Akita the step circuits' witnesses open as one batch, so with Poseidon the rank moves bytes and atoms only through each circuit's own evaluation claim and the last circuit's unused capacity, a few hundred bytes across the range; with BLAKE3 the claims' absorption makes the rank decisive, as the Hashes paragraph says. Mostly the field choice trades: smaller fields buy bytes and prover work at the cost of atoms absorbed per merge. Rank 12 has no Pedersen-IPA rows, since a 1,024-gate circuit holds no binder challenge, and no Akita rows under BLAKE3; it gives BitZ a slightly smaller proof and merge prover. Rank 17 helps Akita under BLAKE3, where fewer circuits absorb fewer claims: 14 to 29 percent off the merge prover and a few kilobytes off the proof. The WHIR-type rows depend on the rank only through their circuit count, so they are shown at ranks 13 to 16. The bit arithmetizations, BitZ and the draft frontend, whose steps commit 2rank bits or digits, go on to ranks 24 and 23, where one step holds the recovery circuit: their merge is nearly a fixed cost, so the transaction measure falls with the rank until then, while the proof and the merge prover barely move, and padding a step beyond the application would only cost.

The hash-based families. The figures in this paragraph are with Poseidon trees, the arithmetic-hash option; the Hashes paragraph below gives the BLAKE3 ones. WHIR with ARC accumulation is dominated by Akita on every measure: with the grinding chosen per row its proofs are 197 to 831 KB, its prover is 16 to 650 times Pedersen-IPA's, and its merge is 0.6 to 3.1 million gates, 20 to 140 times Akita's. The proof is the decider's. At the root nobody has yet combined the two child accumulators with the root's own witness, so the decider's proof opens those three oracles in its first round, each under its own Merkle root (Ulrich Haböck's point). A stand-alone WHIR proof of the same witness is 177 KB at rate 1/8 and rank 13. The two extra first rounds bring it to 326, with their paths pruned as a multiproof, as the reference implementation already prunes its own. The lower rates and the grinding, added after Ulrich Haböck's questions, take a third off its proof at rate 1/32 against rate 1/8 (226 against 326 KB at rank 13) and a third off its merge, at two and a half times the encoding and hashing. The sizes are extrapolated from measurements at 217, 218 and 220; the growth per doubling is not uniform, since a folding round is added every two doublings, and the three-doubling slope used here is about nine KiB against five over the first doubling alone, which is why these rows are larger than the earlier page showed. Goldilocks cuts its prover by about five times and its proof by a sixth, and grows its merge, since a Goldilocks Poseidon costs more gates per node. The Pasta-claims-with-64‑bit-limbs hybrid, unlike Akita's 64‑bit commitment variant, gains nothing: the Pasta merge circuit forces Pasta's hash on the trees, and four limbs per coefficient is more hashing, not less. WHIR over Pasta is dominated by Akita's Pasta variants, which is a like-for-like comparison. What the hash-based route has that Akita does not is a native Pasta field with no design change beyond the commitment scheme, and a prover whose hashing is cheap with a conventional hash; the default view prices that, and the Hashes paragraph says what the recursion then costs.

ARC against each scheme's own recursion. Every hash-based scheme appears twice, with ARC accumulation and, in a lighter shade of the same colour, with full recursion: the merge circuit verifies its two input proofs, each step emits a full proof, and the decider verifies one. The hash and the field are the same; the proofs differ, since the recursive decider verifies one stand-alone proof while the ARC decider's first round opens three oracles, so the recursive rows' proofs are a little over half the ARC rows' (181 against 326 KB for WHIR at rate 1/8 and rank 13). For WHIR the recursion is about a wash on the prover at rate 1/8: the verifier's hashes per input match ARC's openings through the tree's levels (2,600 against 86 × 26 without grinding, and both fall with it), and its merge is two fifths larger. At rate 1/2 it saves a third of the prover and a twentieth of the merge, because the verifier's queries fall with the rate while ARC's openings grow with it. FRI's verifier has twice WHIR's hashes, so its recursion costs about twice ARC. Ligerito's verifier, under BitZ, opens 491 columns over its five levels at 100 bits, against ARC's 86 openings at rate 1/8, so its recursion is seven times ARC's hashing (200 against 28 million witness bits at rate 1/8), and the proof grows with the merge; the ARC row is the better one for BitZ. The 𝔽₂ family's recursion verifies its own FRI-Binius proof, whose ring-switching verifier costs 2ℓ′·2κ field multiplications. So its merge is 85 million witness bits in the 192‑bit design, 134 million in the 191‑bit one, whose one-step ring switching costs the verifier 191 field multiplications per variable rather than 64, and 224 million in the 256‑bit one, against 25 million under ARC, at two and a half to seven times the prover, with the verifier's paths pruned as a multiproof. Its proof is a quarter smaller (123 against 169 KiB at rate 1/8).

What the axes mean

  • Proof bytes. The compressed PCD proof. For Pedersen-IPA it is counted from Ragu's structs: the instance's commitments and scalars, two revdot reductions, two batches, and two IPAs with one round per bit of the rank; the checked-in configuration gives 7,908 bytes, which Ragu's wire format confirms (7,936 with its 28‑byte envelope). The two online polynomial commitments and six openings that every Ragu proof now carries are 448 of those bytes. For Akita it is the planner's byte estimate for the decider's opening of one final group plus the separately committed groups, from the estimation runs, plus an instance of 128-byte commitments, two claims per group, and one claim per step circuit. For WHIR with ARC it is the WHIR proof that decides the final accumulator, measured with the reference implementation over a 256‑bit field at 128‑bit security in the proven Johnson regime, at the batched witness size, at the selected rate and grinding, plus the two extra first-round trees of the decider's batched opening and the accumulator's roots and claims. For the 𝔽₂ family it is the optimizer's proof as the binary-field section describes, and for BitZ its Table 10 proof with the Zinc+ PIOP's, each with the extra trees under ARC. For the binary-groups family it is Akita's measured opening of a binary source, extrapolated to the merge's bits.
  • Accumulation prover. The prover work below plus the fold's own sum-checks: Akita's two norm and range sum-checks run over the accumulator, K groups of 2rank coefficients in digits, at about five field multiplications per digit a pass, the draft frontend's measured Booleanity sum-check (437.7 ms for 227 bits on one core, 9.8 word multiplications per 64‑bit digit), plus the fold's own linear combination of the digit vectors at one multiplication per digit. So this measure grows with the rank where the commitments alone do not, since the merge circuit is fixed and only chopped differently, and it is the measure the transaction prover charges per merge. The other rows add nothing: Pedersen-IPA's fuse is measured whole and its native folding, the batching of about 95 polynomials and a 100-point linear combination, is about one percent of it; the hash-based rows' ARC step already hashes the new accumulator; and the binary-groups row's measured opening includes its sum-checks.
  • Accumulator memory. What the prover holds between fuses, in bytes. A fuse holds the step's witness and one or two of them: two when both children are real proofs, one when the other child is the bootstrap proof, the fixed base case that Ragu's circuits recognise by its dummy suffix, as in a chain or in Tachyon's action steps. For the lattice rows it is the folded digit vector, K groups of 2rank coefficients in digits, each digit stored at the width of its accumulator's norm bound. The certified ℓ₂ bound at the handoff is about 219 (the zero-knowledge note's ledger), which bounds every entry, so 20 bits and a sign. Each fold multiplies the entries by up to 40, so an accumulator a level below the top needs five bits fewer. The measure is of the top level. The sum-checks expand a digit to an element of the commitment field on the fly, 8 bytes at 64 bits, 16 at 128, 32 at Pasta's modulus, which is computation, not storage. Pedersen-IPA's is the uncompressed proof, every committed polynomial's 2rank Pasta coefficients, 26.5 MB measured at rank 13 against 24.9 MB counted. The hash-based rows under ARC hold the accumulator's codeword, the witness over the rate in the field's elements, and its Merkle tree; under their own recursion only the previous proof. BitZ and the 𝔽₂ family hold the packed codeword with a tree of 256‑byte leaves, and the binary-groups row the packed bits. At rank 17 Akita with Pasta claims holds about 175 MB, the draft frontend at rank 21 about 33 MB, and Pedersen-IPA at rank 13 25 MB.
  • Prover work. Native 64‑bit word multiplications for one step's commitments, with every committed polynomial taken as dense. Pedersen over Pasta: a Pippenger multi-scalar multiplication costs about 255/rank mixed additions per coefficient, so the cost falls as 1/rank, and the constant is calibrated to Ragu's measured fuse at the checked-in configuration: 1.46 s on one core of an Apple M4 Max for the step's circuit and its 91 recursion circuits with their stage polynomials, 96 commitments of 213 coefficients, which is about 5,600 word multiplications per coefficient at rank 13 and 5,200 at rank 14, over all of the step's polynomials. The earlier estimate, 11 field multiplications of 32 word multiplications per addition, was 24 percent higher; udon's AArch64 assembly, which Ragu does not enable, takes 13 percent off the measured time. Ajtai by NTT: per digit about rows/64 pointwise multiplications plus 3 for its share of the transform, at the modulus width, which is 32 word multiplications at 255 bits, 8 at 128, 2 at 64; with 85 digits per Pasta coefficient that is about 14,000 per coefficient when the commitment modulus is Pasta, 2,000 when it is 64‑bit, 2,600 at a 128‑bit field, and 400 on Goldilocks, over every step circuit's witness. The cycle's endoscaling and binder polynomials do not exist in the lattice design. Folding and sum-check work is not included. For WHIR with ARC it is the Reed–Solomon encoding by FFT over the witness divided by the rate, plus the Merkle trees of the witness and of the new accumulator, hashed by BLAKE3 at 200 word multiplications per compression by default, or under the arithmetic-hash option by Poseidon at about 35,000 per permutation over a 255‑bit field, since natively its matrix multiplications are full multiplications too; at rate 1/8 that is about 180,000 per coefficient with Poseidon and a few thousand with BLAKE3, but the circuits that a BLAKE3 merge needs multiply the committed witness, which is where the prover's cost then goes. The 𝔽₂ family's prover is counted as the binary-field section describes, a carry-less multiplication as one word multiplication, and BitZ's is its measured Table 10 prover. The binary-groups family's is the measured commit and open times per committed bit, taken for one thread, plus Flock's PIOP.
  • Accumulation atoms. An atom is one element of the merge circuit's native field. A configuration's accumulation atoms are the atoms that the merge circuit absorbs per fold, to derive the fold's challenges from the two proofs that it merges. The count stands in for the merge circuit's size: each absorbed atom costs hashing constraints, and the Pedersen coordinates also feed the point arithmetic. For Pedersen-IPA it is two atoms per commitment folded, the x and y coordinates, over the commitments of both instances. For Akita it is the sum-check messages of the range and norm checks, about 120 elements for two sum-checks of about 20 rounds with three elements each, counted as one atom each, plus each compressed commitment image: 128 bytes at the variant's atom width, which is 8 atoms at a 128‑bit field, 16 on Goldilocks, 4 on Pasta, and 6 for Pasta claims with 64‑bit commitment coordinates, three coordinates to an atom; plus one atom per step circuit's claim, and 18 more with zero knowledge. The folding arithmetic is not included, and for Pedersen-IPA neither is the endoscaling arithmetic, which is what a higher endoscaling count costs in gates. For the hash-based families, with ARC or with their own recursion, the merge absorbs only the accumulators' or input proofs' roots and claims, about 40 atoms, which is why atoms are the wrong measure for them.
  • Merge gates. The gates synthesized across every circuit of one merge: measured for Pedersen-IPA, and estimated for Akita and for WHIR with ARC as the Circuit budget section describes, and for the binary-field families, the binary-groups family and the draft frontend counted in AND gates, witness bits or digits as their sections describe. It is the default third measure. In the 2D view the marker's size follows the logarithm of the third measure over the plotted points, from 5 to 22 pixels, so the sizes read across any selection; the binary-field and BitZ families' witness-bit counts sit at the top of that range. Markers over 16 pixels carry a small contrasting cross at their centre, so that they can be read against the axes. The swatches in the families list are sized like each family's median plotted marker, up to 18 pixels, so the list carries the same cue.
  • Decider work. The decider's verification, in the same word-multiplication equivalents as the prover, at 3e9 per second of one core, which is also what the microsecond option assumes. Pedersen-IPA is modelled: the two IPAs each need a multi-scalar multiplication of 2rank points, plus a scalar multiplication per folded commitment to combine the instance, about 49 ms at rank 13. Akita is measured: the paper's single-thread verifier takes 4.2 ms at the 128‑bit field and 3.9 ms on Goldilocks at 227 committed bits, growing by about 0.4 ms per doubling, so 2.5 to 3 ms at this witness size; the Pasta variants are taken at four times the 128‑bit figure, the ratio of word multiplications per field multiplication, since the verifier's work is sum-check arithmetic in the claim field, about 11 ms. With zero knowledge the final accumulator is at the ordinary length and the terminal's bounds are unchanged, so the verification is unchanged. The hash-based deciders are the WHIR verifier's hashes at the witness size, measured, at the variant's native permutation cost, plus about a million for the arithmetic, and in the ARC rows the decider's two extra first-round trees. At rate 1/8 and rank 13 that is 13 ms on Goldilocks and 62 ms with Poseidon over Pasta under ARC, and 7 and 31 ms under recursion, and about a millisecond in every case with BLAKE3 trees; FRI has 2.07 times the hashes. The 𝔽₂ family's decider is the FRI-Binius verifier: the proof's authentication hashes over pruned paths and its leaf hashes at BLAKE3's native cost plus its field multiplications, 0.1 to 0.4 ms. BitZ is its Table 10 verifier at the merge's bits, 2.4 ms at 225, scaled with the floor and, in the ARC rows, by the decider's two extra first-round trees, plus 2 ms for Zinc+'s PIOP. The binary-groups family's decider is Akita's measured verifier for a binary source, at the paper's slope, 5 ms at the merge's bits.

Circuit budget

A step circuit at rank r holds 2r−2 gates and four times as many constraints, 2,048 and 8,192 at rank 13, and the gate limit binds first. The Pedersen-IPA figures are the internal circuits' synthesis counts pinned in Ragu's tests at rank 13 (tachyon-zcash/ragu 8109a41c). An endoscaling step of four points costs 1,995 gates, taken as 451 per point plus 191, since only the four-point step is pinned. A binder costs 2,032 to 2,048 gates for two of the eight challenges, taken as 590 per challenge plus 860 of overhead; that split is derived, not measured. The fixed circuits are hashes_1 (1,793 gates), hashes_2 (1,874), collapse (1,455, the two collapses merged), compute_v (1,948), bind_beta (1,889) and bind_endoscalar (940) on the native side, and collapse (1,598, with the export merged in), compute_v (1,504) and loading (566) on the nested side. Packing them into a larger rank's budget is first-fit decreasing on these counts, and merging circuits is a design change rather than a parameter.

The Akita merge circuit is estimated, not measured. Its transcript hashing is priced at the selected hash: BLAKE3 at 14,900 gates per 64 bytes absorbed, the sum-check elements and the claims at the claim field's width and the six commitment images at 128 bytes each, or Poseidon absorbing four elements per 288-gate permutation, 72 gates per accumulation atom. Folding each child's 128-byte commitment image needs a modular reduction, taken as one gate per bit. The sum-check verifier's arithmetic is about 400 gates. The step circuits are that total divided by the rank's budget, rounded up; each circuit's own evaluation claim is absorbed by the next merge, so the count is a fixed point. Under BLAKE3 a 16-byte claim costs about 3,700 gates to absorb and a 32-byte one 7,500, more than a rank-13 or rank-14 circuit holds, so those ranks have no Akita rows under the default, nor rank 15 for the Pasta variants. Splitting a compression across circuits is taken as free, although a rank below 16 cannot hold one in a single circuit. Merge gates, in the hover text and the table, is the total over every circuit of one merge.

The hash-based merge is also estimated. A hash commitment is not homomorphic, so Ragu's split accumulation does not apply; ARC accumulates Reed–Solomon proximity claims instead. Its verifier checks 2(λ − g)/log2(1/ρ) Merkle openings per input at rate ρ with g bits of grinding in the query phase, in the proven, Johnson-bound regime: at λ = 128 and 16 bits of grinding, 224, 112, 75, 56, 45 and 38 at rates 1/2 to 1/64, and 256, 128 and 85 at 1/2, 1/4 and 1/8 without grinding. The factor of two would go away only under the list-decoding conjectures refuted in late 2025. Each opening is a path through the accumulator's tree over the witness divided by the rate, one hash per node, a BLAKE3 compression at 14,900 gates by default or a Poseidon permutation at 288 under the option, and a merge has two inputs. The step circuits follow as for Akita, with the tree's depth growing with the batched witness, which is why the number of circuits is in the hundreds. WARP has the same opening count for Reed–Solomon codes and a linear-time prover only with linear-time codes, which does not help here, since the cost is the hashing rather than the encoding. Verifying a WHIR proof in the circuit instead of accumulating, the other route, costs about the same hashing for a single oracle and more with the oracles of a real polynomial IOP, with the proof of each step's growing witness fed back into the next step.

Binary-field systems

The binary-field, sum-check-based systems of 2025 and 2026, Flock (arXiv 2607.27491) and Binius64 on the FRI-Binius and Ligerito commitments, and Blaze on repeat-accumulate-accumulate codes, are the efficient end of the hash-based line. They are Boolean arithmetizations over 𝔽₂ with claims in 𝔽2128, so they have no 2rank Pasta circuits and the rank filter does not apply to them; the Flock/Binius-style family is priced in its own terms. Its merge is ARC's openings as for the other hash-based variants, each Merkle node a BLAKE3 compression of about 213 AND gates, so the merge is a Boolean witness of that many gates, 14 to 37 million from rate 1/64 to 1/4, and its merge-gates figure is a count of AND gates that is not comparable with Pasta gates; bytes and prover work are comparable. Proof bytes come from Ulrich Haböck's FRI-Binius proof-size optimizer, in three designs that each row names: the witness packed into 𝔽2256, which is also the field of the folding and sum-check challenges; packed into 𝔽264 with the challenges drawn from its cubic extension 𝔽2192, which is the field that the agreement bound sees, as Plonky3's FRI pairs a base field with an extension; or packed straight into GF(2191), a prime-degree field with the trinomial x191 + x9 + 1 and no subfields, which Ulrich Haböck suggested for its arithmetic. The proof is at 128 bits of security in the list-decoding regime up to the Johnson bound with the largest list that the correlated-agreement bound allows (the simplified BCHKS25 bound; BCIKS20 for unique decoding), with the codeword's length counted in field elements, the Guruswami–Sudan multiplicity, the selected grinding, and the folding schedule chosen to minimise the compressed proof, with Merkle-cap multiproofs. Checked against the reference WHIR implementation at the same witness, field, regime and grinding, its query counts are within one to five of WHIR's and its proofs about a tenth smaller, from the optimised schedule. A 128‑bit field cannot reach 128 bits in any regime at these sizes, since the agreement term alone is about 100 bits, which is why FRI-Binius's published figures are at 100. Ring switching (ePrint 2024/504, §1.3) packs 2κ bits into each element and assumes a power-of-two degree over 𝔽₂, which 192 and 191 are not; so the 192‑bit design packs into 𝔽264 (κ = 6) and needs the ring-switching sum-check restated over the tensor product of the two fields, and the 191‑bit design lays the witness out as 191 columns, which the PIOP batches and the ring switching takes in one step of degree 191; both are design changes that the paper does not write down. The first-round oracle is the packed witness and has the same bytes under any packing, so the designs differ in the later rounds' elements, 24 bytes against 32, and in the agreement margin, which sets the list: 220 to 221 at 256 bits, 28 to 29 at 192 and one less at 191, which costs them no query at these rates; so the 192‑ and 191‑bit proofs are five to eight percent smaller than the 256‑bit ones (169 and 170 against 183 KiB at rate 1/8), and equal to each other. Under ARC the decider's first round opens three oracles, the two child accumulators and the root's witness, each under its own root, which the optimizer prices and schedules for. Prover work is counted in word multiplications, a 64‑bit carry-less multiplication as one. The PIOP is Flock's measured 82,100 BLAKE3 compressions per second on one core less its commitment (its Table 5: witness generation 5%, zero-check and lincheck 46%), with the zero-check and lincheck, which run over 𝔽2128, scaled by half the challenge field's multiplication cost over 𝔽2128's (4, 8 and 11 carry-less multiplications per product in 128, 192 and 256 bits, Karatsuba over the 64‑bit limbs with the limb reductions, and 7 in GF(2191), whose one trinomial reduction is cheaper than the tower's six). The commitment is counted operation by operation: the additive NTT of the first oracle, with a 32‑bit subfield twiddle per limb in the tower fields and a full-field twiddle in GF(2191), which has no subfield to hold the evaluation domain, and its tree; the ring switching, two passes of a challenge-field element by each witness bit and a sum-check over the packed coefficients; the folds, the folded trees and the BaseFold sum-check; in carry-less multiplications, BLAKE3 compressions and word XORs at an eighth each, scaled to the FRI-Binius paper's single-threaded 144 ms commitment and 160 ms opening at 228 bits, rate 1/2, over 𝔽2128 (its Table 2), which the counts reproduce at 1.1 and 1.7 times. An ARC step pays the NTT, two trees and the ring switching; a recursive step a full proof, and its merge verifies two, the field multiplications by Karatsuba. So the prover grows with the inverse rate below 1/4, as the other hash-based rows do: with the grinding chosen per row it is 430 to 630 million word multiplications at rates 1/4 to 1/2, 450 to 570 million at 1/8 and 1/16, and 1.4 billion at 1/64 in the 192‑bit design; the 256‑bit one a tenth more; and the 191‑bit one six percent less at rate 1/2 but three to ten percent more from rate 1/8 down, where the NTT's full-field twiddles outweigh its cheaper reduction and half-size folds; against 150 million for Flock's whole measured pipeline at this witness size and its own rate 1/2 over 𝔽2128. The decider's verifier is the proof's authentication hashes, with the paths pruned as a multiproof, and its leaf hashes at BLAKE3's native cost plus its field multiplications, 0.1 to 0.4 ms. Zero knowledge is modelled in two variants, a batched mask and a Zook-style one, which the zero-knowledge paragraph below describes. It does not support the Pasta base field, so under the dominance rule it can be dominated by anything. Its proofs under ARC are 105 to 235 KiB from rate 1/64 to 1/4 in the 192‑bit design, with the three first-round trees (rate 1/2 is dominated by 1/4 on every measure and pruned), and under recursion 75 to 165 KiB; the prover is least at rates 1/4 to 1/8, 430 to 445 million word multiplications, eight times Akita over Goldilocks's with Poseidon and a third of it with BLAKE3, whose proof is 68 to 71 KB; and the merge is 14 to 37 million AND gates against Akita's 25 thousand Pasta gates with Poseidon, or 0.83 million with BLAKE3. The price of the field is the application: Pasta arithmetic in a Boolean circuit, or in any small field, pays the embedding and simulation overhead that the small-field survey (ePrint 2026/1371, §5) quantifies, and the Ironwood recovery circuit is Pasta arithmetic; the transaction measure prices that at about 1,700 AND gates per Pasta gate.

BitZ. BitZ (ePrint 2026/2141) removes that price. It is a hash-based commitment for multilinears over any finitely generated ring. The witness is bit-decomposed and committed packed over 𝔽2128 with Flock's Ligerito. An evaluation claim over a ring R is lifted to the integers, through a random prime where R is large, and proved in the exponent of 𝔽2128 by batched GKR grand products. So its cost is per committed bit and does not depend on R. With the Universal Constraint Systems of Zinc+ (ePrint 2026/855) the application is native over Pasta's field: integer witnesses with ideal membership modulo p, projected to a random prime field for the PIOP. So the Ironwood recovery circuit stays Pasta arithmetic: a field multiplication is 255 bits of value and 255 of quotient, 510 committed bits, and a hash gate is one bit, so the recovery circuit is about 8.4 million bits, as the Transaction prover paragraph counts. A step of rank r commits 2r bits of application witness, beside the Merkle hashing of the merge, which is most of its witness. The merge is a hybrid circuit: the Merkle hashing over 𝔽₂ as for the 𝔽₂ family, and the GKR and PIOP verifiers native over the binary extension and the projection field, a few thousand constraints that are not counted. Its costs are the paper's Table 10: the PCS at 220 to 231 committed bits on one thread at 100 bits, interpolated at the merge's bits. The paper's own benchmark, run here on one thread, reproduces those bytes exactly at 225 to 228 and gives the scalings. On one thread this machine is 1.3 times the paper's and on ten threads it matches, ten threads giving four to five times the single-thread prover. At 128 bits, which its implementation reaches only in the unique-decoding regime with fold grinding, the Ligerito part of the proof is 1.8 times the 100-bit one and the verifier 1.17 times, while the prover is unchanged. Rate 1/8 has 0.83 of the Ligerito bytes for 1.25 times the prover, and it is the proof-size minimum: at rate 1/16, the lowest the implementation allows, the proof is 3% larger again and the prover 27% slower. The unique-decoding regime is what the implementation certifies at 128 bits; at the Johnson bound it stops at 118 bits, its out-of-domain binding term with one sample. There the proof is 217 KB at 225 and rate 1/2, against 304 KB in the unique-decoding regime at 128 bits. The Ligerito part grows linearly with the bits (1.18 times from 100 to 118), so a Johnson-regime BitZ at 128 bits, which analysis in progress may reach, would be about 231 KB, a quarter less than the stand-alone figure under the rows. The rows keep the certified figures. The prover is the grand-product GKR, 120 of the 150 ms at 225, with Ligerito at 10 to 30 ms: as Quang Dao puts it, the prover with grand-product GKR is just really expensive. Zinc+'s PIOP is added from its Table 4: 1.6 times the PCS prover on the application's share, 2 ms of verifier, and 36 KB of proof. At rank 13 and rate 1/8 with ARC it comes to 500 KB with the decider's three first-round trees, 3.5e8 word multiplications, a 28 million bit merge, and a 7.5 ms decider. That is on the frontier: it supports Pasta natively, and nothing with Pasta claims has both a smaller proof and a cheaper prover. Akita's Pasta variants beat it on proof size, and its merge is a Boolean witness of the 𝔽₂ family's size. BitZ's paper offers succinctness only; its zero-knowledge rows here are derived, as the zero-knowledge paragraph says. Quang Dao notes that BitZ's arithmetization, the bit-decomposed witnesses with claims over any ring, might in future be combined with a lattice backend such as Akita.

LaBinius (ePrint 2026/2103) is a lattice commitment whose modulus is decoupled from the constraint field: the commitment runs over a composite, NTT-friendly modulus, the evaluation claim lives in a binary extension field, and extraction avoids reduction modulo the commitment modulus by working over the number field. It gives a decider only, with no accumulation or recursion layer, and Akita's own development has since replaced its root reduction with binary groups, which the next paragraph plots.

Zero knowledge. With the box ticked, the Akita configurations carry the masked witness of the zero-knowledge note: the user's fold masks the accumulator witness by rejection sampling, which makes that one accumulator about nine times longer and raises the Module-SIS rank of the accumulation matrix about 2.2 times, since the note's extraction argument needs that matrix binding at the masked bound and every accumulator is committed under it. The length stays at the zero-knowledge layer. The aggregator's first fold on a masked accumulator range-checks its nine digit slices and folds them back to the ordinary length with its other inputs, as every lattice fold decomposes and folds, so the merges above that layer and the decider's opening see witnesses of the usual size. The rank stays with the accumulation matrix: Akita's internal levels and its terminal commit the final accumulator's ordinary witness under their own matrices and keep their bounds, and the accumulation matrix's larger images enter the opening only as the first level's auxiliary digits, which are lower order. On this page that is 2.2 times the rows term of every step's prover work and, in the merge priced here, which is one at the zero-knowledge layer, three more rounds in each of its two sum-checks, 18 atoms; the opening and the decider's verification are unchanged. Merges above the zero-knowledge layer pay the rows term only, which the page does not show separately. Not modelled: the masked accumulator itself, about 14 MB against 1.5 MB, which passes from the user to the aggregator rather than into any proof, and the first level's extra auxiliary digits. Refinements that would move these figures: a design that checks the masked fold under a matrix of its own would confine the rank to that layer, but proving the change of matrix is the obstacle (the note's §5.9); the factor nine comes from base-8 digits, a Gaussian width of twelve times the norm of the challenge times the witness, and vectors of 214 coefficients, so Akita's eventual Pasta profile, its digit base and the witness size that the rank sets all change it; and the 18 atoms assume the first fold's range and norm checks run over the nine slices at once. The note's §5.6 prices the mask's parameters. This page takes weight-20 challenges in the accumulation matrix's ring dimension of 512, which cost nothing, for the 2.2 above, against 2.4 with the note's weight-32 example; a Gaussian six times the norm wide, at 7.5 attempts instead of 2.7, would bring the factor to 2.1 and the masked accumulator to 12.6 MB, and the note's §5.8 moves the attempts off the user's device. Pedersen-IPA's blinding costs nothing visible here, so its points do not change. For the WHIR and FRI families the zero‑knowledge rows carry the overhead measured with Plonky3's HVZK-WHIR, which composes Zook (ePrint 2026/391) into a hiding commitment. On one core over KoalaBear with a quintic extension, at rate 1/4 and 100 bits, the proof is 103.6 KB against 30.9 KB at 216 and 110.1 KB against 37.5 KB at 218: an additive 73 KB of mask spot checks. The prover takes 19 to 22 ms more, 15% at 218 and halving per doubling. Zero knowledge is paid once per transaction, at the handoff from the user's private accumulation to the public aggregator. So under ARC, where the final accumulator carries the users' masked rows, the rows add 73 KB scaled with the floor to the decider's proof and about 1,300 hash checks to its verifier, and put the masked prover's extra work, a few percent of one step at these sizes, in the transaction measure's fold; under recursion the public merges and the decider's proof are of public data, so the rows equal the plain rows but for that fold. That difference between the two accumulation styles is structural, not an artefact of the model: recursion seals the private witness at the handoff, since the aggregator verifies the user's proof and discards it, while accumulation carries the users' oracles onward, as a random combination with everyone else's, until the decider opens the final accumulator, so the masks must still be there then. Akita's accumulation has the same shape, and more: the masked bound sizes the accumulation matrix that every step shares, which is why every Akita step pays the rows term; confining that rank to the zero-knowledge layer, with a second matrix and proven switches at the handoff, is worked through in the note's §5.9, where the switch's verifier cost is the obstacle. The mask symbols would be larger over Pasta's field, which is not applied, and whether ARC's accumulation preserves zero knowledge has not been examined, so the ARC merge is left unchanged. Flock and BitZ offer succinctness only, by their own papers; Flock expects zero knowledge could be added at low cost, through zero‑knowledge IOPPs or VEIL. The 𝔽₂ family's zero‑knowledge rows follow the zk option of Ulrich Haböck's optimizer as of 2026-11-12: a mask of the witness's size added in the first round, which doubles that round's leaves and adds a 32-byte blinder to each; later rounds carry neither, since the full-entropy mask, once combined with the witness in that round's sum-check, lets them run in the clear; and a schedule with no reduction opens witness, mask and blinder at the query positions before the reveal. His revision of 2026-11-10 gave each leaf one mask row, which the page priced for a day; asked whether one row suffices, he confirmed that the mask must be as large as the witness and corrected the script. The script does not model the prover, so the rows charge the mask's commitment, equal to the witness's, once per transaction in the fold. At rate 1/8 that is 27 KiB more proof under ARC (196 against 169 KiB) and two thirds of a step's prover in the fold; under recursion the rows equal the plain rows but for that fold, as for WHIR. He adds that the leaf blinders could probably be dropped, since the mask has full entropy at leaf level, but keeps them because they make the analysis clean; and that only the private witness needs masking, not the public polynomials, so the three first-round oracles that the ARC decider opens masked here are an upper bound. An earlier revision opened the mask in a separate round, which cost 103 KiB under ARC. The second variant, labelled Zook-style and derived rather than measured, prices Zook's masks (ePrint 2026/391) for FRI-Binius's own structure. The main oracle's zero‑knowledge encoding costs nothing in the proof, since its random coefficients fit the witness padding. Each folding round's sum-check variables get a group of four-coefficient masks, each with its own randomness, encoded at rate 1/256 and spot-checked 16 times through a pruned multiproof; the ring-switching claim's revealed element gets a mask of 2κ coefficients; and the terminal takes a full mask of its own size. FRI-Binius has no code switching and no out-of-domain samples, which are what make WHIR's masks large, since their message must hold the previous encoding's randomness, about a hundred coefficients; so its masks stay small, and as they belong to the proof rather than to the oracles, ARC's three trees do not multiply them. That gives 27 to 35 KiB more proof under ARC, with the masks' encoding in the fold, and nothing beyond the fold under recursion. Under ARC these rows cost a little more proof than the batched mask from rate 1/8 down (30 against 27 KiB at 1/8) and less at rate 1/4, and a hundredth of a step's prover against two thirds. Ulrich Haböck's view is that the full-entropy mask in a batching step before FRI is the way to randomize FRI-Binius, and that a more frugal mask would need the first rounds' sum-checks made zero-knowledge too, which is what these rows price; so they stay as the derived lower estimate, and the one cross-check is that the same count reproduces Plonky3's measured 73 KB for WHIR. In the Zook-style rows the ring-switching mask is this page's design, not the paper's, and in both variants the ARC merge is left unchanged, as for WHIR. So the fold grows with the rank where the merges shrink, and for the recovery circuit the frontend's zero-knowledge optimum is rank 18, 2.5 s per note against 0.41 s without, 1.4 s of it the sampling at 300 ns a sample. Its projection rows reveal coordinates and drop out, as the binary-groups family does, and as Pedersen-IPA drops out at 147 bits. BitZ's zero-knowledge rows are derived, since its paper offers succinctness only. Ligero's random row per Ligerito level puts one symbol in every opened column, so that the columns reveal nothing: 16 bytes per opened column in the decider's proof under ARC, about 20 KB at 128 bits with the three first-round trees, and nothing under recursion. A Libra-style mask polynomial per sum-check, for Flock's zero-check and lincheck, Zinc+'s PIOP and each layer of the GKR, a sum of univariates of degree three in each variable, is committed as extra rows of the same Ligerito matrix under those random rows and opened with the witness, so that each sum-check runs over the masked polynomial. Grinding. The hash-based families can spend proof of work in the query phase: with g bits of grinding the queries need carry only λ − g bits, so ARC's openings per input fall to 2(λ − g)/log2(1/ρ), and WHIR's own query counts fall in the same way. A fixed setting applies to every hash-based row at once, from none, 16, 20 and 24 bits; the default, best per row, gives each configuration the measured setting with the smallest prover, from a wider set of measurements, and the row's label says which. Each row's proof size is measured at its setting with the reference implementation over both fields, rates 1/2 to 1/64, at 217, 218 and 220, with the growth per doubling taken over the three doublings (the earlier rows were measured at 20 bits while ARC's openings were counted without grinding, which this corrects). At rate 1/8 over the 256‑bit field the proof is 143 KiB with no grinding, 127 at 16 bits, 123 at 20 and 119 at 24; at rate 1/32 it is 110, 98, 93 and 91 KiB. The prover pays 2g evaluations of the grinding hash per merge, taken as the family's own Merkle hash so that the merge circuit's check is one node per input. Each bit of grinding removes about one opening in a hundred, so about a percent of the circuits and of the prover, while the grinding's cost doubles; the prover is least where the two cross, at 17 to 19 bits for families with Poseidon trees (217 permutations are about half a percent of a merge's prover at rate 1/8 and rank 13, 220 about five percent) and 12 to 18 bits for the 𝔽₂ family and BitZ, and the minimum is shallow: within a percent from 16 to 20 bits. With BLAKE3 trees, the default, the WHIR-type rows take 24 bits, the most measured, since 224 compressions are a small part of a merge spread over tens of thousands of circuits. The settings and the lower rates follow Ulrich Haböck's questions. The capacity regime, which would halve the openings again, is not offered, since its conjectures were refuted.

Hashes in circuits. Every hash that a merge circuit computes is one of two. The default is a conventional hash, BLAKE3, at 14,900 gates per 64-byte compression in a lookup-free R1CS over Pasta or Goldilocks and about 200 word multiplications natively. The option allows an arithmetic hash, Poseidon 1.1 over the circuit's own field: 288 gates per Pasta permutation absorbing four elements, about 480 for the width-12 Goldilocks one, and 35,000 or 7,200 word multiplications natively. Arithmetic hashes deserve consideration on the merits: for large fields and the widths in question, none of the attacks on Poseidon 1.1 has come near an academic break, and Tachyon uses it. The reason to exclude them by default is future-proofing. For long-term storage that reason is decisive, though some of a proof system's hashes are ones that long-term storage does not depend on. If requiring conventional hashes causes only a small regression that does not affect deployability, they are the choice; the option shows what the arithmetic hash buys. With the option the rows priced with Poseidon join the BLAKE3 ones rather than replace them, so a configuration that is better on some measure with the conventional hash stays on the plot, and each frontier takes the better of the two. Ragu's checked-in circuits use Poseidon 1.1, so the Pedersen-IPA rows exist only under the option. What the default changes: Akita's merge absorbs its transcript, 3 to 5 KB, in 45 to 78 compressions instead of 170 to 230 Poseidon atoms, so the merge grows from about 25 thousand gates to 0.83 million at the 128‑bit field and on Goldilocks and 2.0 million for the Pasta variants, at rank 16. Each step circuit's evaluation claim then costs more to absorb than a rank-13 or rank-14 circuit holds, so those ranks have no Akita rows, and rank 15 none for the Pasta variants. At rank 16 the merge takes 51 circuits at the 128‑bit field and on Goldilocks and 122 for the Pasta variants, against two with Poseidon, so the merge prover is 26 times the Poseidon one (8.6e9 against 3.4e8 at the 128‑bit field, 1.3e9 against 5.1e7 on Goldilocks) and 61 times for the Pasta variants; the proof grows by 3 to 4 KB at the small fields and by 9 to 12 KB with Pasta claims, from the per-circuit claims, and the atoms by 50 to 120. The per-step commitment cost and the decider are unchanged. The lever that would recover most of this is to aggregate the step circuits' claims before absorption, a design change. For the WHIR-type rows every Merkle node becomes a BLAKE3 compression, so the merge grows 30 to 60 times (1.1 to 65 million gates for WHIR over Pasta at rate 1/8 and rank 13, 524 to 31,536 circuits), the prover 1.3 to 2 times, since the trees get cheap but the circuits multiply, and the decider's verification falls 17 to 75 times, to a few milliseconds; the proofs grow by a sixth with the larger batched witness, and the best grinding moves to 24 bits. The 𝔽₂ family, BitZ and the binary-groups family hash with BLAKE3 already and do not change, so under the default their comparison with Akita is like for like: at rate 1/8 the 𝔽₂ family's ARC row proves a merge in 4.4e8 word multiplications for a 174 KB proof, against Akita over Goldilocks at rank 16 with 1.3e9 and 71 KB; its merge is 14 to 74 million AND gates against Akita's 0.83 million Pasta gates, which are not the same unit.

Transaction prover. The transaction prover measure prices a whole transaction rather than one merge, so that the cost of zero knowledge can be seen against the steps it belongs to. The transaction proves an application chosen above: the Ironwood recovery circuit; a transaction with four recovery inputs, which is four of the circuit under one zero-knowledge handoff; or a number of Merkle path checks of 32 levels and the leaf; the Tachyon transactions have the next paragraph. The application is costed component by component in each family's own arithmetization, under the selected hash policy. The family's steps are then as many as hold it, rounded up, since a transaction smaller than a step pays a whole step. A step circuit of rank r commits 2r coefficients, which is 2r−2 gates of four wires for the field arithmetizations, 2r bits or digits for BitZ and the draft frontend, and 222 AND gates for the Boolean families, which have no rank. Each step is committed at the family's per-step cost, which is the row's prover divided by its circuits, and there are as many merges at the row's accumulation prover, the merge's commitments and the fold's sum-checks. The recovery circuit's curve arithmetic must be emulated off Pasta: about 14,500 multiplications in Pallas's base field, in five fixed-base and one variable-base scalar multiplication with complete additions, 160 Sinsemilla chunk additions and the nullifier's Poseidon. Its seven BLAKE2b compressions, the chunks' generator selections and its Merkle path are bit operations and cost about the same gates in any prime field. Over Pasta the circuit is 570,000 gates with a Poseidon path and 1.05 million with a BLAKE3 one. Over Goldilocks a multiplication is about 1,100 gates, with nine 29‑bit limbs and no lookups, so the circuit is 16.5 and 17.0 million gates; at a 128‑bit field, with 61‑bit limbs, about 700 gates give 10.7 and 11.2 million. In a Boolean circuit a multiplication is about 120,000 AND gates, the 255‑bit product by Karatsuba and Toom–Cook and a Montgomery reduction by a prime of no special form, while a 64‑bit addition is 64 ANDs and an XOR is free, so the hashes cost half their Pasta gates; the circuit is 1.73 billion AND gates, the multiplications nearly all of it. In BitZ's integer arithmetization a multiplication is 510 committed bits, the value and the quotient, checked as one integer relation, while a hash gate is one bit and a 64‑bit addition 65, so the circuit is 8.4 million bits. In the draft frontend over Akita, whose products are native over Pasta, a multiplication is the value's 255 digits, so it is 4.7 million digits. The bit arithmetizations hash the path with BLAKE3 under either policy, since Poseidon over Pallas's field would cost them 240 multiplications a node. A Merkle path check is 33 nodes: 9,500 Pasta gates, or 15,600 Goldilocks or 128‑bit-field gates, with Poseidon in the field; 492,000 gates in any prime field with BLAKE3; 270,000 AND gates; or 497,000 bits or digits. So the recovery circuit's hashes are what make the bit arithmetizations cheap, a path-heavy application makes them cheaper still, and the Boolean families pay for every Pallas multiplication. For Akita's zero-knowledge rows the user's masking fold is added: the expected rejection-sampling attempts, 2.7 at the page's Gaussian width, each a commitment of a mask the size of the accumulator under the masked accumulation matrix and as many Gaussian samples, at about 300 nanoseconds a sample by Karney's exact method, 900 word multiplications at the page's rate, plus the fold's own merge. The sampling is the larger part, and it does not depend on the modulus. The fold is paid once per transaction whatever it proves, since the masked accumulator is the step's size. The hash-based zero-knowledge rows likewise put their masked prover's extra work in the fold, once per transaction; under recursion that is their only zero-knowledge cost, since the public merges and the decider's proof are of public data. Pedersen-IPA's blinding is free. This is total work, not latency: the zero-knowledge note's §5.8 precomputes candidate masks in idle time and defers the decomposition to the aggregator, which leaves the device a sparse ring product and the transfer of the masked witness. An Orchard-style action has the recovery circuit's mix, with one path check and no BLAKE2b. For the recovery circuit, per note on one core, the draft frontend takes 0.41 s at rank 20 and BitZ with ARC 0.30 s at rank 24, each a few merges; Akita with Pasta claims over 64‑bit commitments takes 12 s at rank 17 with Poseidon, most of it the fold's sum-checks over the accumulator's 85 digits per coefficient, and 144 s with BLAKE3; Akita over Goldilocks 65 s; and Pedersen-IPA at the checked-in configuration about seven minutes, in 278 steps of rank 13, or two and a half minutes at rank 17 with 72 endoscalings per step. With zero knowledge the frontend's best is 2.5 s at rank 18, since the mask's sampling over the accumulator grows with the rank, and Akita with Pasta claims 38 s at rank 15; BitZ stays at 0.30 s, having no rejection-sampling fold, with 20 KB more proof.

Tachyon transactions. The transaction options of 2, 4, 8 or 16 tachyactions, half spends and half outputs, keep Tachyon's own proof tree (tachyon-zcash/tachyon 73031f7, Stamp::prove): a spend is two steps, an output a seed and a step, each action after the first a merge, then the fee step and Ragu's rerandomization, 3s + 3o + 1 nodes for s spends and o outputs. The page re-packs that tree for each rank's step size: a node larger than a step takes whole steps and its remainder joins the rest, which fill steps first-fit decreasing, and the rerandomization is its own step. So at rank 13 over Pasta no two action circuits share a step and the merge rides with the fee step, six steps for two tachyactions against Tachyon's seven, and at rank 15 every circuit of two tachyactions fits one step. The nodes' circuits were measured through Ragu's synthesis analysis on Tachyon's steps at its header size of 9: 1,630 and 1,649 gates for a spend's two steps, 1,467 and 1,351 for an output's, 17 for an action merge and 1,934 for the fee step, in Pasta gates with Poseidon, all within rank 13's 2,048. In the other arithmetizations the Poseidon permutations, 192 gates each in Ragu's gate model, are hashed under the page's policy, the fixed-base multiplications' window selections cost the bits' four monomials, and the remaining gates are taken as Pallas multiplications, an overstatement where a gate only checks a bit. So a node that is one step over Pasta is 27 to 36 steps of rank 17 over Goldilocks and 23 to 30 Boolean steps, which is where the range of step counts comes from. Per-note steps that run in the background are not counted. A Tachyon transaction also needs Tachyon's header of nine elements, so while one is selected every row is shown at that header: the Pedersen-IPA rows at header 9 only, and the post-quantum rows, priced at header 2, with seven more elements of 64 bytes each. The recovery circuit's options do the same, since its primary input needs six or seven elements. On one core at the default floor, two tachyactions take: with Pedersen-IPA at the checked-in configuration six fuses, 8.8 s, where Tachyon's own tree of seven fuses is 10.2 s, and at rank 15 with sixteen endoscalings per step two fuses, 5.4 s; Akita with Pasta claims over 64‑bit commitments 0.42 s at rank 15 with Poseidon; BitZ 0.27 s at rank 22; the draft frontend 0.21 s at rank 19 with BLAKE3; Akita over Goldilocks 20 s in 151 steps; and the 𝔽₂ family 20 s in 129. With zero knowledge Akita with Pasta claims takes 3.6 s and the frontend 1.3 s, and BitZ is unchanged. Sixteen tachyactions take: Pedersen-IPA 50 s in 34 steps at rank 13, or 22 s in 8 steps at rank 15; Akita with Pasta claims 1.7 s; BitZ 0.56 s; the frontend 0.83 s; Goldilocks 132 s. For scale, Orchard's Halo 2 prover, measured on the same machine with assembly, takes 1.88 s for two actions and about 14 s for sixteen on one thread. So Tachyon on Pedersen-IPA is three to five times Orchard per action on one core, and about three times that again on all cores, where the fuse parallelizes less well.

How much to trust the Module-SIS parameters

Akita's advantage over the hash-based route rests on Module-SIS, where that route rests on a random oracle alone and Pedersen-IPA on the discrete logarithm. This section states what the parameters assume and where the uncertainty sits.

The sizing rule. Every matrix in Akita's catalog is sized so that the cheapest known attack on its Module-SIS instance costs at least 2128 operations under the quantum core-SVP model of [ADPS16]. That model charges 20.265β for lattice reduction with BKZ block size β and ignores the polynomial factors. The block size that reaches a given root Hermite factor is taken from the BKZ 2.0 analysis of [CN11]. Every instance in the catalog lands at block size 484 to 486, in lattices of dimension 2,000 to 4,800. The catalog's own security check reports 128.26 bits on each. A cross-check with the maintained lattice estimator [LE] agrees on the ranks. The estimator is maintained; the paper that introduced it, [APS15], is not, and its authors say so in a 2019 note. The estimator costs BKZ as 8·n calls to an SVP oracle of the block size, with sieving per [BDGL16] classically and per [LMvdP15] and [Laa16] quantumly. It follows [ADPS16] for the primal attack, as [AGVW17] established, and leaves out the dimensions for free of [Duc18] and [ADH+19]. The same block size under other models:

classical sieving, 20.292β142[BDGL16]

For calibration, ML-DSA [FIPS204], the standardized Dilithium, sizes its SIS instances at block sizes of about 423, 638 and 909 for its three security levels [Dil21]. These parameters therefore sit between its levels 2 and 3 on the same model.

Where the uncertainty sits. Three places, in decreasing order of concern.

  • Proof-level slack, not the assumption. Lattice schemes have slipped before by sizing for honest norms rather than the extractor's. Akita sizes for the extractor's collision bound, which is why the masked variant needs rank 340 against 143. But the knowledge-soundness proof in the quantum random-oracle model is still in progress. And the decoupled commitment modulus has no soundness argument yet beyond the LaBinius analogue. A gap of that kind moves parameters by a lot, not by twenty bits.
  • The instantiation is unusual. Ring dimension 64 with 128‑bit or 255‑bit moduli, compression rings of dimension 8 and 4, and norm bounds up to 248 are far from the regime of the standardized schemes. No attack exploits module structure beyond constant factors. Module-SIS has a worst-case reduction [LS15], and the quantum attacks on ideal lattices [CDW17] need principal ideals, which modules of rank 64 and more do not present. But there is less cryptanalytic attention on this corner, and the ℓ∞ bounds take their own estimator route.
  • The cost model. Core-SVP is already a lower-bound style model. Refinements since 2016, such as the dimensions for free of [Duc18], have moved concrete estimates by ten to twenty bits either way. The disputed dual-attack improvements of [MATZOV22], reassessed in [DP23], concern LWE rather than SIS, which is attacked through the primal route. A 128‑bit quantum floor is 142 classical bits, and the paranoid model still gives 101.

Against the other assumptions. The hash-based route's assumption is weaker in the good sense: a random oracle and collision resistance only. Its soundness risk is concentrated in the proximity-gap conjectures, which is exactly where the 2025 refutations landed. The proven Johnson regime is therefore where that route is priced here. Pedersen-IPA rests on the discrete logarithm on Pasta, about 126 classical bits and nothing against a quantum adversary. So the fair statement is that Akita dominates on efficiency while adding one assumption whose concrete hardness is estimated rather than proven. That assumption has SIS studied since [Ajt96], Module-SIS since [LS15], and standardization behind it.

A margin. Raising the floor from 128 to 147 quantum bits moves the block size to about 556. The security-floor option above re-plans Akita's catalog at that floor with Akita's own planner. Its single security policy is retargeted to 147 bits, its runtime width tables are regenerated by its estimator at that floor, and its six rank-one compression cells are re-sized from them. So the openings at 147 bits are planned, not scaled. The floor is 147 rather than 150 because the compression chain is built from rank-one maps. At the catalog's geometry its first map, at ring dimension 16 and width 4,096, certifies at 147.3 bits. A 150-bit floor allows that map a width of 3,806, so the chain would need rank-two maps, or two maps where it has one, and either doubles the compressed images at every level. The planner does not give the prover's rows term or the verifier's inner-image share. For those the model takes the 115 weakest Module-SIS instances in the shipped catalog's security report and, for each, the smallest module rank that covers the instance's width at both floors. With the catalog's own mix of moduli the ranks sum to 121 at 128 bits and to 150 at 147 bits, a factor of 1.24, with 29 instances moving up a rank. Over a 64‑bit modulus, where the ranks are higher to begin with, the sums are 185 and 211, a factor of 1.14, with 26 moving. Over Pallas the instances sit at the width cap at both floors but one; the factor of 1.01 at 150 bits is carried as a bound. Where the floor binds, the tables move a long way. At 150 bits the median shrink of the maximum secure width was a factor of 2 to 4 at ring dimensions up to 32, 6 to 13 at 64, 14 to 19 at 128 and 256, and about 20 at 512 and 1024. WHIR's proofs, measured 18 percent larger at 150 bits, are taken as 16 percent larger at 147, and ARC's openings grow by 15 percent.
