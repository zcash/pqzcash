Ragu PCS Pareto

This page costs Ragu over a wide range of parameters — with its pre-quantum Pedersen-IPA decider, and adapted to various post-quantum PCD schemes. The lattice-based scheme Akita is considered under five field and modulus choices, and with an experimental BitZ-style arithmetization over it, called Kishu. For hash-based schemes, we consider: WHIR over Pasta, over Goldilocks, and with Pasta claims in 64‑bit limbs; a Plonky3-style FRI over Goldilocks; the Flock/Binius-style binary-field family; and BitZ, each with ARC accumulation and with its own recursion. A further experimental draft is Flock-style Boolean circuits over Akita's binary groups. Each point is one configuration. Its coordinates are compressed proof bytes and two measures chosen from the same seven: the accumulation prover and the transaction prover by default, or prover commitment work, the step's commitments alone, merge gates, the atoms that the merge circuit absorbs, the decider's verification work, the prover's work for a whole transaction, or the accumulator's memory. The hash-based rows' rate and grinding, the security floor, zero knowledge, and the hashes allowed in circuits (conventional only, or Poseidon 1.1 too) are selectable. Each class, pre-quantum (Pedersen-IPA) and post‑quantum (the rest), has its own frontier: the configurations that nothing else in the class beats on all three at once, where native or relation Pasta arithmetic counts as a requirement rather than a cost. The WHIR-type and Plonky3 families start unticked in the families list, since Akita dominates them on every measure and their many rows crowd the picture.

Frontiers

The table lists the configurations of each class that are not dominated within that class on bytes and the selected second and third measures together, among the selection above. It also lists each family's own frontier, marked "family" in the last column, so that every scheme's best configurations appear even where its class dominates it. Pasta as a requirement

The Pasta arithmetic that the Ironwood recovery circuit needs enters the dominance test as a requirement rather than a cost. The table's Pasta arithmetic column says how each scheme realises it:

  • native: a gate over Pasta's field (Pedersen-IPA, the Pasta variants of Akita, WHIR over Pasta and the limb hybrid, whose witness and merge circuit are over Pasta);
  • relation, limbs: the multiplication as an integer relation over committed bits, with the result and the quotient committed and no committed intermediate wires, checked coefficient by coefficient over 29‑bit limbs with their carries committed (Kishu's limb route);
  • relation, fingerprint: the same relation checked in a few PIOP instances over random primes, as BitZ does it, without the limbs and carries (BitZ, and Kishu's fingerprint route);
  • limbs: limb products as committed wires in another field (the 128‑bit and Goldilocks rows and Plonky3); or
  • bits: a Boolean circuit (the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family and the binary groups).

The rule counts the first three (native and the two relation variants): such a configuration is dominated only by another such, while a limbs or bits configuration can be dominated by any. So the post‑quantum frontier always keeps the best native and relation points beside the cheaper other-field ones. How efficiently each realisation performs is what the transaction measure's application costs show.

Which measures

The second and third measures are chosen independently from the same seven, the accumulation prover by default for the second and the transaction prover for the third, with the Ironwood recovery circuit as the transaction. The frontier is taken on bytes and both. The accumulation prover is what one fuse costs the prover beyond its own step: the merge circuits' commitments, which are prover commitment work, plus the fold's sum-checks over the accumulator, and for Kishu its merge's constraint PIOP.

Merge gates is offered since every family has an estimate of them. Accumulation atoms is kept because it is the more robust proxy for the lattice merge, whose gate count is estimated. For the hash-based families atoms say nothing, since their merge cost is the Merkle hashing. Decider work is the decider's verification cost: under ten milliseconds for the 64-bit and 128-bit lattice rows, the binary-field families and the hash-based deciders with BLAKE3 trees; 2.33 to 26.7 ms for Akita's three Pasta variants, Kishu and BitZ; 41.9 to 384 ms for Pedersen-IPA; and, with Poseidon trees, 4.76 to 30.8 ms for WHIR over Goldilocks and 22.4 to 158 ms for WHIR over Pasta. So it rarely decides a frontier, but it is there to check.

Transaction prover is the expected work to prove the application chosen below: the Ironwood recovery circuit, a transaction with four recovery inputs, the hardware wallet's part of the recovery statement on its own (the device's $\mathsf{H^{qk}}$ statement, or a leanSPHINCS verifier with either BLAKE2s or Keccak-256), a number of Merkle path checks, or a Tachyon transaction of 2 to 16 tachyactions in Tachyon's own proof tree. For the post-quantum families the Tachyon transaction is a post-quantum variant of Tachyon in a new shielded pool, whose hashes follow the page's policy. The recovery circuit recovers notes of the existing pool, so its nullifier stays Poseidon over Pasta. The application is costed in each family's own arithmetization, so it takes more steps where Pallas arithmetic must be emulated. The measure charges each step at the family's per-step commitment cost and one merge per step at the row's accumulation prover. The zero-knowledge rows also pay the masking work at the handoff to the public aggregator. The Transaction prover section below gives each application's cost in each arithmetization.

Parameter changes against design changes

Rank

A step at rank $r$ commits $2^r$ coefficients: $2^{r-2}$ gates of four wires for the field arithmetizations, and $2^r$ bits or digits for BitZ and Kishu, which alone go past rank 17.

For Pedersen-IPA, the circuit budget below limits the endoscalings per step and the binders at each rank, which makes rank a real trade-off. A larger circuit holds more endoscalings and fewer binders, which removes committed components and with them bytes and atoms, while the IPA grows by 128 bytes per rank and every remaining polynomial doubles. Rank 14 with the fixed circuits packed beats rank 13 on bytes, prover commitment work and atoms; ranks 15 and 16 buy bytes and atoms with prover commitment work.

For Akita the step circuits' witnesses open as one batch, so with Poseidon the rank moves bytes and atoms only through each circuit's own evaluation claim and the last circuit's unused capacity, a few hundred bytes across the range. With BLAKE3 the claims' absorption makes the rank decisive, as the Hashes section says. The more significant trade is the field choice: a smaller field buys fewer bytes and less prover commitment work at the cost of more atoms absorbed per merge.

Rank 12 has no Pedersen-IPA rows, since a 1,024-gate circuit holds no binder challenge, and no Akita rows under BLAKE3. Rank 17 helps Akita under BLAKE3, where fewer circuits absorb fewer claims: 14 to 30 percent off the merge prover, and 0.6 kbytes off the proof at the small fields, 3 kbytes for the Pasta variants.

The WHIR-type rows depend on the rank only through their circuit count, so they are shown at ranks 13 to 16.

The bit arithmetizations, BitZ and Kishu, whose steps commit $2^{\mathrm{rank}}$ bits or digits, go on to ranks 24 and 23, where one step holds the recovery circuit. Their merge is nearly a fixed cost, so the transaction measure falls with the rank until then, while the proof and the merge prover barely move; padding a step beyond the application would only cost. Rank 12 gives BitZ a slightly smaller proof and merge prover.

ARC against each scheme's own recursion

Every hash-based scheme appears twice, with ARC accumulation and, in a lighter shade of the same colour, with full recursion: the merge circuit verifies its two input proofs, each step emits a full proof, and the decider verifies one. The hash and the field are the same. The proofs differ, since the recursive decider verifies one stand-alone proof while the ARC decider's first round opens three oracles. So the recursive rows' proofs are a little over half the ARC rows' (181 kbytes against 326 kbytes for WHIR at rate 1/8 and rank 13).

For WHIR the recursion is about a wash on the prover at rate 1/8: the verifier's hashes per input match ARC's openings through the tree's levels (2,600 against 86 $\times$ 26 without grinding, and both fall with it). Its merge is two fifths larger. At rate 1/2 it saves a third of the prover and a twentieth of the merge, because the verifier's queries fall with the rate while ARC's openings grow with it. So with Poseidon trees the rate-1/2 recursive rows of WHIR over either field and of the limb hybrid dominate their ARC rows on every measure. Under BLAKE3 trees no ARC row is dominated by its family's recursive row, nor are the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's or BitZ's under either hash. FRI's verifier has twice WHIR's hashes, so its recursion costs about twice ARC.

Ligerito's verifier, under BitZ, opens 491 columns over its 5 levels at 100 bits, against ARC's 86 openings at rate 1/8. So its recursion is 7 times ARC's hashing (200 against 28 million witness bits at rate 1/8) and 7.4 times the prover, for a proof 22 percent smaller. The ARC row is the better one for BitZ on the prover and the merge.

The $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's recursion verifies its own FRI-Binius proof, whose ring-switching verifier costs $2\ell' \cdot 2^{\kappa}$ field multiplications. So its merge is 87 million witness bits in the 192‑bit design, 133 million in the 191‑bit one, whose one-step ring switching costs the verifier 191 field multiplications per variable rather than 64, and 230 million in the 256‑bit one, against 28 million under ARC. The prover is 2.4 to 6.3 times the ARC row's, with the verifier's paths pruned as a multiproof. Its proof is a quarter smaller (126 kbytes against 176 kbytes at rate 1/8).

What the axes mean

Proof bytes

The compressed PCD proof. For Pedersen-IPA it is counted from Ragu's structs: the instance's commitments and scalars, two revdot reductions, two batches, and two IPAs with one round per bit of the rank; the checked-in configuration gives 7,908 bytes, which Ragu's wire format confirms (7,936 with its 28‑byte envelope). The two online polynomial commitments and six openings that every Ragu proof now carries are 448 of those bytes.

For Akita it is the planner's byte estimate for the decider's opening of one final group plus the separately committed groups, from the estimation runs, plus an instance of 128-byte commitments, two claims per group, and one claim per step circuit.

For WHIR with ARC it is the WHIR proof that decides the final accumulator. It is measured with the reference implementation over a 256‑bit field at 128‑bit security in the proven Johnson regime, at the batched witness size and the selected rate and grinding. Added to it are the two extra first-round trees of the decider's batched opening and the accumulator's roots and claims.

For the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family it is the optimizer's proof as the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family section describes, and for BitZ its Table 10 proof with the Zinc+ PIOP's, each with the extra trees under ARC.

For the binary-groups family it is Akita's measured opening of a binary source, extrapolated to the merge's bits.

Accumulation prover

The prover commitment work below plus the fold's own sum-checks. Akita's two norm and range sum-checks run over the accumulator, $K$ groups of $2^{\mathrm{rank}}$ coefficients in digits, at about five multiplications in the claim field per digit a pass (Pasta's field for the Pasta-claims variant, the 64‑bit field for Kishu). Kishu adds its measured Booleanity sum-check (438 ms for $2^{27}$ bits on one core, 9.8 word multiplications per 64‑bit digit). The fold's own linear combination of the digit vectors costs one multiplication per digit. So this measure grows with the rank where the commitments alone do not, since the merge circuit is fixed and only chopped differently, and it is the measure the transaction prover charges per merge.

The other rows add nothing: Pedersen-IPA's fuse is measured whole and its native folding, the batching of about 95 polynomials and a 100-point linear combination, is about one percent of it; the hash-based rows' ARC step already hashes the new accumulator; and the binary-groups row's measured opening includes its sum-checks.

Accumulator memory

What the prover holds between fuses, in bytes. A fuse holds the step's witness and one or two of them: two when both children are real proofs, one when the other child is the bootstrap proof. That is the fixed base case that Ragu's circuits recognise by its dummy suffix, as in a chain or in Tachyon's action steps.

For the lattice rows it is the folded digit vector, $K$ groups of $2^{\mathrm{rank}}$ coefficients in digits, each digit stored at the width of its accumulator's norm bound. The certified $\ell_2$ bound at the handoff is about $2^{19}$ (the zero-knowledge note's ledger), which bounds every entry, so 20 bits and a sign. Each fold multiplies the entries by up to 40, so an accumulator a level below the top needs five bits fewer. The measure is of the top level. The sum-checks expand a digit to an element of the commitment field on the fly, 8 bytes at 64 bits, 16 at 128, 32 at Pasta's modulus, which is computation, not storage.

Pedersen-IPA's is the uncompressed proof, every committed polynomial's $2^{\mathrm{rank}}$ Pasta coefficients, 26.5 Mbytes measured at rank 13 against 24.9 Mbytes counted.

The hash-based rows under ARC hold the accumulator's codeword, the witness over the rate in the field's elements, and its Merkle tree; under their own recursion only the previous proof. BitZ and the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family hold the packed codeword with a tree of 256‑byte leaves, and the binary-groups row the packed bits.

At rank 17 Akita with Pasta claims holds about 175 Mbytes, Kishu at rank 21 about 33 Mbytes, and Pedersen-IPA at rank 13 25 Mbytes.

Prover commitment work

Native 64‑bit word multiplications for one step's commitments, with every committed polynomial taken as dense. Pedersen over Pasta: a Pippenger multi-scalar multiplication costs about 255/rank mixed additions per coefficient, so the cost falls as 1/rank. The constant is calibrated to Ragu's measured fuse at the checked-in configuration: 1.46 s on one core of an Apple M4 Max for the step's circuit and its 91 recursion circuits with their stage polynomials, 96 commitments of $2^{13}$ coefficients. That is about 5,600 word multiplications per coefficient at rank 13 and 5,200 at rank 14, over all of the step's polynomials. The earlier estimate, 11 field multiplications of 32 word multiplications per addition, was 24 percent higher; udon's AArch64 assembly, which Ragu does not enable, takes 13 percent off the measured time.

Ajtai by NTT: per digit about rows/64 pointwise multiplications plus 3 for its share of the transform, at the modulus width, which is 32 word multiplications at 255 bits, 8 at 128, 2 at 64. With 85 digits per Pasta coefficient that is about 14,000 per coefficient when the commitment modulus is Pasta, 2,000 when it is 64‑bit, 2,600 at a 128‑bit field, and 390 on Goldilocks, over every step circuit's witness. The cycle's endoscaling and binder polynomials do not exist in the lattice design. Folding and sum-check work is not included.

For WHIR with ARC it is the Reed–Solomon encoding by FFT over the witness divided by the rate, plus the Merkle trees of the witness and of the new accumulator. The trees are hashed by BLAKE3 at 200 word multiplications per compression by default, or under the arithmetic-hash option by Poseidon at about 35,000 per permutation over a 255‑bit field, since natively its matrix multiplications are full multiplications too. At rate 1/8 that is about 180,000 per coefficient with Poseidon and a few thousand with BLAKE3; but the circuits that a BLAKE3 merge needs multiply the committed witness, which is where the prover's cost then goes.

The $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's prover is counted as the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family section describes, a carry-less multiplication as one word multiplication, and BitZ's is its measured Table 10 prover. The binary-groups family's is the measured commit and open times per committed bit, taken for one thread, plus Flock's PIOP.

Accumulation atoms

An atom is one element of the merge circuit's native field. A configuration's accumulation atoms are the atoms that the merge circuit absorbs per fold, to derive the fold's challenges from the two proofs that it merges. The count stands in for the merge circuit's size: each absorbed atom costs hashing constraints, and the Pedersen coordinates also feed the point arithmetic. For Pedersen-IPA it is two atoms per commitment folded, the x and y coordinates, over the commitments of both instances. For Akita it is the sum-check messages of the range and norm checks, about 120 elements for two sum-checks of about 20 rounds with three elements each, counted as one atom each. Then each compressed commitment image: 128 bytes at the variant's atom width, which is 8 atoms at a 128‑bit field, 16 on Goldilocks, 4 on Pasta, and 6 for Pasta claims with 64‑bit commitment coordinates, three coordinates to an atom. Then one atom per step circuit's claim, and 18 more with zero knowledge. The folding arithmetic is not included, and for Pedersen-IPA neither is the endoscaling arithmetic, which is what a higher endoscaling count costs in gates.

For the hash-based families, with ARC or with their own recursion, the merge absorbs only the accumulators' or input proofs' roots and claims, about 40 atoms, which is why atoms are the wrong measure for them.

Merge gates

The gates synthesized across every circuit of one merge: measured for Pedersen-IPA, and estimated for Akita and for WHIR with ARC as the Circuit budget section describes. For the binary-field families, the binary-groups family and Kishu they are counted in AND gates, witness bits or digits, as their sections describe. It is the default third measure.

In the 2D view the marker's size follows the logarithm of the third measure over the plotted points, from 5 to 22 pixels, so the sizes read across any selection. The binary-field and BitZ families' witness-bit counts sit at the top of that range. Markers over 16 pixels carry a small contrasting cross at their centre, so that they can be read against the axes. The swatches in the families list are sized like each family's median plotted marker, up to 18 pixels, so the list carries the same cue.

Decider work

The decider's verification, in the same word-multiplication equivalents as the prover, at 3e9 per second of one core, which is also what the millisecond option assumes. Pedersen-IPA is modelled: the two IPAs each need a multi-scalar multiplication of $2^{\mathrm{rank}}$ points, plus a scalar multiplication per folded commitment to combine the instance, about 41.9 ms at rank 13.

Akita is measured: the paper's single-thread verifier takes 4.2 ms at the 128‑bit field and 3.9 ms on Goldilocks at $2^{27}$ committed bits, growing by about 0.4 ms per doubling, so 2.47 to 2.85 ms at this witness size. The Pasta variants are taken at four times the 128‑bit figure, the ratio of word multiplications per field multiplication, since the verifier's work is sum-check arithmetic in the claim field: about 11.4 ms. With zero knowledge the final accumulator is at the ordinary length and the terminal's bounds are unchanged, so the verification is unchanged but for the few percent that one more step circuit's claim can cost.

The hash-based deciders are the WHIR verifier's hashes at the witness size, measured, at the variant's native permutation cost, plus about a million for the arithmetic, and in the ARC rows the decider's two extra first-round trees. At rate 1/8 and rank 13 that is 13.0 ms on Goldilocks and 62.1 ms with Poseidon over Pasta under ARC, and 6.86 and 30.6 ms under recursion, and about a millisecond in every case with BLAKE3 trees; FRI has 2.07 times the hashes.

The $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's decider is the FRI-Binius verifier: the proof's authentication hashes over pruned paths and its leaf hashes at BLAKE3's native cost plus its field multiplications, 0.307 to 1.18 ms.

BitZ is its Table 10 verifier at the merge's bits, 2.42 ms at $2^{25}$, scaled with the floor and, in the ARC rows, by the decider's two extra first-round trees, plus 2.00 ms for Zinc+'s PIOP.

The binary-groups family's decider is Akita's measured verifier for a binary source, at the paper's slope, 5.06 ms at the merge's bits.

Circuit budget

A step circuit at rank $r$ holds $2^{r-2}$ gates and four times as many constraints, 2,048 and 8,192 at rank 13, and the gate limit binds first. The Pedersen-IPA figures are the internal circuits' synthesis counts pinned in Ragu's tests at rank 13 (tachyon-zcash/ragu 8109a41c). Lattice-based families

Akita

The Akita merge circuit is estimated, not measured. Its transcript hashing is priced at the selected hash: BLAKE3 at 14,900 gates per 64 bytes absorbed, the sum-check elements and the claims at the claim field's width and the six commitment images at 128 bytes each, or Poseidon absorbing four elements per 288-gate permutation, 72 gates per accumulation atom. Folding each child's 128-byte commitment image needs a modular reduction, taken as one gate per bit. The sum-check verifier's arithmetic is about 400 gates.

The step circuits are that total divided by the rank's budget, rounded up; each circuit's own evaluation claim is absorbed by the next merge, so the count is a fixed point. Under BLAKE3 a 16-byte claim costs about 3,700 gates to absorb and a 32-byte one 7,500, more than a rank-13 circuit holds, and the 32-byte one more than a rank-14 circuit. So rank 13 has no Akita rows under the default, nor rank 14 for the Pasta variants. Splitting a compression across circuits is taken as free, although a rank below 16 cannot hold one in a single circuit. Merge gates, in the hover text and the table, is the total over every circuit of one merge.

Kishu: BitZ over Akita (draft)

Kishu is the study's name for BitZ's arithmetization over Akita's PCS. Kishu is a Japanese dog breed, like Akita and Hachi. An internal research draft of September 2026, provisional and unreviewed, supplies the piece that such a combination needs: a Booleanity check over the lattice's own prime field. The committed table's bits are certified by one unweighted quadratic identity plus an integer norm bound, which a structured random projection supplies by revealing 160 coordinates, so BitZ's exponent-lift GKR, 79% of its prover, is not needed.

Digits and constraints

The draft measures the reduction alone, single-threaded at $2^{27}$ bits over $2^{64} - 59$. The projection prover takes 113 ms against 438 ms for the optimized conventional Booleanity sumcheck, with verifiers of 0.85 and 0.023 ms and payloads of 2.6 kbytes and 1.7 kbytes. Neither includes the commitment opening or hiding.

The family commits one bit per digit over Akita's 64‑bit modulus, plus the check at 2.5 or 9.8 word multiplications per bit, and a step commits $2^{\mathrm{rank}}$ digits. Its constraints are charged as well as its digits. The constraint PIOP, a sum-check over the committed table, costs about 9.8 word multiplications per product term of a constraint, the measured rate of the Booleanity sum-check. A nonzero of a linear form costs 2 for a small constant or 4 for a field constant, the forms being over word entries. This is charged for the application's constraints and the merge's alike, and the PIOP's transcript is absorbed and its verifier run in the merge above.

Its claims live in the 64‑bit field itself, Akita's commitment modulus, and the verifier recomposes each Pasta value from 32‑bit limbs proved as linear claims in the same sum-check. So Kishu does not use the decoupled commitment, and its soundness rests on Akita's own at the 64‑bit profile and on the draft's Booleanity certificate. Its opening proof and decider are priced on the Goldilocks variant's plan and measurement, the same profile, at Kishu's digit count.

Pasta multiplication by two routes

Akita's claims are in one fixed field, so a Pasta multiplication $a \cdot b = k \cdot p + r$ over committed bits cannot be checked modulo that field alone: the prover could choose $r$. Kishu's rows therefore come in two routes, each a row. By the limb route the relation is checked coefficient by coefficient over 29‑bit limbs, which are linear in the bits and cost nothing to commit. That is 17 constraints of up to nine product terms each, bounded below half the modulus so that they hold over the integers, with a committed carry of 34 bits each. A multiplication is then 1,088 digits and 98 product terms. By the fingerprint route the relation is one constraint in each of 3 PIOP instances over random 52‑bit primes, as BitZ checks it, at 510 digits. The price is per step that has multiplications, so a step of hashes alone pays nothing and the routes tie on the path applications. Each instance's evaluation claim becomes about $\sqrt{2^{\mathrm{rank}}}$ integer column sums by BitZ's tensor split, each exact as one claim in the 64‑bit field and committed as digits of 61 bits: 265,000 digits a step at rank 21, with three more transcripts for the merge above. So the recovery circuit is 17 million digits by the limb route and 8.5 million by the fingerprint route, with constraint PIOPs of 0.0120 and 0.00409 s on one core.

The merge

Kishu's merge is Akita's, costed in digits. A bit gate is one digit, so a BLAKE3 compression of the transcript is 15,000 digits and the images' reductions one per bit. The BLAKE3 accounting is its seven rounds of eight G functions: each 32‑bit addition is one relation $a + b = s + 2^{32} c$ over word entries, with the 32 sum bits and the carry committed; each XOR bit is one constraint $z = x + y - 2xy$ with its bit committed; rotations are free, being wiring; and the finalisation is 16 words of XORs. That is 15,000 digits, 7,904 constraints and 31,616 nonzeros a compression. The same accounting at 64 bits, each addition in two halves since a 64‑bit sum can wrap modulo q, gives BLAKE2b 50,944 digits and 26,368 constraints. Under BLAKE3 every transcript element the prover supplies is first checked to be below the modulus, since a 64‑bit decomposition is canonical only there, about 63 digits a word.

The merge's arithmetic is over the 64‑bit field and its quadratic extension, which is the PIOP's own field. As in BitZ, the constraint PIOP runs over the ring itself, and only the final linear claim is proved on the bits. So a multiplication there is one constraint, with no bit decomposition and no quotient. But every wire is committed as its bits: 64 digits for a field element and 128 for a product in the extension. So the sum-check's arithmetic is 400 such products, each circuit's claim 4 and the Booleanity sum-check's verifier about 3,000; the projection verifier scans 3.1 million sampled coefficients, a digit each. The transcript's elements and each circuit's claim are elements of the extension, 16 bytes, and the Booleanity sum-check's own transcript is absorbed with them.

Under the Poseidon option the transcript is hashed by Poseidon over the 64‑bit field at width 12 and rate 8 with the $x^3$ S-box: 276 multiplications a permutation at 64 digits each, 17% more committed digits than BLAKE3 per absorbed byte. A permutation is 276 constraints with 13,800 nonzeros of field constants, where a BLAKE3 compression is 7,904 with 31,616 of small ones, and these are charged at the rates above. Per 64 bytes absorbed, commitments and constraints together, BLAKE3 costs 670,000 word multiplications and Poseidon 650,000, within 3% of each other, which many modelling choices could sway either way. So both hashes are kept as variants of Kishu, each a family of its own in the list and the plot: the BLAKE3 rows under either option, the Poseidon rows under the arithmetic-hash option, and the frontier decides between them.

Costs at rank 17

At rank 17 with BLAKE3 that is 1.9 million digits in 15 circuits, a proof of 69 kbytes, a merge prover of 6.6e7 word multiplications and a 2.33 ms decider in the sum-check form; with Poseidon 2.2 million digits, 70 kbytes and 7.5e7. Each circuit's claim costs 4,400 digits to absorb under BLAKE3 and 4,900 under Poseidon, so even a rank‑13 circuit of 8,192 digits holds its claim (500 circuits under BLAKE3). Kishu therefore has rows from rank 13 under both hashes, where the lattice rows under BLAKE3 start at 14.

Against the 64‑bit Akita variant, which commits every wire of a hash gate as a 255‑bit value, the merge prover is 170 times smaller under BLAKE3 (1.1e10 at rank 17) and 3.5 times under Poseidon. Under BLAKE3 the proof is smaller too (69 kbytes against 88 kbytes). So under conventional hashes Kishu dominates that variant on every measure but the merge's count, whose units differ; under Poseidon Kishu's proof is 10 kbytes smaller. Against BitZ at rank 17 the proof is 7.1 times smaller and the merge prover does 2.9 to 3.3 times less work.

Quang Dao thinks the prover here is likely overestimated: the commitment is priced at rows sized by the loose security analysis, with its grinding, and a tighter analysis would cut them. So the family's prover should be read as an upper bound. The plot's measures are the recursion's: the step's commitments, the accumulator's opening and the merge circuit, which every design pays and which can be estimated. Which application circuit to benchmark is hard to agree on, since it depends on the application; so the application enters the plot through the rank, and the transaction measure through its cost in digits.

The recovery circuit

The Ironwood recovery circuit itself is hash-heavy: its expensive parts are seven BLAKE2b-512 compressions (the cost section of [ZIP-2005]; beside two Sinsemilla commitments, two full-width Pallas scalar multiplications, a Merkle path, and a BLAKE3 compression in the quantum-key signature). In Kishu a Boolean hash is one bit per wire against many Pasta gates per round in the others, and the integer arithmetic simulates $\mathbb{F}_p$ without a bit decomposition per operation, with range checks free. Costed component by component in this arithmetization, with a hash gate at one digit and a multiplication at 1,088 digits by the limb route or 510 by the fingerprint route, the recovery circuit is 17 million or 8.5 million digits against 570,000 Pasta gates. The transaction measure uses those counts together with the constraint PIOP's work.

LaBinius

LaBinius [OS26] is a lattice commitment whose modulus is decoupled from the constraint field: the commitment runs over a composite, NTT-friendly modulus, the evaluation claim lives in a binary extension field, and extraction avoids reduction modulo the commitment modulus by working over the number field. Its native opening is the amortised one, a square root of the witness in size, 314 kbytes at $2^{18}$ elements and 3.0 Mbytes at $2^{24}$; LaBRADOR compresses it to 75 to 88 kbytes at ten times the prover, with a verifier of 77 to 713 ms, all at 100 bits. For this study its importance is that it is a worked, implemented instance of the decoupled commitment modulus that the Pasta-claims-with-64‑bit-commitments variant assumes for Akita, with a soundness argument to adapt. It gives a decider only, with no accumulation or recursion layer, and Akita's own development has since replaced its root reduction with binary groups, which the next section plots.

Flock-style circuits over Akita's binary groups (experimental)

Akita's development branch commits a dense binary source as a group kind inside one ordinary grouped opening (LayerZero-Labs/akita#285, the binary feature; it replaces the LaBinius root reduction and nested commitment of its earlier PR #229). The bits are packed into 128‑bit carriers, the claim is a multilinear evaluation over a binary field, and the opening switches to the proof field inside. So the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's arithmetization, Boolean circuits with Flock's PIOP over $\mathbb{F}_{2^{128}}$, can commit with Akita instead of FRI-Binius and accumulate with Akita's fold instead of ARC.

The row prices that from the branch's own measurements of November 2026, at the P128 root; the P64 root is one to four percent smaller, with times within run-to-run noise. A binary-claim opening of $2^{22}$ carriers, $2^{29}$ bits, is 82,764 bytes. It is committed in 174 ms, opened in 648 ms and verified in 8.5 ms, on an M4 Max under load with the parallel feature on.

The merge is Akita's fold in a Boolean circuit, and that is our estimate. The transcript's 2.7 kbytes absorbed by BLAKE3 at 8,192 AND gates per compression, native in a Boolean circuit under either hash policy, is 0.35 million gates; the fold of the twelve child images by a ternary challenge of weight 20, one adder bit per image bit per unit of weight, 0.27 million; and one 128‑bit proof-field multiplication per sum-check element, 120 of them, 2.1 million, which dominates.

A full adder is one AND, so schoolbook is about $2n^2$ at $n$ bits; Karatsuba pays from about 20 bits and halves the count at 128; and Toom–Cook 3, with its exact division by three done bit-serially, takes a further two percent off. That is 16,400 AND gates at 128 bits, plus 800 for the reduction by $2^{128} - 275$. That is 2.7 million AND gates in all, a ninth of the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's ARC merge at rate 1/8, and not comparable with Pasta gates.

The proof is the measured opening extrapolated from $2^{29}$ bits down to the merge's $2^{21.3}$ at the planner's 563 bytes per doubling for the 128‑bit field, with the stage groups at that field's growth: 84 kbytes. The measured pair of sizes a doubling apart differ by 1.8 kbytes, which would make it 10 kbytes smaller, but one difference between single runs is not a slope.

The prover takes the measured commit and open times as the step's commitment and its share of the fold's sum-checks, which the lattice rows do not count. They are taken at ten times the measured times for one thread, since BitZ's benchmark gave four to five times on ten threads of this machine: 46 word multiplications per committed bit, plus Flock's PIOP at 2.3. That is 1.3e8 for the merge, an upper bound, against 5e8 for the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's ARC step at rate 1/8, and against 1.3e9 for Akita over Goldilocks at rank 16 under BLAKE3. There the transcript's hashing in a Pasta circuit is what makes 51 circuits. The row is on the post‑quantum frontier under the default.

The decider is the measured verifier at the paper's slope, 5.06 ms, where the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's is 0.307 ms. In the transaction measure a Pasta gate of the recovery circuit is about 1,650 AND gates here, since a Pallas multiplication in a Boolean circuit is about 120,000 of them. So the recovery circuit costs about 80,000 word multiplications per Pasta gate in this family, against 8,100 for Akita with Pasta claims over 64‑bit commitments. Not modelled: zero knowledge, since the groups are not hiding; the 147‑bit floor; the Flock verifier in the merge, as for the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family; and the binary carrier field switch. Hash-based families

These families rest on a random oracle alone: WHIR over Pasta, over Goldilocks and with the Pasta witness in 64‑bit limbs, a Plonky3-style FRI over Goldilocks, the Flock/Binius-style $\mathbb{F}_2$ family, and BitZ, each with ARC accumulation and with its own recursion. What the hash-based route has that Akita does not is native Pasta arithmetic with no design change beyond the commitment scheme, and a prover whose hashing is cheap with a conventional hash. The default view prices that, and the Hashes section says what the recursion then costs.

The figures in this section are with Poseidon trees, the arithmetic-hash option; the Hashes section gives the BLAKE3 ones. WHIR with ARC accumulation is dominated by Akita on every measure, while the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family and BitZ are the efficient end of the line; the subsections say how far.

WHIR and FRI

WHIR with ARC accumulation is dominated by Akita on every measure: with the grinding chosen per row its proofs are 171 kbytes to 1,280 kbytes, its prover is 27 to 1,900 times Pedersen-IPA's, and its merge is 0.57 to 5.4 million gates, 23 to 220 times Akita's.

The proof is the decider's. At the root nobody has yet combined the two child accumulators with the root's own witness, so the decider's proof opens those three oracles in its first round, each under its own Merkle root (Ulrich Haböck's point). A stand-alone WHIR proof of the same witness is 176 kbytes at rate 1/8 and rank 13. The two extra first rounds bring it to 326 kbytes, with their paths pruned as a multiproof, as the reference implementation already prunes its own.

The lower rates and the grinding, added after Ulrich Haböck's questions, take a third off its proof at rate 1/32 against rate 1/8 (226 kbytes against 326 kbytes at rank 13) and a third off its merge, at 2.5 times the encoding and hashing. The sizes are extrapolated from measurements at $2^{17}$, $2^{18}$ and $2^{20}$. The growth per doubling is not uniform, since a folding round is added every two doublings. The three-doubling slope used here is about nine kbytes against five over the first doubling alone.

Goldilocks cuts its prover by about 5 times and its proof by a sixth, and grows its merge, since a Goldilocks Poseidon costs more gates per node. The limb hybrid is WHIR with the Pasta witness committed as four 64‑bit limbs per coefficient, tested over Goldilocks, with a Pasta merge circuit: the family list's "Pasta claims, 64‑bit limbs" rows. Unlike Akita's 64‑bit commitment variant it gains nothing: the Pasta merge circuit forces Pasta's hash on the trees, and four limbs per coefficient is more hashing, not less. WHIR over Pasta is dominated by Akita's Pasta variants, which is a like-for-like comparison.

The merge is estimated, as Akita's is. A hash commitment is not homomorphic, so Ragu's split accumulation does not apply; ARC accumulates Reed–Solomon proximity claims instead. Its verifier checks $2(\lambda - g)/\log_2(1/\rho)$ Merkle openings per input at rate $\rho$ with $g$ bits of grinding in the query phase, in the proven, Johnson-bound regime. At $\lambda = 128$ and 16 bits of grinding that is 224, 112, 75, 56, 45 and 38 at rates 1/2 to 1/64, and 256, 128 and 85 at 1/2, 1/4 and 1/8 without grinding. The factor of two would go away only under the list-decoding conjectures refuted in late 2025.

Each opening is a path through the accumulator's tree over the witness divided by the rate, one hash per node, a BLAKE3 compression at 14,900 gates by default or a Poseidon permutation at 288 under the option. A merge has two inputs. The step circuits follow as for Akita, with the tree's depth growing with the batched witness, which is why the number of circuits is in the hundreds. WARP has the same opening count for Reed–Solomon codes and a linear-time prover only with linear-time codes, which does not help here, since the cost is the hashing rather than the encoding.

Verifying a WHIR proof in the circuit instead of accumulating, the other route, costs about the same hashing for a single oracle and more with the oracles of a real polynomial IOP. The proof of each step's growing witness is fed back into the next step.

The $\mathbb{F}_2$ family

The binary-field, sum-check-based systems of 2025 and 2026, Flock [BRW26] and Binius64 on the FRI-Binius and Ligerito commitments, and Blaze on repeat-accumulate-accumulate codes, are the efficient end of the hash-based line. They are Boolean arithmetizations over $\mathbb{F}_2$ with claims in $\mathbb{F}_{2^{128}}$, so they have no $2^{\mathrm{rank}}$ Pasta circuits and the rank filter does not apply to them; the Flock/Binius-style family is priced in its own terms. Its merge is ARC's openings as for the other hash-based variants, each Merkle node a BLAKE3 compression of about $2^{13}$ AND gates. So the merge is a Boolean witness of that many gates, 17 to 77 million from rate 1/64 to 1/2. Each input's PIOP verifier runs in the merge too, the zerocheck and the lincheck over the witness's variables and a sum-check per base matrix in place of the native verifier's own evaluation of the matrices: about 350.0 multiplications in the challenge field, 1.5 million AND gates by Karatsuba at rate 1/8, 5.7 percent of the merge. Its merge-gates figure is a count of AND gates that is not comparable with Pasta gates; bytes and prover commitment work are comparable.

Proof bytes come from Ulrich Haböck's FRI-Binius proof-size optimizer, in three designs that each row names: the witness packed into $\mathbb{F}_{2^{256}}$, which is also the field of the folding and sum-check challenges; packed into $\mathbb{F}_{2^{64}}$ with the challenges drawn from its cubic extension $\mathbb{F}_{2^{192}}$, which is the field that the agreement bound sees, as Plonky3's FRI pairs a base field with an extension; or packed straight into $\mathbb{F}_{2^{191}}$, a prime-degree field with the trinomial $x^{191} + x^9 + 1$ and no subfields, which Ulrich Haböck suggested for its arithmetic. The proof is at 128 bits of security in the list-decoding regime up to the Johnson bound, with the largest list that the correlated-agreement bound allows (the simplified BCHKS25 bound; BCIKS20 for unique decoding). The codeword's length is counted in field elements, with the Guruswami–Sudan multiplicity, the selected grinding, the folding schedule chosen to minimise the compressed proof, and Merkle-cap multiproofs. Checked against the reference WHIR implementation at the same witness, field, regime and grinding, its query counts are within one to five of WHIR's and its proofs about a tenth smaller, from the optimised schedule. A 128‑bit field cannot reach 128 bits in any regime at these sizes, since the agreement term alone is about 100 bits, which is why FRI-Binius's published figures are at 100.

Ring switching [DP24, §1.3] packs $2^{\kappa}$ bits into each element and assumes a power-of-two degree over $\mathbb{F}_2$, which 192 and 191 are not. So the 192‑bit design packs into $\mathbb{F}_{2^{64}}$ ($\kappa = 6$) and needs the ring-switching sum-check restated over the tensor product of the two fields. The 191‑bit design lays the witness out as 191 columns, which the PIOP batches and the ring switching takes in one step of degree 191. Both are design changes that the paper does not write down. The first-round oracle is the packed witness and has the same bytes under any packing. So the designs differ in the later rounds' elements, 24 bytes against 32, and in the agreement margin, which sets the list: $2^{20}$ to $2^{21}$ at 256 bits, $2^{8}$ to $2^{9}$ at 192 and one less at 191, which costs them no query at these rates. So the 192‑ and 191‑bit proofs are about 8 percent smaller than the 256‑bit ones (176 kbytes and 178 kbytes against 192 kbytes at rate 1/8).

Under ARC the decider's first round opens three oracles, the two child accumulators and the root's witness, each under its own root, which the optimizer prices and schedules for.

Prover commitment work is counted in word multiplications, a 64‑bit carry-less multiplication as one. The PIOP is Flock's measured 82,100 BLAKE3 compressions per second on one core less its commitment (its Table 5: witness generation 5%, zero-check and lincheck 46%). The zero-check and lincheck, which run over $\mathbb{F}_{2^{128}}$, are scaled by half the challenge field's multiplication cost over $\mathbb{F}_{2^{128}}$'s: 4, 8 and 11 carry-less multiplications per product in 128, 192 and 256 bits, Karatsuba over the 64‑bit limbs with the limb reductions, and 7 in $\mathbb{F}_{2^{191}}$, whose one trinomial reduction is cheaper than the tower's six.

The commitment is counted operation by operation. The additive NTT of the first oracle, with a 32‑bit subfield twiddle per limb in the tower fields and a full-field twiddle in $\mathbb{F}_{2^{191}}$, which has no subfield to hold the evaluation domain, and its tree; the ring switching, two passes of a challenge-field element by each witness bit and a sum-check over the packed coefficients; and the folds, the folded trees and the BaseFold sum-check. These are counted in carry-less multiplications, BLAKE3 compressions and word XORs at an eighth each, and scaled to the FRI-Binius paper's single-threaded 144 ms commitment and 160 ms opening at $2^{28}$ bits, rate 1/2, over $\mathbb{F}_{2^{128}}$ (its Table 2). The counts reproduce those at 1.1 and 1.7 times. An ARC step pays the NTT, two trees and the ring switching; a recursive step a full proof, and its merge verifies two, the field multiplications by Karatsuba.

So the prover grows with the inverse rate below 1/4, as the other hash-based rows do. With the grinding chosen per row it is 465 million word multiplications at its least, at rates 1/4 to 1/8, and 1.7 billion at rate 1/64 in the 192‑bit design. The 256‑bit one is up to 10 percent more, and the 191‑bit one from 6 percent less at rate 1/2 to 10 percent more at the lowest rates, where the NTT's full-field twiddles outweigh its cheaper reduction and half-size folds. Flock's whole measured pipeline is 150 million at this witness size and its own rate 1/2 over $\mathbb{F}_{2^{128}}$.

The decider's verifier is the proof's authentication hashes, with the paths pruned as a multiproof, and its leaf hashes at BLAKE3's native cost plus its field multiplications, 0.307 to 1.18 ms. Zero knowledge is modelled in two variants, a batched mask and a Zook-style one, which the zero-knowledge section below describes. It does not support the Pasta base field, so under the dominance rule it can be dominated by anything.

Its proofs under ARC are 110 kbytes to 445 kbytes from rate 1/64 to 1/2 in the 192‑bit design, with the three first-round trees, and under recursion 77 kbytes to 170 kbytes. The prover is least at rate 1/4, with rate 1/8 within a tenth of it, 465 million word multiplications, 9 times Akita over Goldilocks's with Poseidon and a third of it with BLAKE3, whose proof is 68 kbytes to 71 kbytes. The merge is 17 to 77 million AND gates against Akita's 25 thousand Pasta gates with Poseidon, or 0.83 million with BLAKE3.

The price of the field is the application: Pasta arithmetic in a Boolean circuit, or in any small field, pays the embedding and simulation overhead that the small-field survey [Ska26, §5] quantifies, and the Ironwood recovery circuit is Pasta arithmetic. The transaction measure prices that at about 1,650 AND gates per Pasta gate.

BitZ

BitZ [BGK+26] removes that price. It is a hash-based commitment for multilinears over any finitely generated ring. The witness is bit-decomposed and committed packed over $\mathbb{F}_{2^{128}}$ with Flock's Ligerito. An evaluation claim over a ring R is lifted to the integers, through a random prime where R is large, and proved in the exponent of $\mathbb{F}_{2^{128}}$ by batched GKR grand products. So its cost is per committed bit and does not depend on R.

With the Universal Constraint Systems of Zinc+ [AGK+26] the application's Pasta arithmetic is integer relations, integer witnesses with ideal membership modulo p, projected to a random prime field for the PIOP. So the Ironwood recovery circuit stays Pasta arithmetic: a field multiplication is 255 bits of value and 255 of quotient, 510 committed bits, and a hash gate is one bit. So the recovery circuit is about 8.4 million bits, as the Transaction prover section counts. A step of rank $r$ commits $2^r$ bits of application witness, beside the Merkle hashing of the merge, which is most of its witness. The merge is a hybrid circuit: the Merkle hashing over $\mathbb{F}_2$ as for the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family, and the GKR and PIOP verifiers native over the binary extension and the projection field, about 1,700.0 products of committed elements, 424 thousand bits, 1.4 percent of the merge.

Its costs are the paper's Table 10: the PCS at $2^{20}$ to $2^{31}$ committed bits on one thread at 100 bits, interpolated at the merge's bits. The paper's own benchmark, run here on one thread, reproduces those bytes exactly at $2^{25}$ to $2^{28}$ and gives the scalings. On one thread this machine is 1.3 times the paper's and on ten threads it matches, ten threads giving four to five times the single-thread prover.

At 128 bits, which its implementation reaches only in the unique-decoding regime with fold grinding, the Ligerito part of the proof is 1.8 times the 100-bit one and the verifier 1.17 times, while the prover is unchanged. Rate 1/8 has 0.83 of the Ligerito bytes, and at the sizes the rows use its prover is the rate-1/2 one. The grand-product GKR, four fifths of the prover, does not depend on the rate, and the Ligerito phase's penalty for the lower rate appears only from $2^{27}$ bits, where it costs 1.28 times in all. The two phases are priced separately. Rate 1/8 is the proof-size minimum: at rate 1/16, the lowest the implementation allows, the proof is 3% larger again and the prover 27% slower.

The unique-decoding regime is what the implementation certifies at 128 bits; at the Johnson bound it stops at 118 bits, its out-of-domain binding term with one sample. There the proof is 217 kbytes at $2^{25}$ and rate 1/2, against 304 kbytes in the unique-decoding regime at 128 bits. The Ligerito part grows linearly with the bits (1.18 times from 100 to 118). So a Johnson-regime BitZ at 128 bits, which analysis in progress may reach, would be about 231 kbytes, a quarter less than the stand-alone figure under the rows. The rows keep the certified figures.

The prover is the grand-product GKR, 63 of Table 10's 80 ms at $2^{25}$, with Ligerito the rest: as Quang Dao puts it, the prover with grand-product GKR is just really expensive. Zinc+'s PIOP is added from its Table 4: 1.6 times the PCS prover on the application's share, 2.00 ms of verifier, and 36 kbytes of proof.

At rank 13 and rate 1/8 with ARC it comes to 490 kbytes with the decider's three first-round trees, 2.2e8 word multiplications, a 28 million bit merge, and a 7.39 ms decider. Its Pasta arithmetic is one relation per multiplication, and no other hash-based family's row with native or relation Pasta arithmetic has both a smaller proof and a cheaper prover; Kishu, below, beats it on both. Akita's Pasta variants beat it on proof size, and its merge is a Boolean witness of the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's size.

BitZ's paper offers succinctness only; its zero-knowledge rows here are derived, as the zero-knowledge section says. Quang Dao notes that BitZ's arithmetization, the bit-decomposed witnesses with claims over any ring, might in future be combined with a lattice backend such as Akita.

Zero knowledge

Akita's mask

With the box ticked, as it is by default, the Akita configurations carry the masked witness of the zero-knowledge note. The user's fold masks the accumulator witness by rejection sampling. That makes the one masked accumulator about 9 times longer. It also raises the Module-SIS rank of the accumulation matrix about 2.4 times, since the note's extraction argument needs that matrix binding at the masked bound, and every accumulator is committed under it.

The length stays at the zero-knowledge layer. The aggregator's first fold on a masked accumulator range-checks its nine digit slices and folds them back to the ordinary length with its other inputs, as every lattice fold decomposes and folds. So the merges above that layer and the decider's opening see witnesses of the usual size. The rank stays with the accumulation matrix. Akita's internal levels and its terminal commit the final accumulator's ordinary witness under their own matrices and keep their bounds. The accumulation matrix's larger images enter the opening only as the first level's auxiliary digits, which are lower order.

On this page that is 2.4 times the rows term of every step's prover commitment work. The merge priced here is one at the zero-knowledge layer, so it has three more rounds in each of its two sum-checks, 18 atoms. The opening and the decider's verification are unchanged but for one more circuit's claim where those atoms need it. Merges above the zero-knowledge layer pay the rows term only, which the page does not show separately.

Not modelled: the masked accumulator itself, about 14 Mbytes against 1.6 Mbytes, which passes from the user to the aggregator rather than into any proof, and the first level's extra auxiliary digits. Three refinements would move these figures. A design that checks the masked fold under a matrix of its own would confine the rank to that layer, but proving the change of matrix is the obstacle (the note's §5.9). The factor 9 comes from base-8 digits, a Gaussian width of 48 times the norm of the challenge times the witness, and vectors of $2^{14}$ coefficients. So Akita's eventual Pasta profile, its digit base and the witness size that the rank sets all change it. And the 18 atoms assume the first fold's range and norm checks run over the nine slices at once.

The note's §5.6 prices the mask's parameters. This page takes weight-20 challenges in the accumulation matrix's ring dimension of 512, which cost nothing, and a Gaussian 48 times the norm wide, for the 2.4 above, against 2.6 with the note's weight-32 challenges. The note's own example, twelve times the norm, has 2.7 attempts instead of 1.3 and a factor of 2.2; six times the norm has 7.5 attempts, 2.1 and a masked accumulator of 12.6 Mbytes. The choice of width is explained below, and the note's §5.8 moves the attempts off the user's device.

Pedersen-IPA

Pedersen-IPA's blinding costs nothing visible here, so its points do not change.

WHIR and FRI

For the WHIR and FRI families the zero‑knowledge rows carry the overhead measured with Plonky3's HVZK-WHIR, which composes Zook [CFW26] into a hiding commitment. On one core over KoalaBear with a quintic extension, at rate 1/4 and 100 bits, the proof is 103.6 kbytes against 30.9 kbytes at $2^{16}$ and 110.1 kbytes against 37.5 kbytes at $2^{18}$: an additive 73 kbytes of mask spot checks. The prover takes 19 to 22 ms more, 15% at $2^{18}$ and halving per doubling.

Zero knowledge is paid once per transaction, at the handoff from the user's private accumulation to the public aggregator. Under ARC the final accumulator carries the users' masked rows. So the rows add 73 kbytes, scaled with the floor, to the decider's proof and about 1,300 hash checks to its verifier. They put the masked prover's extra work, a few percent of one step at these sizes, in the transaction measure's fold. Under recursion the public merges and the decider's proof are of public data, so the rows equal the plain rows but for that fold.

That difference between the two accumulation styles is structural, not an artefact of the model. Recursion seals the private witness at the handoff, since the aggregator verifies the user's proof and discards it. Accumulation carries the users' oracles onward, as a random combination with everyone else's, until the decider opens the final accumulator, so the masks must still be there then. Akita's accumulation has the same shape, and more: the masked bound sizes the accumulation matrix that every step shares, which is why every Akita step pays the rows term. Confining that rank to the zero-knowledge layer, with a second matrix and proven switches at the handoff, is worked through in the note's §5.9, where the switch's verifier cost is the obstacle. The mask symbols would be larger over Pasta's field, which is not applied, and whether ARC's accumulation preserves zero knowledge has not been examined, so the ARC merge is left unchanged.

The $\mathbb{F}_2$ family

Flock and BitZ offer succinctness only, by their own papers; Flock expects zero knowledge could be added at low cost, through zero‑knowledge IOPPs or VEIL. The $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's zero‑knowledge rows follow the zk option of Ulrich Haböck's optimizer as of 2026-11-12. A mask of the witness's size is added in the first round, which doubles that round's leaves and adds a 32-byte blinder to each. Later rounds carry neither, since the full-entropy mask, once combined with the witness in that round's sum-check, lets them run in the clear. A schedule with no reduction opens witness, mask and blinder at the query positions before the reveal. His revision of 2026-11-10 gave each leaf one mask row, which the page priced for a day; asked whether one row suffices, he confirmed that the mask must be as large as the witness and corrected the script.

The script does not model the prover, so the rows charge the mask's commitment, equal to the witness's, once per transaction in the fold. At rate 1/8 that is 28 kbytes more proof under ARC (204 kbytes against 176 kbytes) and two thirds of the row's prover in the fold. Under recursion the rows equal the plain rows but for that fold, as for WHIR. He adds that the leaf blinders could probably be dropped, since the mask has full entropy at leaf level, but keeps them because they make the analysis clean. Only the private witness needs masking, not the public polynomials, so the three first-round oracles that the ARC decider opens masked here are an upper bound. An earlier revision opened the mask in a separate round, which cost about 105 kbytes under ARC.

The second variant, labelled Zook-style and derived rather than measured, prices Zook's masks [CFW26] for FRI-Binius's own structure. The main oracle's zero‑knowledge encoding costs nothing in the proof, since its random coefficients fit the witness padding. Each folding round's sum-check variables get a group of four-coefficient masks, each with its own randomness, encoded at rate 1/256 and spot-checked 16 times through a pruned multiproof; the ring-switching claim's revealed element gets a mask of $2^{\kappa}$ coefficients; and the terminal takes a full mask of its own size. FRI-Binius has no code switching and no out-of-domain samples, which are what make WHIR's masks large, since their message must hold the previous encoding's randomness, about a hundred coefficients. So its masks stay small, and as they belong to the proof rather than to the oracles, ARC's three trees do not multiply them. That gives about 30 kbytes more proof under ARC, with the masks' encoding in the fold, and nothing beyond the fold under recursion. Under ARC these rows cost a little more proof than the batched mask from rate 1/8 down (32 kbytes against 28 kbytes at 1/8) and less at rate 1/4, and a hundredth of the row's prover against two thirds.

Ulrich Haböck's view is that the full-entropy mask in a batching step before FRI is the way to randomize FRI-Binius. A more frugal mask would need the first rounds' sum-checks made zero-knowledge too, which is what these rows price. So they stay as the derived lower estimate. The one cross-check is that the same count reproduces Plonky3's measured 73 kbytes for WHIR. In the Zook-style rows the ring-switching mask is this page's design, not the paper's, and in both variants the ARC merge is left unchanged, as for WHIR.

Kishu

Kishu's sum-check rows carry Akita's mask as the lattice rows do: the masked rank on every step's digits, 18 more atoms in the zk-layer merge, and the user's masking fold at the handoff. The fold's rejection sampling runs over the accumulator's $K \cdot 2^{\mathrm{rank}}$ digits, one per committed bit, so the fold grows with the rank where the merges shrink. For the recovery circuit Kishu's zero-knowledge optimum is rank 20, 1.44 s per note against 0.781 s without, 0.0566 s of it the sampling at 7.1 ns a sample. Its projection rows reveal coordinates and drop out, as the binary-groups family does, and as Pedersen-IPA drops out at 147 bits.

Where that cost goes. Of the 1.44 s, the fold is 0.231 s: the sampling 0.0566 s, the masks' commitments under the masked matrix 0.133 s and the fold's own merge 0.0413 s. The larger part is the steps and their merges: 9 at 134 ms, 1.20 s against 0.777 s without zero knowledge. Two things make them dear. The masked rank raises the commitment rate of every step and merge from 23.7 to 49.3 word multiplications a digit. And the fold grows with the rank, so it pulls the optimum from rank 21 to rank 20, which multiplies the merges. The constraint PIOP is 4.09 ms.

Three things set it.

The sampler

The sampler is the one term that is pure implementation. The page's 7.1 ns a coordinate is measured on one core of an Apple M4 Max: AES-CTR randomness on the AES instructions, Box–Muller in doubles, Sheppard's correction, the tail check and the rounding, with 1.5 ns of it the randomness. With ChaCha20 in plain C the randomness is 7.7 ns and the whole path 14. The AVX Box–Muller sampler of Hülsing, Lange and Smeets [HLS18] takes 7.7 to 13 ns a coordinate on a 2014 core, measured without and with its ChaCha20 randomness. By Jeudy's theorem [Jeu26], a rounded Gaussian with Sheppard's correction, $\sigma - 1/(24\sigma)$, is a discrete Gaussian of width $\sigma$ up to a Rényi divergence of $1 + 73aN/(33{,}177{,}600\,\sigma^8)$ over $N$ coordinates. At this width that is below $2^{-179}$, so the rejection-sampling argument carries over unchanged. What double precision cannot do is round exactly. A coordinate's error is its size times $2^{-53}$, which leaves about $2^{-25}$ of the coordinates undecided between two integers, one coordinate in $2^{3}$ folds. That fraction is also the worst-case bound of [HLS18] on the divergence the error would leave. Refining those few coordinates in higher precision makes the rounding exact. The exact alternative, Karney's method [Kar16], costs about 300 ns a coordinate, measured at 290 to 470 ns by Du, Fan and Wei [DFW20]. With it the recovery would take 3.00 s at rank 19, and at 100 ns 2.19 s at rank 19.

The transaction's shape

The fold is paid once per transaction, so a transaction with four recovery inputs takes 0.971 s per note at rank 21. What does not help: the two-matrix design of the note's §5.9, whose switch costs an in-circuit opening; the bimodal variant, which needs a structure that hides a sign (the note's open point 9); and masking in blocks (its §5.7), which is open.

BitZ

BitZ's zero-knowledge rows are derived, since its paper offers succinctness only. Ligero's random row per Ligerito level puts one symbol in every opened column, so that the columns reveal nothing. That is 16 bytes per opened column in the decider's proof under ARC, about 20 kbytes at 128 bits with the three first-round trees, and nothing under recursion. Each sum-check gets a Libra-style mask polynomial: Flock's zero-check and lincheck, Zinc+'s PIOP and each layer of the GKR, a sum of univariates of degree three in each variable. The masks are committed as extra rows of the same Ligerito matrix under those random rows and opened with the witness, so that each sum-check runs over the masked polynomial. The masks are 1,600 field elements for a $2^{25}$-bit witness, under a percent of the step, paid in the fold. Zook-style spot-checked mask oracles would cost about 100 kbytes here, one oracle per sum-check round over the GKR's 325 rounds, so they are not the choice. Whether ARC's accumulation preserves zero knowledge is not examined for BitZ either.

Security floor

Akita's catalog sizes every matrix so that the cheapest known attack on its Module-SIS instance costs at least 128 bits under the ADPS16 quantum core-SVP model, $2^{0.265\beta}$ for BKZ block size $\beta$. That puts every instance at block size 484 to 486. ARC's openings are counted at the same $\lambda$, and the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's optimizer runs at the floor, which shrinks its lists.

The 147‑bit setting re-plans Akita's catalog at that floor with Akita's own planner, so its openings are planned rather than scaled. The floor is 147 rather than 150 because Akita's rank-one compression maps certify at 147.3 bits at the catalog's geometry; the margin section below explains. The rank growth at that floor is applied to the prover's rows term and to the inner images' share of the verification. That share is the terminal's clear inner images at the shipped geometry, 1,344 coordinates whose bytes Module-SIS sets independently of the modulus. They are 33 percent of the opening at the 128-bit field, 35 on Goldilocks, 30 with 64-bit commitments, 26 with Pasta images at SIS size, and 42 with Pasta for everything, where they are 32-byte field elements. The verifier's work is taken to divide as the message does.

WHIR's proofs, measured over both fields, are 16 percent larger at 147 bits than at 128, and ARC's openings grow with the floor; the margin section below has both.

Pedersen-IPA over Pasta rests on the discrete logarithm in a group of order near $2^{254}$: about 126 classical bits by Pollard rho, and nothing against a quantum adversary. It is shown at the 128‑bit floor as the conventional pre‑quantum baseline. It drops out at 147 bits, which it does not reach even classically.

The binary-groups row is shown at 128 bits only too, since its Module-SIS certificate is not yet issued.

Grinding

The hash-based families can spend proof of work in the query phase: with $g$ bits of grinding the queries need carry only $\lambda - g$ bits. So ARC's openings per input fall to $2(\lambda - g)/\log_2(1/\rho)$, and WHIR's own query counts fall in the same way. A fixed setting applies to every hash-based row at once, from none, 16, 20 and 24 bits. The default, best per row, gives each configuration the measured setting with the smallest prover, from a wider set of measurements, and the row's label says which.

Each row's proof size is measured at its setting with the reference implementation over both fields, rates 1/2 to 1/64, at $2^{17}$, $2^{18}$ and $2^{20}$, with the growth per doubling taken over the three doublings. The earlier rows were measured at 20 bits while ARC's openings were counted without grinding, which this corrects. At rate 1/8 over the 256‑bit field the proof is 147 kbytes with no grinding, 130 kbytes at 16 bits, 126 kbytes at 20 and 121 kbytes at 24; at rate 1/32 it is 113 kbytes, 100 kbytes, 96 kbytes and 93 kbytes.

The prover pays $2^g$ evaluations of the grinding hash per merge, taken as the family's own Merkle hash so that the merge circuit's check is one node per input. Each bit of grinding removes about one opening in a hundred, so about a percent of the circuits and of the prover, while the grinding's cost doubles. The prover is least where the two cross: at 17 to 19 bits for families with Poseidon trees ($2^{17}$ permutations are about 0.6 percent of a merge's prover at rate 1/8 and rank 13, $2^{20}$ about 5 percent), and 12 to 18 bits for the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family and BitZ. The minimum is shallow, within a percent from 16 to 20 bits.

With BLAKE3 trees, the default, the WHIR-type rows take 24 bits, the most measured, since $2^{24}$ compressions are a small part of a merge spread over tens of thousands of circuits. The settings and the lower rates follow Ulrich Haböck's questions. The capacity regime, which would halve the openings again, is not offered, since its conjectures were refuted.

Hashes in circuits

Every hash that a merge circuit computes is one of two. The default is a conventional hash, BLAKE3, at 14,900 gates per 64-byte compression in a lookup-free R1CS over Pasta or Goldilocks and about 200 word multiplications natively. The option allows an arithmetic hash, Poseidon 1.1 over the circuit's own field: 288 gates per Pasta permutation absorbing four elements, about 480 for the width-12 Goldilocks one, and 35,000 or 7,200 word multiplications natively.

Arithmetic hashes deserve consideration on the merits: for large fields and the widths in question, none of the attacks on Poseidon 1.1 has come near an academic break, and Tachyon uses it. The reason to exclude them by default is future-proofing. For long-term storage that reason is decisive, though some of a proof system's hashes are ones that long-term storage does not depend on. If requiring conventional hashes causes only a small regression that does not affect deployability, they are the choice; the option shows what the arithmetic hash buys.

With the option the rows priced with Poseidon join the BLAKE3 ones rather than replace them. So a configuration that is better on some measure with the conventional hash stays on the plot, and each frontier takes the better of the two. Ragu's checked-in circuits use Poseidon 1.1, so the Pedersen-IPA rows exist only under the option.

What the default changes: Akita's merge absorbs its transcript, 4 to 9 kbytes, in 55 to 133 compressions instead of 146 to 218 Poseidon atoms. So the merge grows from about 25 thousand gates to 0.83 million at the 128‑bit field and on Goldilocks and 2.0 million for the Pasta variants, at rank 16. Each step circuit's evaluation claim then costs more to absorb than a rank-13 circuit holds, and a 32-byte claim more than a rank-14 circuit. So rank 13 has no Akita rows and rank 14 none for the Pasta variants, and the first ranks that hold them take 1,740 and 1,471 circuits.

At rank 16 the merge takes 51 circuits at the 128‑bit field and on Goldilocks and 122 for the Pasta variants, against 2 with Poseidon. So the merge prover is 26 times the Poseidon one (8.6e9 against 3.4e8 at the 128‑bit field, 1.3e9 against 5.1e7 on Goldilocks) and 61 times for the Pasta variants. The proof grows by 3 to 4 kbytes at the small fields and by 9 to 12 kbytes with Pasta claims, from the per-circuit claims, and the atoms by 49 to 120. The per-step commitment cost and the decider are unchanged. The lever that would recover most of this is to aggregate the step circuits' claims before absorption, a design change.

For the WHIR-type rows every Merkle node becomes a BLAKE3 compression, so the merge grows 35 to 61 times (1.1 to 65 million gates for WHIR over Pasta at rate 1/8 and rank 13, 524 to 31,536 circuits). The prover grows 1.3 to 1.9 times for WHIR over either field and for FRI, since the trees get cheap but the circuits multiply. The limb hybrid's prover instead falls 2.2 times, since its Poseidon over limbs is dear natively. The decider's verification falls 17 to 81 times, to a few milliseconds; the proofs grow by a sixth with the larger batched witness, and the best grinding moves to 24 bits.

The $\mathbb{F}_2$ family, BitZ and the binary-groups family hash with BLAKE3 already and do not change, so under the default their comparison with Akita is like for like. At rate 1/8 the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's ARC row proves a merge in 5e8 word multiplications for a proof of 176 kbytes, against Akita over Goldilocks at rank 16 with 1.3e9 and 71 kbytes. Its merge is 17 to 77 million AND gates against Akita's 0.83 million Pasta gates, which are not the same unit.

Transaction prover

The transaction prover measure prices a whole transaction rather than one merge, so that the cost of zero knowledge can be seen against the steps it belongs to. The transaction proves an application chosen above: the Ironwood recovery circuit; a transaction with four recovery inputs, which is four of the circuit under one zero-knowledge handoff; or a number of Merkle path checks of 32 levels and the leaf; or the hardware wallet's part of the recovery statement on its own, priced in the application subsection; the Tachyon transactions have the next section.

The application is costed component by component in each family's own arithmetization, under the selected hash policy. The family's steps are then as many as hold it, rounded up, since a transaction smaller than a step pays a whole step. A step circuit of rank $r$ commits $2^r$ coefficients, which is $2^{r-2}$ gates of four wires for the field arithmetizations, $2^r$ bits or digits for BitZ and Kishu, and $2^{22}$ AND gates for the Boolean families, which have no rank. Each step is committed at the family's per-step cost, which is the row's prover divided by its circuits, and there are as many merges at the row's accumulation prover, the merge's commitments and the fold's sum-checks.

The application in each arithmetization

Off Pasta the recovery circuit's curve arithmetic is emulated, in the ways Pasta as a requirement lists: about 14,500 multiplications in Pallas's base field, in five fixed-base and one variable-base scalar multiplication with complete additions, 160 Sinsemilla chunk additions and the nullifier's Poseidon. Its seven BLAKE2b compressions, the chunks' generator selections and its Merkle path are bit operations and cost about the same gates in any prime field. Over Pasta the circuit is 570,000 gates with a Poseidon path and 1.05 million with a BLAKE3 one. Over Goldilocks a multiplication is about 1,100 gates, with nine 29‑bit limbs and no lookups, so the circuit is 16.5 and 17.0 million gates; at a 128‑bit field, with 61‑bit limbs, about 700 gates give 10.7 and 11.2 million.

In a Boolean circuit a multiplication is about 120,000 AND gates, the 255‑bit product by Karatsuba and Toom–Cook and a Montgomery reduction by a prime of no special form. A 64‑bit addition is 64 ANDs and an XOR is free, so the hashes cost half their Pasta gates. The circuit is 1.73 billion AND gates, the multiplications nearly all of it.

In BitZ's integer arithmetization a multiplication is 510 committed bits, the value and the quotient, checked as one integer relation, while a hash gate is one bit and a 64‑bit addition 65, so the circuit is 8.4 million bits. In Kishu over Akita the claims are in one fixed field, so a multiplication is either 1,088 digits by the limb route or 510 by the fingerprint route, which pays per step instead. The circuit is 17 million or 8.5 million digits, plus its constraint PIOP, which the Kishu section prices. The bit arithmetizations hash the path with BLAKE3 under either policy, since Poseidon over Pallas's field would cost them 240 multiplications a node.

A Merkle path check is 33 nodes: 9,500 Pasta gates, or 15,600 Goldilocks or 128‑bit-field gates, with Poseidon in the field; 492,000 gates in any prime field with BLAKE3; 270,000 AND gates; or 497,000 bits or digits. So the recovery circuit's hashes are what make the bit arithmetizations cheap, a path-heavy application makes them cheaper still, and the Boolean families pay for every Pallas multiplication.

Two more applications price the hardware wallet's part of the Recovery Statement, which ZIP 2005's Option A proves on the device that holds the quantum spending key, the host proving the rest ([ZIP-2005], Key storage options). The device's own statement, $\mathsf{qk} = \mathsf{H^{qk}}(\mathsf{qsk})$ with the linking commitment left out, is one BLAKE3 compression: 14,900 gates in any prime field, 8,190 AND gates, or 15,100 bits or digits. If the device instead signs SigHash with leanSPHINCS, the stateless hash-based signature of post-quantum Ethereum [EIP-8288] [Lit26], the host proves the verifier with the signature and the public key as witnesses, so recovery spends stay unlinkable. For the layered two-level WOTS forest key of lifetime $2^{30}$ of [Wam26] that is 408 Keccak-f permutations or 462 BLAKE2s compressions, plus the 6,332-byte signature as input bits; Ethereum's provisional set, with FORS in place of the forest, is 515 permutations. XOR is not free in a digit arithmetization, so a Keccak-f permutation is 154,000 committed bits where a BLAKE2s compression is 21,300. The Keccak verifier is 62.7 million digits against 9.90 million for the BLAKE2s one, 6.3 times as many, which is Ethereum's open hash question seen from the circuit. In a Boolean circuit with XOR free the ratio is 3.2. With zero knowledge Kishu proves the BLAKE2s verifier in 1.38 s on one core at rank 21 and the Keccak one in 6.39 s at rank 21, against 1.44 s for the recovery circuit. The device's statement alone is 0.109 s at rank 15, the merge 48 percent of it and the masking fold 47 percent: the circuit itself is a few percent.

The wallet need not prove that fold. The handoff merge's witness is simulatable (the zero-knowledge note's §4.2: the hiding commitment, the mask's commitment, the challenge, the claims and the obligation transcripts), so the host can derive the challenge, prove the fold's merge and the obligations (the note's §5.8 deferral), and verify the leaf's sum-checks in its own merge. For that the wallet proves its constraint and Booleanity sum-checks under Libra-style masking polynomials, 120 committed elements here, since the host sees their transcripts. The wallet keeps what touches the secret: its leaf's commitment with a hiding padding at the masked rank, the sum-checks, and the response $z = y + cs$ with the rejection test, the mask sampled and committed in idle time. The padding is computational rather than the note's leftover-hash one: $A\rho$ for $\rho$ of 5,000 uniform bits is pseudorandom if knapsack-LWE is hard at the commitment's rows, which the primal estimate at the page's block size puts at about 3,300 LWE dimensions for binary error at the 64‑bit modulus, to be confirmed with the lattice estimator; the statistical padding would be 89 thousand bits, six times the statement. So the wallet commits 27,800 digits. Online that is 0.603 ms on one M4 core, under a twentieth of the handoff fold the measure charges the user, with 0.836 ms in idle time and a response of 76 kbytes at 22 bits a coordinate. At 10 million word multiplications a second for an MCU-class device and 3 million for a secure element, assumptions scaled from [Wam26]'s hash rates on a Ledger Nano S+, the computation takes 0.18 and 0.60 s. The transfer of $z$ at that device's measured 21 kbytes a second takes 3.6 s, 1.3 s with a pipelined transport, and is the cost that does not move.

The masking fold

For Akita's zero-knowledge rows the user's masking fold is added. It is the expected rejection-sampling attempts, 1.3 at the page's Gaussian width, plus the fold's own merge. Each attempt is a commitment of a mask the size of the accumulator under the masked accumulation matrix, and as many Gaussian samples at about 7.1 nanoseconds a sample as a rounded Gaussian with exact rounding, 21 word multiplications at the page's rate. The sampling does not depend on the modulus. The fold is paid once per transaction whatever it proves, since the masked accumulator is the step's size. The hash-based zero-knowledge rows likewise put their masked prover's extra work in the fold, once per transaction; under recursion that is their only zero-knowledge cost, since the public merges and the decider's proof are of public data. Pedersen-IPA's blinding is free. This is total work, not latency: the zero-knowledge note's §5.8 precomputes candidate masks in idle time and defers the decomposition to the aggregator, which leaves the device a sparse ring product and the transfer of the masked witness. An Orchard-style action has the recovery circuit's mix, with one path check and no BLAKE2b.

Tachyon transactions

The transaction options of 2, 4, 8 or 16 tachyactions, half spends and half outputs, keep Tachyon's own proof tree (tachyon-zcash/tachyon 73031f7, Stamp::prove). A spend is two steps, an output a seed and a step, each action after the first a merge, then the fee step and Ragu's rerandomization: 3s + 3o + 1 nodes for s spends and o outputs.

The page re-packs that tree for each rank's step size: a node larger than a step takes whole steps and its remainder joins the rest, which fill steps first-fit decreasing, and the rerandomization is its own step. So at rank 13 over Pasta no two action circuits share a step and the merge rides with the fee step, 6 steps for two tachyactions against Tachyon's 7. At rank 15 every circuit of two tachyactions fits one step.

The nodes' circuits were measured through Ragu's synthesis analysis on Tachyon's steps at its header size of 9: 1,630 and 1,649 gates for a spend's two steps, 1,467 and 1,351 for an output's, 17 for an action merge and 1,934 for the fee step, in Pasta gates with Poseidon, all within rank 13's 2,048. In the other arithmetizations the transaction is a post-quantum variant of Tachyon in a new shielded pool: the Poseidon permutations, 192 gates each in Ragu's gate model, are hashed under the page's policy; the fixed-base multiplications' window selections cost the bits' four monomials; and the window additions and the openings of staged values at $\zeta$ are Pallas multiplications. The remaining gates are the scalars' bit checks, the canonicity and range comparisons and the step's glue, priced as bit operations, one gate or committed bit each, which understates any among them that multiply field elements. So a node that is one step over Pasta is 6 to 8 steps of rank 17 over Goldilocks with Poseidon and 5 to 7 Boolean steps, which is where the range of step counts comes from. Pricing the remaining gates as multiplications too would give 27 to 39 Goldilocks steps.

Per-note steps that run in the background are not counted. A Tachyon transaction also needs Tachyon's header of nine elements, so while one is selected every row is shown at that header. The recovery circuit's options do the same, since its primary input needs six or seven elements.

How much to trust the Module-SIS parameters

Akita's advantage over the hash-based route rests on Module-SIS, where that route rests on a random oracle alone and Pedersen-IPA on the discrete logarithm. This section states what the parameters assume and where the uncertainty sits.

The sizing rule

Every matrix in Akita's catalog is sized so that the cheapest known attack on its Module-SIS instance costs at least $2^{128}$ operations under the quantum core-SVP model of [ADPS16]. That model charges $2^{0.265\beta}$ for lattice reduction with BKZ block size $\beta$ and ignores the polynomial factors.

The block size that reaches a given root Hermite factor is taken from the BKZ 2.0 analysis of [CN11]. Every instance in the catalog lands at block size 484 to 486, in lattices of dimension 2,000 to 4,800. The catalog's own security check reports 128.26 bits on each.

A cross-check with the maintained lattice estimator [LE] agrees on the ranks. The estimator is maintained; the paper that introduced it, [APS15], is not, and its authors say so in a 2019 note. The estimator costs BKZ as $8n$ calls to an SVP oracle of the block size, with sieving per [BDGL16] classically and per [LMvdP15] and [Laa16] quantumly. It follows [ADPS16] for the primal attack, as [AGVW17] established, and leaves out the dimensions for free of [Duc18] and [ADH+19]. The same block size under other models:

For calibration, ML-DSA [FIPS204], the standardized Dilithium, sizes its SIS instances at block sizes of about 423, 638 and 909 for its three security levels [Dil21]. These parameters therefore sit between its levels 2 and 3 on the same model.

Where the uncertainty sits

Three places, in decreasing order of concern.

  • Proof-level slack, not the assumption. Lattice schemes have slipped before by sizing for honest norms rather than the extractor's. Akita sizes for the extractor's collision bound, which is why the masked variant needs rank 340 against 143. But the knowledge-soundness proof in the quantum random-oracle model is still in progress. And the decoupled commitment modulus has no soundness argument yet beyond the LaBinius analogue. A gap of that kind moves parameters by a lot, not by twenty bits.
  • The instantiation is unusual. Ring dimension 64 with 128‑bit or 255‑bit moduli, compression rings of dimension 8 and 4, and norm bounds up to $2^{48}$ are far from the regime of the standardized schemes. No attack exploits module structure beyond constant factors. Module-SIS has a worst-case reduction [LS15], and the quantum attacks on ideal lattices [CDW17] need principal ideals, which modules of rank 64 and more do not present. But there is less cryptanalytic attention on this corner, and the $\ell_\infty$ bounds take their own estimator route.
  • The cost model. Core-SVP is already a lower-bound style model. Refinements since 2016, such as the dimensions for free of [Duc18], have moved concrete estimates by ten to twenty bits either way. The disputed dual-attack improvements of [MATZOV22], reassessed in [DP23], concern LWE rather than SIS, which is attacked through the primal route. A 128‑bit quantum floor is 142 classical bits, and the paranoid model still gives 101.

Against the other assumptions

The hash-based route's assumption is weaker in the good sense: a random oracle and collision resistance only. Its soundness risk is concentrated in the proximity-gap conjectures, which is exactly where the 2025 refutations landed. The proven Johnson regime is therefore where that route is priced here.

Pedersen-IPA rests on the discrete logarithm on Pasta, about 126 classical bits and nothing against a quantum adversary. So the fair statement is that Akita dominates on efficiency while adding one assumption whose concrete hardness is estimated rather than proven. That assumption has SIS studied since [Ajt96], Module-SIS since [LS15], and standardization behind it.

A margin

Raising the floor from 128 to 147 quantum bits moves the block size to about 556. The security-floor option above re-plans Akita's catalog at that floor with Akita's own planner. Its single security policy is retargeted to 147 bits, its runtime width tables are regenerated by its estimator at that floor, and its six rank-one compression cells are re-sized from them. So the openings at 147 bits are planned, not scaled.

The floor is 147 rather than 150 because the compression chain is built from rank-one maps. At the catalog's geometry its first map, at ring dimension 16 and width 4,096, certifies at 147.3 bits. A 150-bit floor allows that map a width of 3,806, so the chain would need rank-two maps, or two maps where it has one, and either doubles the compressed images at every level.

The planner does not give the prover's rows term or the verifier's inner-image share. For those the model takes the weakest Module-SIS instance of each of the 115 schedules in the shipped catalog's security report and, for each, the smallest module rank that covers the instance's width at both floors. With the catalog's own mix of moduli the ranks sum to 121 at 128 bits and to 150 at 147 bits, a factor of 1.24, with 29 instances moving up a rank. Over a 64‑bit modulus, where the ranks are higher to begin with, the sums are 185 and 211, a factor of 1.14, with 26 moving. Over Pallas the instances sit at the width cap at both floors but one: the sums are 117 and 118, a factor of 1.01, with 1 moving.

Where the floor binds, the maximum secure widths shrink several-fold. At 147 bits the median shrink, over the cells where neither width is at the estimator's search cap and the width falls, is a factor of 2 to 3 at ring dimensions up to 32, 4 to 10 at 64, 4 to 13 at 128 and 256, and 6 to 15 at 512 and above, with the 32-bit modulus at the low end of each range. The few cells at the largest dimensions whose 147-bit width is still finite shrink about 36-fold.

The hash-based rows at the higher floor

The floor moves the hash-based rows too, through $\lambda$ alone and independently of any lattice instance. WHIR's proofs, measured with the reference implementation at $2^{17}$ over both fields at rates 1/2 to 1/8, are 16 percent larger at 147 bits than at 128, and its verifier's hashes 12 percent more; ARC's openings per input, $2(\lambda - g)/\log_2(1/\rho)$, grow by 15 percent.
