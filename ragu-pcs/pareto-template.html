Ragu PCS Pareto

This page costs Ragu over a wide range of parameters — with its pre-quantum Pedersen-IPA decider, and adapted to various post-quantum PCD schemes. The lattice-based scheme Akita is considered under five field and modulus choices, and with an experimental BitZ-style arithmetization over it, called Kishu. For hash-based schemes, we consider: WHIR over Pasta, over Goldilocks, and with Pasta claims in 64‑bit limbs; a Plonky3-style FRI over Goldilocks; the Flock/Binius-style binary-field family; and BitZ, each with ARC accumulation and with its own recursion. A further experimental draft is Flock-style Boolean circuits over Akita's binary groups.

The frontier plot

Each point is one configuration. Its coordinates are compressed proof bytes and two measures chosen from the same eight: the accumulation prover; the prover work for one step's commitments; merge gates; the atoms that the merge circuit absorbs; the decider's verification work; the prover work for a whole transaction; the accumulator's memory; or the peak memory of a fuse. The hash-based rows' rate and grinding, the security floor, zero knowledge, and the hashes allowed in circuits (conventional only, or Poseidon 1.1 too) are selectable. The WHIR-type and Plonky3 families start unticked in the families list, since Akita beats them on every measure and their many rows crowd the picture.

Each class, pre-quantum (Pedersen-IPA) and post‑quantum (the rest), has its own frontier: the configurations of the class, among the selection above, that nothing else in the class dominates. A configuration dominates another when it is at least as good on six axes and better on at least one. The axes are compressed proof bytes, the selected second and third measures, and three requirements, described under Pasta and the scheme assumption and under Parameter changes against design changes: native or relation Pasta arithmetic, the security assumption, and the changes a configuration needs. The table lists both frontiers, and each family's own frontier besides, marked "family" in the last column, so that every scheme's best configurations appear even where its class dominates it. Header size only adds bytes, the same to every row, so the control sets one header for all.

Tolerance

Dominance is taken to a tolerance, 2% by default. This is ε-dominance [LTDZ02]: a configuration ε-dominates another when it is no more than the tolerance worse on every measure, and no worse on the three requirements. So a step rank that buys a hair less proof is not thereby a different choice. The tolerance is selectable under Frontier: exact, 2%, or 5%.

The frontier is built without a grid, which would keep two configurations that are within the tolerance of each other whenever a cell boundary fell between them. The configurations are taken in increasing order of the product of their three measures, and one is kept unless a configuration already kept ε-dominates it. So every configuration left out is ε-dominated by a kept one, every kept configuration is undominated in the exact sense, and of two kept configurations with the same requirements, the one taken later is better than the other by more than the tolerance on some measure. The product orders the configurations whatever the measures' units.

Pasta and the scheme assumption

Three things enter the dominance test as requirements rather than costs: the Pasta arithmetic that the Ironwood recovery circuit needs, the security assumption, and the changes to Ragu that a configuration needs, which a later section takes up. The table's Pasta arithmetic column says how each scheme realises the first:

  • native: a gate over Pasta's field (Pedersen-IPA, the Pasta variants of Akita, WHIR over Pasta and the limb hybrid, whose witness and merge circuit are over Pasta);
  • relation, limbs: the multiplication as an integer relation over committed digits, with the result and the quotient committed and no committed intermediate wires, checked coefficient by coefficient over 29‑bit limbs with their carries committed (Kishu's limb route);
  • relation, fingerprint: the same relation checked in a few PIOP instances over random primes, as BitZ does it, without the limbs and carries (BitZ, and Kishu's fingerprint route);
  • limbs: limb products as committed wires in another field (the 128‑bit and Goldilocks rows and Plonky3); or
  • bits: a Boolean circuit (the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family and the binary groups).

The rule counts the first three (native and the two relation variants): such a configuration is dominated only by another such, while a limbs or bits configuration can be dominated by any. So the post‑quantum frontier always keeps the best native and relation points beside the cheaper other-field ones. How efficiently each realisation performs is what the transaction measure's application costs show.

The assumption works the same way: a configuration is dominated only by one that assumes no more. The hash-based families rest on a random oracle alone. Akita's and Kishu's rows rest on Module-SIS as well, so a Kishu row never removes a Flock or BitZ row from the post‑quantum frontier, however much cheaper it is. The Boolean-over-Akita row's security ledger is open, which is treated as making a stronger assumption. So are BitZ's rows under BEEP, which rest on an analysis that no implementation certifies yet.

Which measures

The second and third measures are chosen independently from the same eight. The defaults are the accumulation prover for the second and the transaction prover for the third, with the Ironwood recovery circuit as the transaction. The transaction prover may be both measures, each with its own application, so that two applications can be compared. The frontier is taken on proof bytes and both measures. The accumulation prover is what one fuse costs the prover beyond its own step: the merge circuits' commitments, which are prover commitment work, plus the fold's sum-checks over the accumulator, and for Kishu its merge's constraint PIOP.

Merge gates is offered since every family has an estimate of them. Accumulation atoms is kept because it is the more robust proxy for the lattice merge, whose gate count is estimated. For the hash-based families atoms say nothing, since their merge cost is the Merkle hashing. Decider work is the decider's verification cost: under ten milliseconds for the 64-bit and 128-bit lattice rows, the binary-field families and the hash-based deciders with BLAKE3 trees; 2.04 to 27.9 ms for Akita's three Pasta variants, Kishu and BitZ; 41.9 to 384 ms for Pedersen-IPA; and, with Poseidon trees, 5.33 to 33.8 ms for WHIR over Goldilocks and 25.3 to 167 ms for WHIR over Pasta. So it rarely decides a frontier, but it is there to check.

Transaction prover is the expected work to prove the application chosen below: the Ironwood recovery circuit, a transaction with four recovery inputs, the hardware wallet's part of the recovery statement on its own (the device's $\mathsf{H^{qk}}$ statement, or a leanSPHINCS verifier with either BLAKE2s or Keccak-256), a Zoodles transaction (two inputs and two outputs of a sketched protocol with no curve arithmetic) or one epoch of a Zoodles note's lineage, a number of Merkle path checks, or a Tachyon transaction of 2 to 16 tachyactions in Tachyon's own proof tree. For the post-quantum families the Tachyon transaction is Tachyon's own circuits, a pre-quantum protocol, proven in a post-quantum proof system, with their hashes under the page's policy. That is not a useful thing to deploy, which is why the Zoodles options exist, but it compares the proof systems on the same application. The recovery circuit recovers notes of the existing pool, so its nullifier stays Poseidon over Pasta. The application is costed in each family's own arithmetization, so it takes more steps where Pallas arithmetic must be emulated. The measure charges each step at the family's per-step commitment cost and one merge per step at the row's accumulation prover. The zero-knowledge rows also pay the masking work at the handoff to the public aggregator. The Transaction prover section below gives each application's cost in each arithmetization.

Parameter changes against design changes

The changes a configuration needs are the third requirement and work the same way, in the order of the table's Changes needed column: parameters only, then changes to Ragu's code, then changes to its design. A configuration is dominated only by one that needs no more, so a design change never removes a parameter-only configuration from the frontier, for example. The table's last column says whether a row is on its class's frontier or only on its family's. The per-family box collapses each family to its own frontier instead, so that a dominated family still shows its best configurations.

Step rank

A step at step rank $r$ commits $2^r$ coefficients: $2^{r-2}$ gates of four wires for the field arithmetizations, and $2^r$ bits or digits for BitZ and Kishu, which alone go past step rank 17.

For Pedersen-IPA, the circuit budget below limits the endoscalings per step and the binders at each step rank, which makes step rank a real trade-off. A larger circuit holds more endoscalings and fewer binders, which removes committed components and with them bytes and atoms, while the IPA grows by 128 bytes per step rank and every remaining polynomial doubles. Step rank 14 with the fixed circuits packed beats step rank 13 on bytes, prover commitment work and atoms; step ranks 15 and 16 buy bytes and atoms with prover commitment work.

For Akita the step circuits' witnesses open as one batch, so with Poseidon the step rank moves bytes and atoms only through each circuit's own evaluation claim and the last circuit's unused capacity, a few hundred bytes across the range. With BLAKE3 the claims' absorption makes the step rank decisive, as the Hashes section says. The more significant trade is the field choice: a smaller field buys fewer bytes and less prover commitment work at the cost of more atoms absorbed per merge.

Step rank 12 has no Pedersen-IPA rows, since a 1,024-gate circuit holds no binder challenge, and no Akita rows under BLAKE3. Step rank 17 helps Akita under BLAKE3, where fewer circuits absorb fewer claims: 10 to 28 percent off the merge prover, and 0.5 kbytes off the proof at the small fields, 3 kbytes for the Pasta variants.

The WHIR-type rows depend on the step rank only through their circuit count, so they are shown at step ranks 13 to 16.

BitZ, whose steps commit $2^{\text{step rank}}$ bits, goes on to step rank 24, where one step holds the recovery circuit. Its merge is nearly a fixed cost, so the transaction measure falls with the step rank until then, while the proof and the merge prover barely move.

Kishu's rows are not indexed by a step rank, and the step-rank filter does not apply to them. A Kishu step is one committed witness of a chosen length in digits, with one evaluation claim, since its constraints are a sum-check over the whole table. The rows are at lengths from $1.5 \times 2^{20}$ to $1.875 \times 2^{25}$ digits, eight to each doubling, where the application's room is at least a sixteenth of the step. The step's merge is nearly a fixed cost, and the rest of the step is the application's. Step rank 12 gives BitZ a slightly smaller proof and merge prover.

ARC against each scheme's own recursion

Every hash-based scheme appears twice, with ARC accumulation and, in a lighter shade of the same colour, with full recursion: the merge circuit verifies its two input proofs, each step emits a full proof, and the decider verifies one. The hash and the field are the same. The proofs differ, since the recursive decider verifies one stand-alone proof while the ARC decider's first round opens three oracles. So the recursive rows' proofs are a little over half the ARC rows' (212 kbytes against 376 kbytes for WHIR at rate 1/8 and step rank 13).

For WHIR the recursion is about a wash on the prover at rate 1/8: the verifier's hashes per input match ARC's openings through the tree's levels (2,600 against 98 $\times$ 26 without grinding, and both fall with it). Its merge is two fifths larger. At rate 1/2 it saves a third of the prover and a twentieth of the merge, because the verifier's queries fall with the rate while ARC's openings grow with it. So with Poseidon trees the rate-1/2 recursive rows of WHIR over either field and of the limb hybrid dominate their ARC rows on every measure. Under BLAKE3 trees no ARC row is dominated by its family's recursive row, nor are the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's or BitZ's under either hash. FRI's verifier has twice WHIR's hashes, so its recursion costs about twice ARC.

Ligerito's verifier, under BitZ at the 128-bit floor, opens 826 columns over its 6 levels in the unique-decoding regime, against ARC's 86 openings at rate 1/8. So its recursion is 9 times ARC's hashing (506 against 56 million witness bits at rate 1/8) and 8.5 times the prover, for a proof 1 percent smaller. The ARC row is the better one for BitZ on the prover and the merge. At the default floor the recursion's merge is larger than any size measured, so it has no row there.

The $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's recursion verifies its own FRI-Binius proof, whose ring-switching verifier costs $2\ell'$ field multiplications times the packing degree, for $\ell'$ packed variables. The packing degree is 64 in the 192‑bit design, 191 in the 191‑bit one, which switches in one step, and 256 in the 256‑bit one. So its merge is 172, 224, and 315 million witness bits in the three, against 67 million under ARC. The prover is 1.9 to 3.5 times the ARC row's, with the verifier's paths pruned as a multiproof. Its proof is a quarter smaller (176 kbytes against 240 kbytes at rate 1/8).

What the axes mean

Proof bytes

The compressed PCD proof. For Pedersen-IPA it is counted from Ragu's structs: the instance's commitments and scalars, two revdot reductions, two batches, and two IPAs with one round per bit of the step rank; the checked-in configuration gives 7,908 bytes, which Ragu's wire format confirms (7,936 with its 28‑byte envelope). The two online polynomial commitments and six openings that every Ragu proof now carries are 448 of those bytes.

For Akita it is the planner's byte estimate for the decider's opening of one final group plus the separately committed groups, from the estimation runs, plus an instance of 128-byte commitments, two claims per group, and one claim per step circuit.

For WHIR with ARC it is the WHIR proof that decides the final accumulator. It is measured with the reference implementation over a 256‑bit field at 128‑bit security in the proven Johnson regime, at the batched witness size and the selected rate and grinding. Added to it are the two extra first-round trees of the decider's batched opening and the accumulator's roots and claims.

For the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family it is the optimizer's proof as the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family section describes, and for BitZ its Table 10 proof with the Zinc+ PIOP's, each with the extra trees under ARC.

For the binary-groups family it is Akita's measured opening of a binary source, extrapolated to the merge's bits.

Accumulation prover

The prover commitment work below plus the fold's own sum-checks, and for Kishu the merge's own constraint PIOP. Akita's two norm and range sum-checks run over the accumulator in digits, at about five multiplications in the claim field per digit a pass (Pasta's field for the Pasta-claims variant, the 64‑bit field for Kishu). The accumulator is the batch group and the five stage groups. For the Akita variants the batch is every step circuit's witness in one committed vector of circuits × $2^{\text{step rank}}$ coefficients, since the merge absorbs one image for it and the decider opens it at that length. For Kishu it is the step's one witness. A stage polynomial holds its values at the wires of its reserved gates and is zero elsewhere. Those zeros are structural and public, so what is committed and folded is the stage's support: 584 values in Ragu's native fuse at a header of 9. A lattice fuse's stages are taken to hold the same, a claim-field element each, which in Kishu is 26,000 committed digits. Akita's grouped opening admits groups of different dimensions [DBK+26, §5]. Kishu adds its range check: the measured Booleanity sum-check for its bits (438 ms for $2^{27}$ bits on one core, 9.8 word multiplications a digit), and for its tribits three products more at the same rate, in the merge's constraint PIOP. The fold's own linear combination of the digit vectors costs one multiplication per digit. For Kishu the accumulator is the step, so this measure grows with the step's length: 97 million word multiplications at the shortest step, $1.625 \times 2^{20}$ digits, and 1,450 million at the longest, $1.875 \times 2^{25}$. That is with the sum-checks over the whole step. The transaction measure charges a merge's sum-checks over the digits in use only, since a prover can skip the zeros it knows of. It is the measure the transaction prover charges per merge.

The other rows add nothing: Pedersen-IPA's fuse is measured whole and its native folding, the batching of about 95 polynomials and a 100-point linear combination, is about one percent of it; the hash-based rows' ARC step already hashes the new accumulator; and the binary-groups row's measured opening includes its sum-checks.

Accumulator memory

What the prover holds between fuses, in bytes. A fuse holds the step's witness and one or two of them: two when both children are real proofs, one when the other child is the bootstrap proof. That is the fixed base case that Ragu's circuits recognise by its dummy suffix, as in a chain or in Tachyon's action steps.

For the lattice rows it is the folded digit vector, the accumulator's coefficients in digits (the batch group's circuits × $2^{\text{step rank}}$ and the stage groups' values, as the accumulation prover counts them), each digit stored at the width of its accumulator's norm bound. The certified $\ell_2$ bound at the handoff is about $2^{19}$ (the ledger of the zero-knowledge note, the zk note below), which bounds every entry, so 20 bits and a sign. Each fold multiplies the entries by up to 40, so an accumulator a level below the top needs five bits fewer. The measure is of the top level. The sum-checks expand a digit to an element of the commitment field on the fly, 8 bytes at 64 bits, 16 at 128, 32 at Pasta's modulus, which is computation, not storage.

Pedersen-IPA's is the uncompressed proof, every committed polynomial's $2^{\text{step rank}}$ Pasta coefficients, 26.5 Mbytes measured at step rank 13 against 24.9 Mbytes counted.

The hash-based rows under ARC hold the accumulator's codeword, the witness over the rate in the field's elements, and its Merkle tree; under their own recursion only the previous proof. BitZ and the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family hold the packed codeword with a tree of 256‑byte leaves, and the binary-groups row the packed bits.

At step rank 17 Akita with Pasta claims holds about 29.4 Mbytes, Kishu at step rank 21 about 5.6 Mbytes, and Pedersen-IPA at step rank 13 25 Mbytes.

Peak fuse memory

What a fuse holds at its peak, in bytes. For the lattice rows it is three things. First, the two children's accumulators, with the output written over one of them as the linear combination is computed. Second, the fold's second-round sum-check table. The first round can stream over the packed digits, expanding each to a claim-field element as it goes, so the first table stored is the second round's: half the accumulator's digits at the claim field's width, 16 bytes for Kishu's extension, the 128‑bit field, and Goldilocks's extension, 32 at Pasta's modulus. Third, under zero knowledge, the mask: the accumulator's digits at the row's own coordinate width, 26 to 32 bits across the lattice rows, a masked coordinate stored compactly, with the response written over it. The new step's witness and the commitment's working set are small beside these. The other rows hold two accumulators, the output over one of them.

Kishu at step rank 21 peaks at 35 Mbytes with zero knowledge and 28 Mbytes without; Akita with Pasta claims at step rank 17 at 238 Mbytes; and Pedersen-IPA at step rank 13 at 50 Mbytes, two uncompressed proofs.

Prover commitment work

Native 64‑bit word multiplications for one step's commitments, with every committed polynomial taken as dense. Pedersen over Pasta: a Pippenger multi-scalar multiplication costs about 255/rank mixed additions per coefficient, so the cost falls as 1/rank. The constant is calibrated to Ragu's measured fuse at the checked-in configuration: 1.46 s on one core of an Apple M4 Max for the step's circuit and its 91 recursion circuits with their stage polynomials, 96 commitments of $2^{13}$ coefficients. That is about 5,600 word multiplications per coefficient at step rank 13 and 5,200 at step rank 14, over all of the step's polynomials. The earlier estimate, 11 field multiplications of 32 word multiplications per addition, was 24 percent higher; udon's AArch64 assembly, which Ragu does not enable, takes 13 percent off the measured time.

Ajtai by NTT, with the accumulation matrix shaped as Akita's catalog shapes its own, a module of a few rows over a ring of degree 512 or 1,024: per digit the module rank in pointwise ring products plus $\log_2(d)/2$ for its share of the transform, at the modulus width, which is 32 word multiplications at 255 bits, 8 at 128, 2 at 64. The module rank and degree are the cheapest that Akita's ℓ∞ estimator certifies at the floor for the row's bound and its accumulator's width (the security floor section). Without the mask every lattice row is at module rank 1: 5.5 multiplications a digit at degree 512, or 6.0 at 1,024, which the widest accumulators at the 64‑bit modulus need. With 85 digits per Pasta coefficient, at a vector of $2^{22}$ digits, that is about 15,000 per coefficient when the commitment modulus is Pasta, 940 when it is 64‑bit, 1,900 at a 128‑bit field, and 180 on Goldilocks, over every step circuit's witness. The cycle's endoscaling and binder polynomials do not exist in the lattice design. Folding and sum-check work is not included.

For WHIR with ARC it is the Reed–Solomon encoding by FFT over the witness divided by the rate, plus the Merkle trees of the witness and of the new accumulator. The trees are hashed by BLAKE3 at 200 word multiplications per compression by default, or under the arithmetic-hash option by Poseidon at about 35,000 per permutation over a 255‑bit field, since natively its matrix multiplications are full multiplications too. At rate 1/8 that is about 180,000 per coefficient with Poseidon and a few thousand with BLAKE3; but the circuits that a BLAKE3 merge needs multiply the committed witness, which is where the prover's cost then goes.

The $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's prover is counted as the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family section describes, a carry-less multiplication as one word multiplication, and BitZ's is its measured Table 10 prover. The binary-groups family's is the measured commit and open times per committed bit, taken for one thread, plus Flock's PIOP.

Accumulation atoms

An atom is one element of the merge circuit's native field. A configuration's accumulation atoms are the atoms that the merge circuit absorbs per fold, to derive the fold's challenges from the two proofs that it merges. The count stands in for the merge circuit's size: each absorbed atom costs hashing constraints, and the Pedersen coordinates also feed the point arithmetic. For Pedersen-IPA it is two atoms per commitment folded, the x and y coordinates, over the commitments of both instances. For Akita it is the sum-check messages of the range and norm checks, about 120 elements for two sum-checks of about 20 rounds with three elements each, counted as one atom each. Then each compressed commitment image: 128 bytes at the variant's atom width, which is 8 atoms at a 128‑bit field, 16 on Goldilocks, 4 on Pasta, and 6 for Pasta claims with 64‑bit commitment coordinates, three coordinates to an atom. Then one atom per step circuit's claim, and 18 more with zero knowledge. The folding arithmetic is not included, and for Pedersen-IPA neither is the endoscaling arithmetic, which is what a higher endoscaling count costs in gates.

For the hash-based families, with ARC or with their own recursion, the merge absorbs only the accumulators' or input proofs' roots and claims, about 40 atoms, which is why atoms are the wrong measure for them.

Merge gates

The gates synthesized across every circuit of one merge: measured for Pedersen-IPA, and estimated for Akita and for WHIR with ARC as the Circuit budget section describes. For the binary-field families, the binary-groups family and Kishu they are counted in AND gates, witness bits or digits, as their sections describe. It is the default third measure.

In the 2D view the marker's size follows the logarithm of the third measure over the plotted points, from 5 to 22 pixels, so the sizes read across any selection. The binary-field and BitZ families' witness-bit counts sit at the top of that range. Markers over 16 pixels carry a small contrasting cross at their centre, so that they can be read against the axes. The swatches in the families list are sized like each family's median plotted marker, up to 18 pixels, so the list carries the same cue.

Decider work

The decider's verification, in the same word-multiplication equivalents as the prover, at 3e9 per second of one core, which is also what the millisecond option assumes. Pedersen-IPA is modelled: the two IPAs each need a multi-scalar multiplication of $2^{\text{step rank}}$ points, plus a scalar multiplication per folded commitment to combine the instance, about 41.9 ms at step rank 13.

Akita is measured: the paper's single-thread verifier takes 4.2 ms at the 128‑bit field and 3.9 ms on Goldilocks at $2^{27}$ committed bits, growing by about 0.4 ms per doubling, so 2.59 to 3.08 ms at this witness size. The Pasta variants are taken at four times the 128‑bit figure, the ratio of word multiplications per field multiplication, since the verifier's work is sum-check arithmetic in the claim field: about 11.9 ms. With zero knowledge the final accumulator is at the ordinary length and the terminal's bounds are unchanged, so the verification is unchanged but for the few percent that one more step circuit's claim can cost.

The hash-based deciders are the WHIR verifier's hashes at the witness size, measured, at the variant's native permutation cost, plus about a million for the arithmetic, and in the ARC rows the decider's two extra first-round trees. At rate 1/8 and step rank 13 that is 13.7 ms on Goldilocks and 65.6 ms with Poseidon over Pasta under ARC, and 7.68 and 34.5 ms under recursion, and about a millisecond in every case with BLAKE3 trees; FRI has 2.07 times the hashes.

The $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's decider is the FRI-Binius verifier: the proof's authentication hashes over pruned paths and its leaf hashes at BLAKE3's native cost plus its field multiplications, 0.404 to 1.64 ms.

BitZ is its measured verifier at the merge's bits, 3.16 ms at $2^{25}$ bits and 128 bits of security, scaled with the floor and, in the ARC rows, by the decider's two extra first-round trees, plus 2.00 ms for Zinc+'s PIOP.

The binary-groups family's decider is Akita's measured verifier for a binary source, at the paper's slope, 5.40 ms at the merge's bits.

Circuit budget

A step circuit at step rank $r$ holds $2^{r-2}$ gates and four times as many constraints, 2,048 and 8,192 at step rank 13, and the gate limit binds first. The Pedersen-IPA figures are the internal circuits' synthesis counts pinned in Ragu's tests at step rank 13 (tachyon-zcash/ragu 8109a41c). A binder costs 2,032 to 2,048 gates for 2 of the 8 challenges, taken as 590 per challenge plus 860 of overhead; that split is derived, not measured.

Lattice-based families

Akita

Akita [DBK+26] is the lattice polynomial commitment scheme that these rows price. Its merge circuit is estimated, not measured. Its transcript hashing is priced at the selected hash: BLAKE3 at 14,900 gates per 64 bytes absorbed, the sum-check elements and the claims at the claim field's width and the six commitment images at 128 bytes each, or Poseidon absorbing four elements per 288-gate permutation, 72 gates per accumulation atom. Folding each child's 128-byte commitment image needs a modular reduction, taken as one gate per bit. The sum-check verifier's arithmetic is about 400 gates.

The step circuits are that total divided by the step rank's budget, rounded up; each circuit's own evaluation claim is absorbed by the next merge, so the count is a fixed point. Under BLAKE3 a 16-byte claim costs about 3,700 gates to absorb and a 32-byte one 7,500, more than a circuit of step rank 13 holds, and the 32-byte one more than a circuit of step rank 14. So step rank 13 has no Akita rows under the default, nor step rank 14 for the Pasta variants. Splitting a compression across circuits is taken as free, although a step rank below 16 cannot hold one in a single circuit. Merge gates, in the hover text and the table, is the total over every circuit of one merge.

Kishu: BitZ over Akita (draft)

Kishu is the study's name for an arithmetization in BitZ's style over Akita's PCS. Every wire is committed as small digits under Akita's 64‑bit modulus, bits where the circuit needs bits and tribits for integer words; the constraints and the range check are sum-checks over that field's quadratic extension; and Pasta values enter as limbs or through a fingerprint rather than as claims in the Pasta field. Kishu is a Japanese dog breed, like Akita and Hachi. The arithmetization, its unit costs, and how it differs from Ragu's are on Kishu's Arithmetization. That page also gives the Booleanity check's two forms, the two routes for a Pasta multiplication, the merge's accounting under BLAKE3 and Poseidon, and the recovery circuit's count. The rows' figures are here.

Costs of a step

In a step of $2^{21}$ digits with BLAKE3 the merge is 1.2 million digits. The proof is 74 kbytes, the merge's commitments are 3.0e7 word multiplications, and the decider takes 2.48 ms in the sum-check form. With Poseidon the merge is 0.72 million digits, the proof 74 kbytes, and the commitments 1.8e7. The shortest step with a row is $1.5 \times 2^{20}$ digits under BLAKE3 and $1.75 \times 2^{19}$ under Poseidon: a step has to hold its own merge.

Against the 64‑bit Akita variant, which commits every wire of a hash gate as a 255‑bit value, the merge prover is 190 times smaller under BLAKE3 (5.9e9 at step rank 17) and 6.8 times under Poseidon. Under BLAKE3 the proof is smaller too (74 kbytes against 95 kbytes). So under conventional hashes Kishu dominates that variant on every measure but the merge's count, whose units differ; under Poseidon Kishu's proof is 10 kbytes smaller. Against BitZ at step rank 17 the proof is 7.3 times smaller and the merge prover does 38 to 64 times less work.

Quang Dao thinks the prover here is likely overestimated: the commitment is priced at rows sized by the loose security analysis, with its grinding, and a tighter analysis would cut them. So the family's prover should be read as an upper bound. The plot's measures are the recursion's: the step's commitments, the accumulator's opening and the merge circuit, which every design pays and which can be estimated. Which application circuit to benchmark is hard to agree on, since it depends on the application; so the application enters the plot through the step rank, and the transaction measure through its cost in digits.

Zero knowledge and status

The projection form of the Booleanity check reveals coordinates of the committed table, so no row that uses it has zero knowledge. The zero-knowledge rows use the sum-check form, masked as the zero-knowledge section describes.

The draft's certificates for the Booleanity check are conditional and not yet formalized.

LaBinius

LaBinius [OS26] is a lattice commitment whose modulus is decoupled from the constraint field: the commitment runs over a composite, NTT-friendly modulus, the evaluation claim lives in a binary extension field, and extraction avoids reduction modulo the commitment modulus by working over the number field. Its native opening is the amortised one, a square root of the witness in size, 314 kbytes at $2^{18}$ elements and 3.0 Mbytes at $2^{24}$; LaBRADOR compresses it to 75 to 88 kbytes at ten times the prover, with a verifier of 77 to 713 ms, all at 100 bits. For this study its importance is that it is a worked, implemented instance of the decoupled commitment modulus that the Pasta-claims-with-64‑bit-commitments variant assumes for Akita, with a soundness argument to adapt. It gives a decider only, with no accumulation or recursion layer, and Akita's own development has since replaced its root reduction with binary groups, which the next section plots.

Flock-style circuits over Akita's binary groups (experimental)

Akita's development branch commits a dense binary source as a group kind inside one ordinary grouped opening (LayerZero-Labs/akita#285, the binary feature; it replaces the LaBinius root reduction and nested commitment of its earlier PR #229). The bits are packed into 128‑bit carriers, the claim is a multilinear evaluation over a binary field, and the opening switches to the proof field inside. So the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's arithmetization, Boolean circuits with Flock's PIOP over $\mathbb{F}_{2^{128}}$, can commit with Akita instead of FRI-Binius and accumulate with Akita's fold instead of ARC.

The row prices that from the branch's own measurements of November 2026, at the P128 root; the P64 root is one to four percent smaller, with times within run-to-run noise. A binary-claim opening of $2^{22}$ carriers, $2^{29}$ bits, is 82,764 bytes. It is committed in 174 ms, opened in 648 ms and verified in 8.5 ms, on an M4 Max under load with the parallel feature on.

The merge is Akita's fold in a Boolean circuit, and that is our estimate. The transcript's 2.7 kbytes absorbed by BLAKE3 in blocks of 16,384 committed bits per compression, Flock's layout, native in a Boolean circuit under either hash policy, is 0.70 million gates; the fold of the twelve child images by a ternary challenge of weight 20, one adder bit per image bit per unit of weight, 0.27 million; one 128‑bit proof-field multiplication per sum-check element, 120 of them, 2.1 million, which dominates; and the two inputs' Flock PIOP verifiers, which the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's merge counts too, 1.5 million, for 4.5 million in all.

A multiplication is counted in AND gates with XOR free, the garbled-circuit cost model, by the cheapest of schoolbook, Karatsuba and Toom–Cook 3 at each width. A full adder is one AND, so schoolbook is about $2n^2$ at $n$ bits; Karatsuba pays from about 20 bits and halves the count at 128; and Toom–Cook 3, with its exact division by three done bit-serially, takes a further two percent off. That is 16,400 AND gates at 128 bits, plus 800 for the reduction by $2^{128} - 275$. That is 4.5 million AND gates in all, 15 times fewer than the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's ARC merge at rate 1/8, and not comparable with Pasta gates.

The proof is the measured opening extrapolated from $2^{29}$ bits down to the merge's $2^{21.3}$ at the planner's 563 bytes per doubling for the 128‑bit field, with the stage groups at that field's growth: 97 kbytes. The measured pair of sizes a doubling apart differ by 1.8 kbytes, which would make it 10 kbytes smaller, but one difference between single runs is not a slope.

The prover takes the measured commit time as the step's commitment, at ten times the measured time for one thread, since BitZ's benchmark gave four to five times on ten threads of this machine: 9.7 word multiplications per committed bit, plus Flock's PIOP at 2.3. The accumulation prover adds the fold's sum-checks over the accumulator, the merge's bits and the stage groups' values as bits, at the binary field's multiplication cost, as the lattice rows count them. The measured open time is the opening's, which the accumulation prover does not count for the lattice rows either. That is 4.9e7 for the merge's commitments, against 1.0e9 for the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's ARC step at rate 1/8, and against 6.6e8 for Akita over Goldilocks at step rank 16 under BLAKE3. There the transcript's hashing in a Pasta circuit is what makes 51 circuits. So with conventional hashes a Boolean merge circuit over the lattice commitment is the cheapest merge prover on the page, since its hashing is native and its bits are cheap to commit. The row is on the post‑quantum frontier under the default.

The decider is the measured verifier at the paper's slope, 5.40 ms, where the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's is 0.404 ms. In the transaction measure a Pasta gate of the recovery circuit is about 1,650 AND gates here, since a Pallas multiplication in a Boolean circuit is about 120,000 of them. So the recovery circuit costs about 18,000 word multiplications per Pasta gate in this family, against 3,700 for Akita with Pasta claims over 64‑bit commitments. The family would suit an application that is a Boolean circuit in its own right, such as the batches of hashes that Flock targets; the recovery circuit, which is Pasta arithmetic, is not one.

Not modelled: zero knowledge, since the groups are not hiding; the 147‑bit floor; and the binary carrier field switch. The security ledger is open: the q24 Module-SIS certificate is not yet issued and the branch makes no security claim.

Hash-based families

These families rest on a random oracle alone: WHIR over Pasta, over Goldilocks and with the Pasta witness in 64‑bit limbs, a Plonky3-style FRI over Goldilocks, the Flock/Binius-style $\mathbb{F}_2$ family, and BitZ, each with ARC accumulation and with its own recursion. What the hash-based route has that Akita does not is native Pasta arithmetic with no design change beyond the commitment scheme, and a prover whose hashing is cheap with a conventional hash. The default view prices that, and the Hashes section says what the recursion then costs.

The figures in this section are with Poseidon trees, the arithmetic-hash option; the Hashes section gives the BLAKE3 ones. WHIR with ARC accumulation is dominated by Akita on every measure, while the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family and BitZ are the efficient end of the line; the subsections say how far.

WHIR and FRI

WHIR with ARC accumulation is dominated by Akita on every measure: with the grinding chosen per row its proofs are 202 kbytes to 1,530 kbytes, its prover is 32 to 2,300 times Pedersen-IPA's, and its merge is 0.68 to 6.4 million gates, 27 to 250 times Akita's.

The proof is the decider's. At the root nobody has yet combined the two child accumulators with the root's own witness, so the decider's proof opens those three oracles in its first round, each under its own Merkle root (Ulrich Haböck's point). A stand-alone WHIR proof of the same witness is 177 kbytes at rate 1/8 and step rank 13. The two extra first rounds bring it to 376 kbytes, with their paths pruned as a multiproof, as the reference implementation already prunes its own.

The lower rates and the grinding, added after Ulrich Haböck's questions, take a third off its proof at rate 1/32 against rate 1/8 (261 kbytes against 376 kbytes at step rank 13) and a third off its merge, at 2.5 times the encoding and hashing. The sizes are extrapolated from measurements at $2^{17}$, $2^{18}$ and $2^{20}$. The growth per doubling is not uniform, since a folding round is added every two doublings. The three-doubling slope used here is about nine kbytes against five over the first doubling alone.

Goldilocks cuts its prover by about 5 times and its proof by a sixth, and grows its merge, since a Goldilocks Poseidon costs more gates per node. The limb hybrid is WHIR with the Pasta witness committed as four 64‑bit limbs per coefficient, tested over Goldilocks, with a Pasta merge circuit: the family list's "Pasta claims, 64‑bit limbs" rows. Unlike Akita's 64‑bit commitment variant it gains nothing: the Pasta merge circuit forces Pasta's hash on the trees, and four limbs per coefficient is more hashing, not less. WHIR over Pasta is dominated by Akita's Pasta variants, which is a like-for-like comparison.

The merge is estimated, as Akita's is. A hash commitment is not homomorphic, so Ragu's split accumulation does not apply; ARC accumulates Reed–Solomon proximity claims instead. Its verifier checks $2(\lambda - g)/\log_2(1/\rho)$ Merkle openings per input at rate $\rho$ with $g$ bits of grinding in the query phase, in the proven, Johnson-bound regime. At $\lambda = 128$ and 16 bits of grinding that is 224, 112, 75, 56, 45 and 38 at rates 1/2 to 1/64, and 256, 128 and 85 at 1/2, 1/4 and 1/8 without grinding. The factor of two is the Johnson bound's. The conjectures that would remove it all the way to capacity were refuted in late 2025 [CS25].

Between the Johnson bound and capacity the question is open and moving, and the answer depends on the code and the field. Over binary fields, where BitZ and the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family work, there are counterexamples to proximity gaps beyond the bound [DKTZ26]. For Reed–Solomon codes over fields of sufficiently large characteristic, on any evaluation domain, [DKT26] proves list-decoding and correlated-agreement bounds beyond the Johnson bound and up to capacity, with the bounds and its concrete parameters formally verified in Lean.

Every row's query counts are for a proximity parameter within the Johnson bound, for now. That includes the rows derived from BEEP [BG26]. BEEP's analysis needs only list decodability, which every code has up to the Johnson bound. It would allow a larger proximity parameter, and so fewer queries, wherever the code is list-decodable beyond that bound. The rows do not take one: what BEEP changes in them is the folding error and the field.

The result for large characteristic would serve the prime-field WHIR rows, and its gain there is modest at first. Its first step past the Johnson bound is an explicit agreement threshold below $\sqrt{\rho}$, where the list has size $O_\rho(n/\eta^2)$ and the correlated-agreement error is $O_\rho(n^2/(q\eta^4))$, for a code of length $n$ over a field of $q$ elements at a gap $\eta$ above the threshold. So the list is long, and its size enters BEEP's errors at a cost in grinding or field size. The paper reports proofs 4.6 to 11 percent smaller in three deployed systems at unchanged security, which is the order of gain to expect here, not the halving of the openings that capacity would give. This page does not use the result yet.

Each opening is a path through the accumulator's tree over the witness divided by the rate, one hash per node, a BLAKE3 compression at 14,900 gates by default or a Poseidon permutation at 288 under the option. A merge has two inputs. The step circuits follow as for Akita, with the tree's depth growing with the batched witness, which is why the number of circuits is in the hundreds. WARP has the same opening count for Reed–Solomon codes and a linear-time prover only with linear-time codes, which does not help here, since the cost is the hashing rather than the encoding.

Verifying a WHIR proof in the circuit instead of accumulating, the other route, costs about the same hashing for a single oracle and more with the oracles of a real polynomial IOP. The proof of each step's growing witness is fed back into the next step.

Over Goldilocks the claims are in the cubic extension, since the correlated-agreement bound leaves the quadratic one at about 87 bits. BEEP [BG26], which the BitZ section describes, lifts that: with affine folding challenges its folding error is $2/|\mathbb{F}|$, 127 bits in the quadratic extension. The rows marked "quadratic extension, Johnson bound with BEEP (derived)" are the reference implementation measured there at a 128‑bit target, with its proximity-gap term taken as BEEP's and its other terms covered by 7 to 21 bits of proof of work, plus BEEP's samples at each fold. They are rows of the Goldilocks variant, the limb hybrid, and Plonky3. The query counts are the cubic extension's, so the merge and its prover are the same, and what shrinks is every element after the first round: the proof by 7.6 to 11 percent, 294 kbytes against 321 kbytes at step rank 13 and rate 1/8, and the accumulator by 29 percent. As for BitZ, no implementation of BEEP exists, so the rows are at the 128‑bit floor alone and are ranked with the strongest assumption. Over Pasta's 255‑bit field the bound was never the constraint, and nothing changes.

The $\mathbb{F}_2$ family

The binary-field, sum-check-based systems of 2025 and 2026, Flock [BRW26] and Binius64 on the FRI-Binius and Ligerito commitments, and Blaze on repeat-accumulate-accumulate codes, are the efficient end of the hash-based line. They are Boolean arithmetizations over $\mathbb{F}_2$ with claims in $\mathbb{F}_{2^{128}}$, so they have no $2^{\text{step rank}}$ Pasta circuits and the step-rank filter does not apply to them; the Flock/Binius-style family is priced in its own terms. Its merge is ARC's openings as for the other hash-based variants, each Merkle node a BLAKE3 compression in a block of $2^{14}$ committed bits, Flock's layout for a batch: a compression is 10,416 AND gates, the carries of its additions, and the block also holds its inputs, its outputs, and the words committed to keep the constraint matrices sparse. So the merge is a Boolean witness of that many bits, 37 to 188 million from rate 1/64 to 1/2. Each input's PIOP verifier runs in the merge too, the zerocheck and the lincheck over the witness's variables and a sum-check per base matrix in place of the native verifier's own evaluation of the matrices: about 380.0 multiplications in the challenge field, 1.6 million AND gates by Karatsuba at rate 1/8, 2.5 percent of the merge. Its merge-gates figure is a count of AND gates that is not comparable with Pasta gates; bytes and prover commitment work are comparable.

Proof bytes come from Ulrich Haböck's FRI-Binius proof-size optimizer, in three designs that each row names: the witness packed into $\mathbb{F}_{2^{256}}$, which is also the field of the folding and sum-check challenges; packed into $\mathbb{F}_{2^{64}}$ with the challenges drawn from its cubic extension $\mathbb{F}_{2^{192}}$, which is the field that the agreement bound sees, as Plonky3's FRI pairs a base field with an extension; or packed straight into $\mathbb{F}_{2^{191}}$, a prime-degree field with the trinomial $x^{191} + x^9 + 1$ and no subfields, which Ulrich Haböck suggested for its arithmetic. The proof is at the selected security floor in the list-decoding regime up to the Johnson bound, with the largest list that the correlated-agreement bound allows (the simplified BCHKS25 bound; BCIKS20 for unique decoding). The codeword's length is counted in field elements, with the Guruswami–Sudan multiplicity, the selected grinding, the folding schedule chosen to minimise the compressed proof, and Merkle-cap multiproofs. Checked against the reference WHIR implementation at the same witness, field, regime and grinding, its query counts are within one to five of WHIR's and its proofs about a tenth smaller, from the optimised schedule. Under that bound a 128‑bit field cannot reach 128 bits in any regime at these sizes, since the agreement term alone is about 100 bits, which is why FRI-Binius's published figures are at 100.

BEEP [BG26] replaces the correlated-agreement bound, as the BitZ section describes. With affine folding challenges its folding error is $2/|\mathbb{F}|$ at any list size, so FRI-Binius's own $\mathbb{F}_{2^{128}}$ serves at the 128‑bit floor, with no second field: the witness is packed into the challenge field itself and ring-switched as the paper has it, where the 192‑bit design restates the ring switching over two fields. The rows marked "128‑bit field, Johnson bound with BEEP (derived)" are the optimizer's proof in that field, with a list of $2^{8}$ and BEEP's 5 samples before each fold. Against the 192‑bit design at the same floor they are 7 to 9 percent smaller, 182 kbytes against 198 kbytes at rate 1/8 and 111 kbytes against 120 kbytes at 1/64, and their merge prover is 6.7 to 23 percent cheaper. The gain is smaller than BitZ's because these rows were already at the Johnson bound; what BEEP buys here is the smaller field and simpler design. As for BitZ, no implementation exists, so the rows are at the 128‑bit floor alone and are ranked with the strongest assumption.

Ring switching [DP24, §1.3] packs $2^{\kappa}$ bits into each element and assumes a power-of-two degree over $\mathbb{F}_2$, which 192 and 191 are not. So the 192‑bit design packs into $\mathbb{F}_{2^{64}}$ ($\kappa = 6$) and needs the ring-switching sum-check restated over the tensor product of the two fields. The 191‑bit design lays the witness out as 191 columns, which the PIOP batches and the ring switching takes in one step of degree 191. Both are design changes that the paper does not write down. The first-round oracle is the packed witness and has the same bytes under any packing. So the designs differ in the later rounds' elements, 24 bytes against 32, and in the agreement margin, which sets the list and with it the queries. At the rate‑1/8 merge the list's size has 17 bits at 256 bits, 5 at 192, and 4 at 191, and the proof has 88, 92, and 95 queries. So the 192‑ and 191‑bit proofs are 4 and 2 percent smaller than the 256‑bit one (240 kbytes and 247 kbytes against 251 kbytes at rate 1/8).

Under ARC the decider's first round opens three oracles, the two child accumulators and the root's witness, each under its own root, which the optimizer counts and schedules for.

Prover commitment work is counted in word multiplications, a 64‑bit carry-less multiplication as one. The PIOP is Flock's measured 82,100 BLAKE3 compressions per second on one core less its commitment (its Table 5: witness generation 5.0%, zero-check and lincheck 46%). The zero-check and lincheck, which run over $\mathbb{F}_{2^{128}}$, are scaled by half the challenge field's multiplication cost over $\mathbb{F}_{2^{128}}$'s: 4, 8, and 11 carry-less multiplications per product in 128, 192, and 256 bits, Karatsuba over the 64‑bit limbs with the limb reductions, and 7 in $\mathbb{F}_{2^{191}}$, whose one trinomial reduction is cheaper than the tower's six.

The commitment is counted operation by operation. The additive NTT of the first oracle, with a 32‑bit subfield twiddle per limb in the tower fields and a full-field twiddle in $\mathbb{F}_{2^{191}}$, which has no subfield to hold the evaluation domain, and its tree; the ring switching, two passes of a challenge-field element by each witness bit and a sum-check over the packed coefficients; and the folds, the folded trees and the BaseFold sum-check. These are counted in carry-less multiplications, BLAKE3 compressions and word XORs at an eighth each, and scaled to the FRI-Binius paper's single-threaded 144 ms commitment and 160 ms opening at $2^{28}$ bits, rate 1/2, over $\mathbb{F}_{2^{128}}$ (its Table 2). The counts reproduce those at 1.1 and 1.9 times. An ARC step pays the NTT, two trees and the ring switching; a recursive step a full proof, and its merge verifies two, the field multiplications by Karatsuba.

So the prover grows with the inverse rate below 1/4, as the other hash-based rows do. With the grinding chosen per row it is 924 million word multiplications at its least, at rate 1/4, and 3.7 billion at rate 1/64 in the 192‑bit design. The 256‑bit one is up to 7 percent more, and the 191‑bit one from 3 percent less at rate 1/2 to 10 percent more at the lowest rates, where the NTT's full-field twiddles outweigh its cheaper reduction and half-size folds. Flock's whole measured pipeline is 148 million at the rate‑1/8 merge's size and its own rate 1/2 over $\mathbb{F}_{2^{128}}$.

The decider's verifier is the proof's authentication hashes, with the paths pruned as a multiproof, and its leaf hashes at BLAKE3's native cost plus its field multiplications, 0.404 to 1.64 ms. Zero knowledge is modelled in two variants, a batched mask and a Zook-style one, which the zero-knowledge section below describes. It does not support the Pasta base field, so under the dominance rule it can be dominated by anything.

Its proofs under ARC are 148 kbytes to 620 kbytes from rate 1/64 to 1/2 in the 192‑bit design, with the three first-round trees, and under recursion 104 kbytes to 234 kbytes. The prover is least at rate 1/4, with rate 1/8 about 12% higher: 924 million word multiplications, 40 times Akita over Goldilocks's with Poseidon and 1.4 times it with BLAKE3, whose proof is 73 kbytes to 77 kbytes. The merge is 37 to 188 million AND gates against Akita's 25 thousand Pasta gates with Poseidon, or 0.83 million with BLAKE3.

The price of the field is the application: Pasta arithmetic in a Boolean circuit, or in any small field, pays the embedding and simulation overhead that the small-field survey [Ska26, §5] quantifies, and the Ironwood recovery circuit is Pasta arithmetic. The transaction measure prices that at about 1,650 AND gates per Pasta gate.

BitZ

BitZ [BGK+26] removes that price. It is a hash-based commitment for multilinears over any finitely generated ring. The witness is bit-decomposed and committed packed over $\mathbb{F}_{2^{128}}$ with Flock's Ligerito. An evaluation claim over a ring R is lifted to the integers, through a random prime where R is large, and proved in the exponent of $\mathbb{F}_{2^{128}}$ by batched GKR grand products. So its cost is per committed bit and does not depend on R.

The application is proved by the paper's hybrid SNARK: a PIOP over the integers for arithmetic, and a binary-field PIOP for bit logic, over one commitment to every witness bit. So the Ironwood recovery circuit stays Pasta arithmetic. A field multiplication is an integer relation, 255 bits of value and as many of quotient, 510 committed bits. Bit logic is counted as a Boolean circuit's AND gates, one committed bit each, since XOR is linear over the binary field and costs nothing. So the recovery circuit is about 8.2 million bits, as the Transaction prover section counts. A step of step rank $r$ commits $2^r$ bits of application witness, beside the Merkle hashing of the merge, which is most of its witness. The merge is a hybrid circuit: the Merkle hashing over $\mathbb{F}_2$ as for the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family, and the GKR and PIOP verifiers native over the binary extension and the projection field, about 1,900.0 products of committed elements, 484 thousand bits, 0.65 percent of the merge.

Its costs are measured with the paper's own benchmark on one thread of this machine, at $2^{22}$ to $2^{29}$ committed bits, and interpolated at the merge's bits. A row whose merge falls outside those sizes is left out. One thread needs care. The benchmark's thread option sizes only its main pool, and the library's Merkle trees, encoder, and two other phases hop onto a second pool of every core. The measurements set RAYON_NUM_THREADS=1, which closes that pool; without it a commitment reads about 1.6 times too fast.

The prover is the commitment and the opening. At $2^{25}$ bits and rate 1/2 the opening's grand-product GKR is 97 of the prover's 122 ms, 80 percent, and it does not depend on the rate. As Quang Dao puts it, the prover with grand-product GKR is just really expensive. The commitment about doubles with each halving of the rate: 8.10 ms at rate 1/2, 31.7 at 1/8, and 248 at 1/64. The hybrid SNARK's two PIOPs come on top. The paper's Table 14 measures the whole prover on witnesses split evenly between SHA-256 and multiplications modulo $2^{32}$, and its Table 10 the PCS alone at the same size. The ratio is 1.2 to 1.5 from $2^{23}$ to $2^{29}$ bits, so the model adds 0.36 of the PCS prover on every committed bit, the merge's as well as the application's, with 2.00 ms of verifier and 36 kbytes of proof.

The implementation certifies 128 bits only in the unique-decoding regime, with grinding on the fold challenges. There a query is worth under one bit, so each of Ligerito's levels needs more queries than the floor has bits, whatever its rate: 309 at rate 1/2, 156 at 1/8, and 129 at rate 1/256. A proof at $2^{25}$ bits has 567 in all at rate 1/8. Rate 1/8 has 0.82 of rate 1/2's Ligerito bytes, at 1.28 times the prover, and is the proof-size minimum: the stand-alone proof at $2^{25}$ bits is 304 kbytes at rate 1/2 and 257 kbytes at 1/8, and lower rates only deepen the Merkle paths. These are the rows marked "unique decoding", at both floors.

Under the Johnson bound a query is worth half of $\log_2(1/\rho)$ bits, so squaring the rate about halves a level's queries: 228 in all at rate 1/8 and 137 at 1/64. The implementation's Johnson profile cannot be certified at 128 bits in its 128-bit field. Its level-0 binding stops at 118.7 bits, its folding-phase bound is 86 to 97 bits, and its outer round's out-of-domain bound 95 to 100. BEEP [BG26] is the analysis that meets them. It takes several out-of-domain samples before each fold in place of mutual correlated agreement, and with affine folding challenges its folding error is $2/|\mathbb{F}|$, 127 bits. The rows marked "Johnson bound with BEEP (derived)" are derived from that: the Johnson profile measured at a 128-bit target with those three checks switched off, plus BEEP's 5 samples at each of 13 folds, 2.1 kbytes. No implementation of BEEP exists, so these rows are ranked with the strongest assumption and remove no certified row from the frontier. They are at the 128-bit floor alone, since the 147-bit floor would require excessive grinding, and is mainly a hedge against attacks on lattices.

At $2^{25}$ bits the stand-alone proof under BEEP is 236 kbytes at rate 1/2, 150 kbytes at 1/8, and 126 kbytes at 1/64. With ARC at the 128-bit floor, BitZ's smallest row is 213 kbytes under BEEP, at rate 1/64, against 464 kbytes under unique decoding, at 1.8 times the merge prover, since the commitment costs more at the lower rate.

At step rank 13 and rate 1/8 with ARC, under unique decoding at the default floor, it comes to 538 kbytes with the decider's three first-round trees, 1.1e9 word multiplications, a 67 million bit merge, and a 9.74 ms decider. Its Pasta arithmetic is one relation per multiplication, and no other hash-based family's row with native or relation Pasta arithmetic has both a smaller proof and a cheaper prover; Kishu, below, beats it on both. Akita's Pasta variants beat it on proof size, and its merge is a Boolean witness of the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's size.

BitZ's paper offers succinctness only; its zero-knowledge rows here are derived, as the zero-knowledge section says. Quang Dao notes that BitZ's arithmetization, the bit-decomposed witnesses with claims over any ring, might in future be combined with a lattice backend such as Akita.

Control knobs

The PCD header, the elements carried between proofs, is the application's choice and applies to every row alike: 2, 4 or Tachyon's 9 from the control. It is 9 whenever a Tachyon transaction or the recovery circuit is the application. The recovery statement's primary input, the anchor, rk, the nullifier and SigHash, is six or seven elements with the suffix, which a header of 2 or 4 does not hold. Each element is a packed Pasta field element for every scheme alike, 64 bytes in the proof for the left and right child's copies. The rows are priced without a header, and so are the figures in the text unless they say otherwise.

  • Pedersen-IPA: step rank 13 to 16; endoscalings per step on both curves, 4 as checked in, up to what the step rank's circuit holds; challenge binders, 4 as checked in, down to the fewest that bind the eight challenges at the step rank; and the fixed circuits as today or packed into the step rank's budget.
  • Akita: step rank, and the field and modulus variant. The committed groups are five stages plus one batch of every step circuit's witness, and the number of step circuits follows from the merge circuit's gate estimate. Each step circuit's polynomial carries its own evaluation claim: one claim-field element in the proof, one atom per merge, and a few gates, which is why fewer, larger circuits help a little. The step rank itself is set by the largest application step, not by the merge, which is spread over as many step circuits of that size as it needs. The opening sizes come from the planner's runs with groups of $2^{14}$ coefficients, which overstates the cost of the small stage groups.
  • WHIR with ARC: step rank; the code rate, from 1/2 to 1/64, which trades the prover's encoding and hashing against the number of openings and the proof size; the grinding, from the control above, one setting for every hash-based family or the best per row; and the field. Pasta is FFT friendly, so the witness, the trees and the merge circuit can all stay over it. Goldilocks puts everything over the 64‑bit field with the claims in its cubic extension, which 128‑bit security needs. For Akita it is a design change: the Pasta variants need a modulus profile that Akita does not have, and the 64‑bit commitment variant a decoupled commitment modulus with its own soundness argument. Zero knowledge

Akita's mask

With the box ticked, as it is by default, the Akita configurations carry the masked witness of the zk note. The user's fold masks the accumulator witness by rejection sampling. That makes the one masked accumulator longer, 9 to 11 base-8 slices a coordinate across the lattice rows at their own accumulators, nine in the zk note's example at $2^{22}$ digits. It also asks more of the accumulation matrix, since the zk note's extraction argument needs that matrix binding at the masked bound, and every accumulator is committed under it. On the coefficient ℓ∞ route that Akita's estimator certifies, the masked bound is the extraction's slack times the response's width, $14\sigma$, with σ at each row's own accumulator: 18 to 24 bits above the unmasked bound, a fold's output. The table answers with one more module rank, or the larger degree, or both: a factor of 1.0 to 1.33 on the commitment rate, 1.27 for Kishu's rows, whose module rank 1 at degree 512 becomes module rank 2 at 1,024. The zk note's own Euclidean route, where the rows grow as the square of the bound's log, puts the factor at 2.4 at its example; the ℓ∞ bound carries no $\sqrt{N}$, and a box in place of a ball, which is the difference (its §5.5).

The length stays at the zero-knowledge layer. The aggregator's first fold on a masked accumulator range-checks its nine digit slices and folds them back to the ordinary length with its other inputs, as every lattice fold decomposes and folds. So the merges above that layer and the decider's opening see witnesses of the usual size. The extra module rank or degree stays with the accumulation matrix. Akita's internal levels and its terminal commit the final accumulator's ordinary witness under their own matrices and keep their bounds. The accumulation matrix's larger images enter the opening only as the first level's auxiliary digits, which are lower order.

On this page that factor of 1.0 to 1.33 falls on every step's prover commitment work. The merge priced here is one at the zero-knowledge layer, so it has three more rounds in each of its two sum-checks, 18 atoms. The opening and the decider's verification are unchanged but for one more circuit's claim where those atoms need it. Merges above the zero-knowledge layer pay the masked matrix's rate only, which the page does not show separately.

The accumulator memory measure counts the ordinary accumulator, stored at the handoff's norm bound. The masked accumulator is not in it, since it passes from the user to the aggregator rather than into any proof: as base-8 digit slices it is about 14 Mbytes, against 1.6 Mbytes for the ordinary accumulator's digits packed the same way. The first level's extra auxiliary digits are not modelled either. Three refinements would move these figures. A design that checks the masked fold under a matrix of its own would confine the extra module rank or degree to that layer, worth at most the factor above, and proving the change of matrix is the obstacle (the zk note's §5.9). The slices come from base-8 digits, a Gaussian width of 48 times the norm of the challenge times the witness, and each row's accumulator, so Akita's eventual Pasta profile and its digit base change them. And the 18 atoms assume the first fold's range and norm checks run over the slices at once.

The zk note's §5.6 prices the mask's parameters on its Euclidean route, where every bit on the bound costs rows: weight-20 challenges in the accumulation matrix's ring of degree 512 against weight 32 in a smaller ring, and a Gaussian 48 times the norm wide against twelve or six, which trade rows for attempts, 2.7 at twelve and 7.5 at six against 1.3, with a masked accumulator of 12.6 Mbytes at six. On the ℓ∞ route the table moves in steps, and none of those widths crosses one here, so the width is a question of attempts, slices, and the response's size. The choice is explained below, and the zk note's §5.8 moves the attempts off the user's device.

Pedersen-IPA

Ragu's zero knowledge is in every fuse: each draws two fold randomizers and a batch mask a side, uniformly random polynomials of $2^{\text{step rank}}$ coefficients, folds the randomizers as claims of their own and batches the mask, and a rerandomization is two such fuses with the public bootstrap proof as the right child (tachyon-zcash/ragu at ef7668fb6bbf5df0b77e92760fdb52af726ebaf1; its book says the hiding argument is not yet audited). The Pedersen-IPA rows carry those commitments in every fuse, so the zero-knowledge rows are the plain rows.

WHIR and FRI

For the WHIR and FRI families the zero‑knowledge rows carry the overhead measured with Plonky3's HVZK-WHIR, which composes Zook [CFW26] into a hiding commitment. On one core over KoalaBear with a quintic extension, at rate 1/4 and 100 bits, the proof is 103.6 kbytes against 30.9 kbytes at $2^{16}$ and 110.1 kbytes against 37.5 kbytes at $2^{18}$: an additive 73 kbytes of mask spot checks. The prover takes 19 to 22 ms more, 15% at $2^{18}$ and halving per doubling.

Zero knowledge is paid once per transaction, at the handoff from the user's private accumulation to the public aggregator. Under ARC the final accumulator carries the users' masked rows. So the rows add 73 kbytes, scaled with the floor, to the decider's proof and about 1,300 hash checks to its verifier. They put the masked prover's extra work, a few percent of one step at these sizes, in the transaction measure's fold. Under recursion the public merges and the decider's proof are of public data, so the rows equal the plain rows but for that fold.

That difference between the two accumulation styles is structural, not an artefact of the model. Recursion seals the private witness at the handoff, since the aggregator verifies the user's proof and discards it. Accumulation carries the users' oracles onward, as a random combination with everyone else's, until the decider opens the final accumulator, so the masks must still be there then. Akita's accumulation has the same shape, and more: the masked bound sizes the accumulation matrix that every step shares, which is why every Akita step pays the masked matrix's rate. Confining that module rank to the zero-knowledge layer, with a second matrix and proven switches at the handoff, is worked through in the zk note's §5.9, where the switch's verifier cost is the obstacle. The mask symbols would be larger over Pasta's field, which is not applied, and whether ARC's accumulation preserves zero knowledge has not been examined, so the ARC merge is left unchanged.

The $\mathbb{F}_2$ family

Flock and BitZ offer succinctness only, by their own papers; Flock expects zero knowledge could be added at low cost, through zero‑knowledge IOPPs or VEIL. The $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's zero‑knowledge rows follow the zk option of Ulrich Haböck's optimizer as of 2026-11-12. A mask of the witness's size is added in the first round, which doubles that round's leaves and adds a 32-byte blinder to each. Later rounds carry neither, since the full-entropy mask, once combined with the witness in that round's sum-check, lets them run in the clear. A schedule with no reduction opens witness, mask and blinder at the query positions before the reveal. His revision of 2026-11-10 gave each leaf one mask row, which the page priced for a day; asked whether one row suffices, he confirmed that the mask must be as large as the witness and corrected the script.

The script does not model the prover, so the rows charge the mask's commitment, equal to the witness's, once per transaction in the fold. At rate 1/8 that is 34 kbytes more proof under ARC (274 kbytes against 240 kbytes) and two thirds of the row's prover in the fold. Under recursion the rows equal the plain rows but for that fold, as for WHIR. He adds that the leaf blinders could probably be dropped, since the mask has full entropy at leaf level, but keeps them because they make the analysis clean. Only the private witness needs masking, not the public polynomials, so the three first-round oracles that the ARC decider opens masked here are an upper bound. An earlier revision opened the mask in a separate round, which cost about 105 kbytes under ARC.

The second variant, labelled Zook-style and derived rather than measured, prices Zook's masks [CFW26] for FRI-Binius's own structure. The main oracle's zero‑knowledge encoding costs nothing in the proof, since its random coefficients fit the witness padding. Each folding round's sum-check variables get a group of four-coefficient masks, each with its own randomness, encoded at rate 1/256 and spot-checked 16 times through a pruned multiproof; the ring-switching claim's revealed element gets a mask of $2^{\kappa}$ coefficients; and the terminal takes a full mask of its own size. FRI-Binius has no code switching and no out-of-domain samples, which are what make WHIR's masks large, since their message must hold the previous encoding's randomness, about a hundred coefficients. So its masks stay small, and as they belong to the proof rather than to the oracles, ARC's three trees do not multiply them. That gives about 30 kbytes more proof under ARC, with the masks' encoding in the fold, and nothing beyond the fold under recursion. Under ARC these rows cost a little more proof than the batched mask from rate 1/8 down (38 kbytes against 34 kbytes at 1/8) and less at rate 1/4, and a hundredth of the row's prover against two thirds.

Ulrich Haböck's view is that the full-entropy mask in a batching step before FRI is the way to randomize FRI-Binius. A more frugal mask would need the first rounds' sum-checks made zero-knowledge too, which is what these rows price. So they stay as the derived lower estimate. The one cross-check is that the same count reproduces Plonky3's measured 73 kbytes for WHIR. In the Zook-style rows the ring-switching mask is this page's design, not the paper's, and in both variants the ARC merge is left unchanged, as for WHIR.

Kishu

Kishu's sum-check rows carry Akita's mask as the lattice rows do: the masked matrix's rate on every step's digits, 18 more atoms in the zk-layer merge, and the user's masking fold at the handoff. The fold's rejection sampling runs over the accumulator's digits: the whole step, since the mask has to hide which application the step holds, and the stages' 26,000. For the recovery circuit Kishu's zero-knowledge optimum is a step of $1.75 \times 2^{21}$ digits, 0.116 s per note against 0.0475 s without, 0.0332 s of it the sampling at 7.1 ns a sample. Its projection rows reveal coordinates and drop out, as the binary-groups family does, and as Pedersen-IPA drops out at 147 bits.

Where that cost goes. Of the 0.116 s, the fold is 0.0571 s: the sampling 0.0332 s, the masks' commitments under the masked matrix 0.0221 s and the handoff's merge 0.00170 s. The larger part is the steps and their merges: 1 at 69.0 ms, 0.0690 s against 0.109 s without zero knowledge. What zero knowledge adds to them is the masked matrix, module rank 2 at degree 1,024 in place of module rank 1 at 512, which raises the commitment rate of every step and merge from 11.0 to 14.0 word multiplications a digit, and the zk-layer merge's atoms. The fold, the merge's worth of digits and the stages', and the masked rate together move the optimum down: with zero knowledge the best row is in a step of $1.75 \times 2^{21}$ digits and takes 1 steps, where without it the best is a step of $1.625 \times 2^{22}$ digits in a single step. The constraint PIOP is 29.9 ms.

Three things set it.

The sampler

The sampler is the one term that is pure implementation. The page's 7.1 ns a coordinate is measured on one core of an Apple M4 Max: AES-CTR randomness on the AES instructions, Box–Muller in doubles, Sheppard's correction, the tail check and the rounding, with 1.5 ns of it the randomness. With ChaCha20 in plain C the randomness is 7.7 ns and the whole path 14. The AVX Box–Muller sampler of Hülsing, Lange and Smeets [HLS18] takes 7.7 to 13 ns a coordinate on a 2014 core, measured without and with its ChaCha20 randomness. By Jeudy's theorem [Jeu26], a rounded Gaussian with Sheppard's correction, $\sigma - 1/(24\sigma)$, is a discrete Gaussian of width $\sigma$ up to a Rényi divergence of $1 + 73aN/(33{,}177{,}600\,\sigma^8)$ over $N$ coordinates. At this width that is below $2^{-179}$, so the rejection-sampling argument carries over unchanged. What double precision cannot do is round exactly. A coordinate's error is its size times $2^{-53}$, which leaves about $2^{-25}$ of the coordinates undecided between two integers, one coordinate in $2^{3}$ folds. That fraction is also the worst-case bound of [HLS18] on the divergence the error would leave. Refining those few coordinates in higher precision makes the rounding exact. The exact alternative, Karney's method [Kar16], costs about 300 ns a coordinate, measured at 290 to 470 ns by Du, Fan and Wei [DFW20]. With it the recovery would take 0.855 s in a step of $1.625 \times 2^{20}$ digits, and at 100 ns 0.401 s in a step of $1.75 \times 2^{20}$ digits.

BitZ's zero-knowledge rows are derived, since its paper offers succinctness only. Ligero's random row per Ligerito level puts one symbol in every opened column, so that the columns reveal nothing. Security floor

Akita's catalog sizes every matrix so that the cheapest known attack on its Module-SIS instance costs at least 128 bits under the ADPS16 quantum core-SVP model, $2^{0.265\beta}$ for BKZ block size $\beta$. That puts every instance at block size 484 to 486. ARC's openings are counted at the same $\lambda$, and the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's optimizer runs at the floor, which shrinks its lists.

For the lattice rows the floor is the lattice assumption's. A proof's soundness has a second part that no lattice estimate enters: the chance that a challenge lets a false claim pass. That is a count over the challenge's field or set, multiplied by the attacker's hash queries. Akita holds it to 128 bits at both floors, by a proof of work before each challenge whose error would otherwise exceed that. So the 147‑bit floor is 128 bits with the lattice part held to a more conservative cost model, not 147 bits throughout. Kishu's own claim reduction, in its 128‑bit field, has an error of about $2^{-121}$ an attempt, which is 7 bits short of that target counted in attempts. It needs no proof of work for so few: an attempt costs the adversary more than $2^7$ operations, since it has to hash the challenge and check what it gives.

The accumulation matrix that the lattice rows commit under is sized the same way. For each row the model takes the cheapest module rank and ring degree, among the catalog's 512 and 1,024, that Akita's ℓ∞ estimator certifies at the floor for the row's bound, with the row's accumulator as the width, from the estimator's own width tables at the catalog's bound ladder, the bound rounded up to the ladder. The bound is the one the zk note's extraction argument needs: without the mask a fold's output, the two children at the challenge's weight and the step's own digits, and with the mask the response's width $14\sigma$ at the row's own accumulator, times the extraction's slack. The zero-knowledge section says what the mask adds; without it every row is at module rank 1, as the catalog's own accumulation matrix is.

The 147‑bit floor, the page's default, re-plans Akita's catalog at that floor with Akita's own planner, so its openings are planned rather than scaled. It is 147 rather than 150 because Akita's rank-one compression maps certify at 147.3 bits at the catalog's geometry; the margin section below explains. The module-rank growth at that floor is applied to the inner images' share of the verification; the accumulation matrix that every step commits under is looked up in the floor's own table, as the margin section says. That share is the terminal's clear inner images at the shipped geometry, 1,344 coordinates whose bytes Module-SIS sets independently of the modulus. They are 33 percent of the opening at the 128-bit field, 35 on Goldilocks, 30 with 64-bit commitments, 26 with Pasta images at SIS size, and 42 with Pasta for everything, where they are 32-byte field elements. The verifier's work is taken to divide as the message does.

WHIR's proofs, measured over both fields, are 16 percent larger at 147 bits than at 128, and ARC's openings grow with the floor; the margin section below has both.

Pedersen-IPA over Pasta rests on the discrete logarithm in a group of order near $2^{254}$: about 126 classical bits by Pollard rho, and nothing against a quantum adversary. It is shown at the 128‑bit floor as the conventional pre‑quantum baseline, and under the arithmetic-hash option, since its circuits hash with Poseidon, so the page's defaults do not show it. It drops out at 147 bits, which it does not reach even classically.

The binary-groups row is shown at 128 bits only too, since its Module-SIS certificate is not yet issued.

The table's Assumption column names what each family rests on: the discrete logarithm on Pasta, Module-SIS with a random oracle, or a random oracle alone. It is one of the dominance test's requirements (Pasta and the scheme assumption). The next section says how much to trust the Module-SIS figures.

The 165‑bit level

The third option is shown for the lattice-based schemes only, since hash-based schemes cannot reach 165‑bit security at feasible cost. It is based on lattice parameters with a similar level of conjectured security to NIST's Level 3 (e.g., to ML-KEM-768 and ML-DSA-65): a block size of about 623. That block size should in theory reach 165 quantum core-SVP bits, or 182 classical core-SVP bits.

⚠️ This level makes significant design changes to its schemes relative to Akita, including a three-stage map construction using larger module ranks that has not been fully reviewed or analyzed. Therefore, it is ranked with the strongest assumption.

A scheme at the level differs from its 147‑bit form in four ways:

  • A commitment's image is compressed to the same 128 bytes by three maps where Akita's chain has two. Akita's maps, with outputs of 2,048 and then 1,024 bits, stop at 147.3 and 153.2 bits. By Akita's estimator a chain with outputs of 2,304, 1,152, and 1,024 bits has at least 170.7 bits at every map. Its first two maps alone would give an image of 144 bytes, which works but is not optimal. A fourth map gives an image of 120 bytes which provides no significant additional benefit. So the level could be re-planned at a floor of 170 bits, the most that this chain allows, as 147 bits is the most that Akita's chain allows. It has not been: everything else at the level is sized for 165. The intermediate outputs are not one power-of-two ring element, so the first two maps have module rank 9 over smaller power-of-two rings, of dimension 2 and 1 for the 128‑bit modulus. The estimator's tables at the level certify each map with 15% to 27% of input width to spare. The chain exists in the planner only: the prover and the verifier are not adapted to it.
  • A claim is in a larger challenge field. A sum-check's error is a small multiple of its number of variables over the field's size. For Kishu's claim reduction over a field $E$ in $m$ variables it is at most $(4m+1)/|E|$, with $m \le 31$, a numerator of at most 125 (Theorem B). So the level needs a field of at least 172 bits, not 165. The 128‑bit field and Goldilocks's quadratic extension, each of 128 bits, give an error of about $2^{-121}$. So the 128‑bit field's claims move to its quadratic extension, of 256 bits, and the 64‑bit fields' to their cubic extension, of 192 bits, where the error is about $2^{-185}$. The errors of Akita's own sum-checks at the level have not been worked out here, and are taken to be of the same form. The sum-checks' prover is scaled by the field products of a multiplication in the larger field.
  • The fold's ternary challenge has weight 25, for $2^{165}$ challenges in degree 512, where the catalog's weight 20 gives $2^{138}$. That raises the bound that the accumulation matrix is certified at by 0.64 bits.
  • The transcript's hash is a choice, which the control offers: BLAKE3 or SHA3-384. No attack on a 256‑bit transcript hash is known at the level. But the security proofs for Fiat–Shamir over a chained or sponge hash lose a term of about $T^2/2^c$ for work $T$ and a state of $c$ bits. The level allows work $T$ a success probability of $T/2^{165}$, so a proof at the level needs a state of 330 bits. BLAKE3's chaining value is 256 bits, which gives a proof only up to $2^{91}$ work. SHA3-384's capacity is 768 bits.

Nothing at the level is measured. Its rows are obtained as follows, in three kinds: planned or certified by Akita's own tools at the level, derived from the 147‑bit rows, and not re-examined.

  • The opening's bytes and their growth with the witness are planned: Akita's planner was run at a 165‑bit floor with the three-map chain. The plan is below the line through the 128‑ and 147‑bit plans. Summed over the group counts, the 128‑bit field's opening is 1.2% larger than at 147 bits where the line gives 11%, and Goldilocks's is 6.3% larger where the line gives 5.5%. The planner keeps each profile's own challenge field, so the opening's 976 challenge-field elements, counted from the level's plan, are then widened to the level's.
  • The accumulation matrix is looked up in the estimator's width table, regenerated at 165 bits, for the 64‑bit and 128‑bit moduli. The estimator has not been run at the level for the Pallas modulus, whose table is extrapolated from the 147‑bit one, by a rule that reproduces the other two moduli's widths at the level closely.
  • The growth of the Module-SIS ranks, which scales the prover's rows term, is computed from the regenerated tables for the catalog's own moduli, a factor of 1.31 over the 128‑bit floor. For the 64‑bit modulus and for Pallas it is on the line through its 128‑ and 147‑bit values, factors of 1.27 and 1.02. Kishu's rows and Akita's over Goldilocks read the 64‑bit modulus's.
  • Kishu's Booleanity check by projection has no row at the level. Its certificate's error is $2^{-136}$, short of the level, so the level's rows use the sum-check form alone. At the 128‑ and 147‑bit floors the rows without zero knowledge keep the check. Akita's transcript is held to 128 bits at those floors, by a proof of work before each challenge whose error would otherwise exceed that, and the check's error is within it. The 147‑bit plan keeps that target, so the 147‑bit floor is the lattice assumption's alone.
  • The rest of a row is derived from its 147‑bit row, with the four changes above.
  • The mask's statistical parameters are the zk note's, unchanged. They bound what a distinguisher learns from each proof it sees, which grows with the number of proofs and not with its work, so they need not follow the level. An error that an adversary can retry against does: under Fiat–Shamir a challenge is a hash of the transcript, so each attempt at a sum-check round, or at the projection, costs a hash, and the error is multiplied by the work.
  • Not re-examined for the level: the errors of Akita's own sum-checks; and a Keccak permutation's count of prime-field gates, which is taken as Kishu's count of digits.

With a BLAKE3 transcript and zero knowledge, Akita over Goldilocks in circuits of $2^{16}$ gates takes a step of $1.41 \times 2^{22}$ gates against $1.78 \times 2^{21}$ at 147 bits, 2.6 times the accumulation prover, and 20% more bytes. Kishu's merge with zero knowledge is 1.7 million digits, against 1.3 million at 147 bits, so its shortest step is $1.125 \times 2^{21}$ digits against $1.625 \times 2^{20}$. The merge's commitments are 94% dearer and the proof 18% larger. In Kishu's cubic extension a multiplication is six field products with three values of output. Its sum-checks' prover, measured in the quadratic extension, is taken at twice that rate, for the six field products against three.

With a SHA3-384 transcript the step that holds Akita's merge is of $1.25 \times 2^{25}$ to $1.03 \times 2^{27}$ gates, over its five variants. Kishu's merge with zero knowledge is then 7.6 million digits, so its shortest step is $1.25 \times 2^{23}$ digits, and the merge's commitments are 9.1 times the 147‑bit ones.

The hash-based families have no row at the level. A Merkle tree's binding needs a digest of twice the level's bits, as a transcript needs a state: a collision search of work $T$ succeeds with about $T^2/2^n$ for $n$ bits of output. So their trees would hash with SHA3-384, whose output is 384 bits; BLAKE3's longer outputs have 256‑bit bottlenecks inside. A tree node under SHA3-384 is a Keccak permutation, 6.2 times BLAKE3's gates in a prime field, and the openings grow with the level. So WHIR over Pasta at rate 1/8 and step rank 16 would need 38,525 circuits against 5,011 with BLAKE3 at 147 bits, 7.7 times as many. The $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's merge, with Flock's block of $2^{16}$ bits for a Keccak permutation, would be 323 million bits against 68 million in its 256‑bit design, 4.7 times. The Goldilocks variants would also need a challenge field beyond the cubic extension. BitZ's proof of concept is fixed to a 128‑bit field and cannot reach the level, and Pedersen-IPA and the binary-groups row stop at 128 bits as they do at the default floor.

A signature scheme at the 192‑bit level compounds its cost with the proof system's. For illustration only, the transaction list has a Zoodles transaction whose two spends are authorized by one SLH-DSA-SHAKE-192s signature, which the SLH-DSA page describes: 283 million of Kishu's digits, which Kishu with a BLAKE3 transcript proves at the default floor with zero knowledge in 8.20 s on one core, against 0.110 s with leanSPHINCS over BLAKE3.

Grinding

The hash-based families can spend proof of work in the query phase: with $g$ bits of grinding the queries need carry only $\lambda - g$ bits. So ARC's openings per input fall to $2(\lambda - g)/\log_2(1/\rho)$, and WHIR's own query counts fall in the same way. A fixed setting applies to every hash-based row at once, from none, 16, 20 and 24 bits. The default, best per row, gives each configuration the measured setting with the smallest prover, from a wider set of measurements, and the row's label says which.

Each row's proof size is measured at its setting with the reference implementation over both fields, rates 1/2 to 1/64, at $2^{17}$, $2^{18}$ and $2^{20}$, with the growth per doubling taken over the three doublings. The earlier rows were measured at 20 bits while ARC's openings were counted without grinding, which this corrects. At rate 1/8 over the 256‑bit field the proof is 147 kbytes with no grinding, 130 kbytes at 16 bits, 126 kbytes at 20 and 121 kbytes at 24; at rate 1/32 it is 113 kbytes, 100 kbytes, 96 kbytes and 93 kbytes.

The prover pays $2^g$ evaluations of the grinding hash per merge, taken as the family's own Merkle hash so that the merge circuit's check is one node per input. Each bit of grinding removes about one opening in a hundred, so about a percent of the circuits and of the prover, while the grinding's cost doubles. The prover is least where the two cross: at 17 to 19 bits for families with Poseidon trees ($2^{17}$ permutations are about 0.5 percent of a merge's prover at rate 1/8 and step rank 13, $2^{20}$ about 4 percent), and 12 to 18 bits for the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family and BitZ. The minimum is shallow, within a percent from 16 to 20 bits.

With BLAKE3 trees, the default, the WHIR-type rows take 24 bits, the most measured, since $2^{24}$ compressions are a small part of a merge spread over tens of thousands of circuits. The settings and the lower rates follow Ulrich Haböck's questions. The capacity regime, which would halve the openings again, is not offered: its conjectures were refuted in their strongest form, and what has been proved beyond the Johnson bound since does not reach it (see WHIR and FRI).

Hashes in circuits

Every hash that a merge circuit computes is one of two. The default is a conventional hash, BLAKE3, at 14,900 gates per 64-byte compression in a lookup-free R1CS over Pasta or Goldilocks and about 200 word multiplications natively. The option allows an arithmetic hash, Poseidon 1.1 over the circuit's own field: 288 gates per Pasta permutation absorbing four elements, about 480 for the width-12 Goldilocks one, and 35,000 or 7,200 word multiplications natively.

Arithmetic hashes deserve consideration on the merits: for large fields and the widths in question, none of the attacks on Poseidon 1.1 has come near an academic break, and Tachyon uses it. The reason to exclude them by default is future-proofing. For long-term storage that reason is decisive, though some of a proof system's hashes are ones that long-term storage does not depend on. If requiring conventional hashes causes only a small regression that does not affect deployability, they are the choice; the option shows what the arithmetic hash buys.

With the option the rows priced with Poseidon join the BLAKE3 ones rather than replace them. So a configuration that is better on some measure with the conventional hash stays on the plot, and each frontier takes the better of the two. Ragu's checked-in circuits use Poseidon 1.1, so the Pedersen-IPA rows exist only under the option.

What the default changes: Akita's merge absorbs its transcript, 4 to 9 kbytes, in 55 to 133 compressions instead of 146 to 218 Poseidon atoms. The transcript is a Merkle–Damgård chain at the application level: each block is one keyed BLAKE3 call, whose key is the previous call's output. That is one compression a block, where one BLAKE3 call over the whole transcript would add a parent compression for each 1,024-byte chunk, 6.25% more. So the merge grows from about 25 thousand gates to 0.83 million at the 128‑bit field and on Goldilocks and 2.0 million for the Pasta variants, at step rank 16. Each step circuit's evaluation claim then costs more to absorb than a circuit of step rank 13 holds, and a 32-byte claim more than a circuit of step rank 14. So step rank 13 has no Akita rows and step rank 14 none for the Pasta variants, and the first step ranks that hold them take 1,740 and 1,472 circuits.

At step rank 16 the merge takes 51 circuits at the 128‑bit field and on Goldilocks and 122 for the Pasta variants, against 2 with Poseidon. So the merge prover is 26 times the Poseidon one at the 128‑bit field (6.3e9 against 2.5e8) and 28 times on Goldilocks (6.6e8 against 2.4e7), whose wider BLAKE3 accumulator at the 64‑bit modulus takes the matrix to degree 1,024 at this floor; 61 times for the Pasta variants, and 67 with 64‑bit commitments, for the same reason. The proof grows by 4 to 4 kbytes at the small fields and by 10 to 11 kbytes with Pasta claims, from the per-circuit claims, and the atoms by 49 to 120. The per-step commitment cost and the decider are unchanged. The lever that would recover most of this is to aggregate the step circuits' claims before absorption, a design change.

For the WHIR-type rows every Merkle node becomes a BLAKE3 compression, so the merge grows 35 to 61 times (1.3 to 76 million gates for WHIR over Pasta at rate 1/8 and step rank 13, 614 to 37,225 circuits). The prover grows 1.3 to 2.0 times for WHIR over either field and for FRI, since the trees get cheap but the circuits multiply. The limb hybrid's prover instead falls 2.2 times, since its Poseidon over limbs is dear natively. The decider's verification falls 17 to 83 times, to a few milliseconds; the proofs grow by a sixth with the larger batched witness, and the best grinding moves to 24 bits.

The $\mathbb{F}_2$ family, BitZ and the binary-groups family hash with BLAKE3 already and do not change, so under the default their comparison with Akita is like for like. At rate 1/8 the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family's ARC row proves a merge in 1.0e9 word multiplications for a proof of 240 kbytes, against Akita over Goldilocks at step rank 16 with 6.6e8 and 77 kbytes. Its merge is 37 to 188 million AND gates against Akita's 0.83 million Pasta gates, which are not the same unit.

Transaction prover

The transaction prover measure prices a whole transaction rather than one merge, so that the cost of zero knowledge can be seen against the steps it belongs to. The transaction proves an application chosen above: the Ironwood recovery circuit; a transaction with four recovery inputs, which is four of the circuit under one zero-knowledge handoff; or a number of Merkle path checks of 32 levels and the leaf; or the hardware wallet's part of the recovery statement on its own, or a Zoodles transaction of two inputs and two outputs, both priced in the application subsection; the Tachyon transactions have the next section.

The application is costed component by component in each family's own arithmetization, under the selected hash policy. The family's steps are then as many as hold it, rounded up, since a transaction smaller than a step pays a whole step. A step circuit of step rank $r$ commits $2^r$ coefficients, which is $2^{r-2}$ gates of four wires for the field arithmetizations, $2^r$ bits or digits for BitZ and Kishu, and $2^{22}$ AND gates for the Boolean families, which have no step rank. Each step is committed at the family's per-step cost, which is the row's prover divided by its circuits, and there are as many merges at the row's accumulation prover, the merge's commitments and the fold's sum-checks. Kishu's step is a slot of whole circuits that the merge and the application share: the merge's digits and the step's transcript take the first part, and the application's nodes fill the rest, at least half a circuit, as many as fit. Its digits are charged at their own number, at the row's cost per digit, since a zero digit costs the Ajtai hash and the range sum-check nothing, while the fold's sum-checks and the mask run over the whole slot.

The application in each arithmetization

Off Pasta the recovery circuit's curve arithmetic is emulated, in the ways the requirements section lists: about 14,500 multiplications in Pallas's base field, in five fixed-base and one variable-base scalar multiplication with complete additions, 160 Sinsemilla chunk additions and the nullifier's Poseidon. Its seven BLAKE2b compressions, the chunks' generator selections and its Merkle path are bit operations and cost about the same gates in any prime field. Over Pasta the circuit is 570,000 gates with a Poseidon path and 1.05 million with a BLAKE3 one. Over Goldilocks a multiplication is about 1,100 gates, with nine 29‑bit limbs and no lookups, so the circuit is 16.5 and 17.0 million gates; at a 128‑bit field, with 61‑bit limbs, about 700 gates give 10.7 and 11.2 million.

In a Boolean circuit a multiplication is about 120,000 AND gates, the 255‑bit product by Karatsuba and Toom–Cook and a Montgomery reduction by a prime of no special form. A 64‑bit addition is 64 ANDs and an XOR is free, so the hashes cost half their Pasta gates. The circuit is 1.73 billion AND gates, the multiplications nearly all of it.

In BitZ's integer arithmetization a multiplication is 510 committed bits, the value and the quotient, checked as one integer relation, while a hash gate is one bit and a 64‑bit addition 65, so the circuit is 8.2 million bits. In Kishu over Akita the claims are in one fixed field, so a multiplication is either 374 digits by the limb route or 170 by the fingerprint route, which pays per step instead. The circuit is 6.1 million or 3.2 million digits, plus its constraint PIOP, which the Kishu section prices. The bit arithmetizations hash the path with BLAKE3 under either policy, since Poseidon over Pallas's field would cost them 240 multiplications a node.

A Merkle path check is 33 nodes: 9,500 Pasta gates, or 15,600 Goldilocks or 128‑bit-field gates, with Poseidon in the field; 492,000 gates in any prime field with BLAKE3; 340,000 AND gates, which are also BitZ's committed bits; or 374,000 Kishu digits. So the recovery circuit's hashes are what make the bit arithmetizations cheap, a path-heavy application makes them cheaper still, and the Boolean families pay for every Pallas multiplication.

Further applications price the hardware wallet's part of the Recovery Statement, which ZIP 2005's Option A proves on the device that holds the quantum spending key, the host proving the rest ([ZIP-2005], Key storage options). The device's own statement, $\mathsf{qk} = \mathsf{H^{qk}}(\mathsf{qsk})$ with the linking commitment left out, is one BLAKE3 compression: 14,900 gates in any prime field, 10,400 AND gates, or 11,344 Kishu digits. If the device instead signs SigHash with leanSPHINCS, the stateless hash-based signature of post-quantum Ethereum [EIP-8288] [Lit26], the host proves the verifier with the signature and the public key as witnesses. For [Wam26]'s pruned key that is 262 Keccak-f permutations, or 298 compressions of BLAKE2s or of BLAKE3, plus the 4,276-byte signature as input bits. XOR is not free in a digit arithmetization, so a Keccak-f permutation is 92,160 committed bits where a BLAKE2s compression is 16,096 and a BLAKE3 compression 11,344. What the wallet proves, what it hands over, and what the two routes cost on the device and in the transaction are the subject of the Spend authorization section.

Zoodles is a sketch of a spend protocol with no curve arithmetic at all, named for the pasta-free noodle, and priced as a transaction of two inputs and two outputs. It keeps Tachyon's architecture (the next section): the same tree of spend, output, merge, and fee steps, and a note's membership and unspentness attested by a background lineage over the chain's anchors rather than by a Merkle path in the spend. The published tachygrams are roots of set polynomials that the merges multiply at the challenge: a spend's two epoch nullifiers, an output's commitment and padding, and the fee step's change commitment. In Tachyon the set polynomials are Pedersen vector commitments and the bridge hash is a Pedersen hash onto Pallas. Here they are the proof system's own committed vectors with evaluation claims, and the bridge hash is a BLAKE3 hash of the commitment, computed natively. A root then costs one multiplication in a 256-bit extension of the claim field, and an opening one more claim, absorbed by the merge above and folded by a few multiplications: 3,020 Kishu digits. The curve goes. Amounts are range-checked and summed in the PCD headers in place of value commitments and the binding signature. Spend authorization is chosen for each spending key, usually the same for all, and is proved once for each key that a transaction spends from, not once an input, in a node of its own that every spend from that key uses (the Spend authorization page). It is a Sprout-like preimage check: the payment key is the BLAKE3 hash of the nullifier key and the quantum key, and the quantum key is the BLAKE3 hash of the quantum spending key, as ZIP 2005 derives it, so the spend step derives the payment key as Tachyon's does and the authorization is one compression more; or a leanSPHINCS signature at the pruned key, with BLAKE3, BLAKE2s, or Keccak, over a transaction digest, verified in the circuit with the public key as a witness, since the zero-knowledge fold hides the key and no re-randomized signature is needed. The preimage check is proved inside the transaction's own proof, whose graph binds it to the rest of the transaction, directly and through the PCD headers. Its transcript is tied to the transaction's digest too, so that an accumulator holding it cannot authorize another transaction. A signature is made outside the proof, so its message must bind the transaction's effecting data: each spend's two nullifiers, each output's commitment, the change commitment, the fee, and the epoch, 235 bytes or 4 compressions. The digest is a PCD header value: the authorization node verifies the signature over it, and the fee step recomputes it from the nullifiers and commitments that its children's headers carry, which a small transaction's headers hold. At the step rank the signature transaction favours that hashing is under a tenth of one step; the signature verification is the expensive part. The fee step fixes the fee last and hashes the change note, the maximum-fee note's value less the fee, as Tachyon's does. Every hash is BLAKE3. The proof's 13 nodes hash 16 compressions in all, with 12 roots and 15 openings. The lineage's steps run in the background and are not in the transaction, as Tachyon's are not; one epoch of them is priced below.

In Kishu the preimage transaction is 240,013 digits and the signature one with BLAKE3 3.67 million, the two verifiers being most of it. With zero knowledge Kishu proves the preimage transaction in 0.0209 s on one core in a step of $2^{20}$ digits and the BLAKE3 signature one in 0.110 s in a step of $1.875 \times 2^{21}$ digits, so the preimage route is under a quarter of the signature route; without zero knowledge they are 0.00232 and 0.0364 s. In the other families, preimage then BLAKE3 signature: BitZ with zero knowledge 0.386 and 0.443 s; Boolean over Akita 0.0794 and 0.0794 s at its 128‑bit floor; Flock 0.322 and 0.322 s; and Akita over Goldilocks with a Poseidon tree 0.287 and 4.68 s. Tachyon's own four tachyactions, with their curve arithmetic, take 0.0966 s in Kishu with zero knowledge and 0.0663 without, and 1.23 s in BitZ, so Zoodles by preimage costs less in each, 22 percent of Tachyon with zero knowledge. A simpler Zoodles that proves a 32-level Merkle path in each spend, opening the note there and deriving one nullifier, with no set polynomials, is 919,071 digits by preimage and 0.0320 s with zero knowledge, 33 percent of Tachyon, and still costs less than Tachyon in Kishu and BitZ; by leanSPHINCS with BLAKE3 it is 0.131 s. Its two paths are most of its digits. The Pasta-native families reverse the order: with Pedersen-IPA under the arithmetic-hash option Tachyon is 8.44 s and the Merkle variant 50.6, since a path's hashes cost a prime field more than the curve does. Flock takes 17.4 s on Tachyon, 54 times its Zoodles figure, due to the Pallas multiplications in a Boolean circuit.

The lineage's own cost is the wallet's witness update, one step per note per epoch of 4,096 blocks, about 1.2 days at 25-second blocks: the shape of Tachyon's unspent lift. The step proves that the note's nullifier for the epoch is not among the epoch's published tachygrams. Tachyon attests that with a bucket, one residue profile's share of the epoch's tachygrams. The share is partitioned by whether $x + R_j$ is a square at successive discriminants, down to at most 8,192 members (the size of its online polynomial slot), and folded as a set polynomial that the step opens at the nullifier. Over Pedersen a commitment folds in one scalar multiplication. Over digits the bucket's tachygrams would enter the wallet's accumulator element by element, 2.10 million bits or digits, and set its step rank. Set polynomials are the right tool over Pedersen and a dear one over digits, so Zoodles uses a sorted Merkle tree instead. The builder sorts the epoch's tachygrams once for everyone and hashes consecutive pairs into the leaves, so an exclusion proof is one leaf, the nullifier's two neighbours, with its path. The step derives the epoch's nullifier, hashes the leaf, proves the path, compares the nullifier with both neighbours, and range-checks the epoch. For a tree of 1,048,576 tachygrams that is 22 hashes, 250,000 Kishu digits, under a sixth of the bucket's. The tree's size is a choice, 256 tachygrams a block over the epoch, between the bucket's $2^{13}$ and a busy chain's $2^{24}$. Each doubling adds one hash to the step, and a shorter epoch adds steps a day. Kishu takes 0.00248 s a note an epoch on one core in a step of $1.625 \times 2^{22}$ digits, without zero knowledge, since the lineage never leaves the wallet. BitZ takes 0.385 s, Flock 0.322 s, Akita over Goldilocks 0.0319 s, and Pedersen-IPA 6.33 s at its 128‑bit floor. The lineage step's length is set apart from the spend's, since the two would not choose the same one. The spend's own best step with zero knowledge is $2^{20}$ digits. In a step of $1.625 \times 2^{22}$ digits Zoodles by preimage would take 0.107 s with zero knowledge, 5.1 times its least.

The masking fold

For Akita's zero-knowledge rows the user's masking fold is added. It is the expected rejection-sampling attempts, 1.3 at the page's Gaussian width, plus the handoff's merge. That merge is the small one that the zk note's §5.8 deferral leaves the user. It derives the challenge from the mask's commitments and its evaluations at the claims' points, checks the commitment identity on those images, and folds the claims, in whole step circuits of the step rank. The decomposition of the response and the range and norm proofs are the aggregator's first fold's, which the zero-knowledge-layer merge that the accumulation prover prices already carries. Each attempt is a commitment of a mask the size of the accumulator under the masked accumulation matrix, and as many Gaussian samples at about 7.1 nanoseconds a sample as a rounded Gaussian with exact rounding, 21 word multiplications at the page's rate. The sampling does not depend on the modulus. The fold is paid once per transaction whatever it proves. The mask covers the user chain's last accumulator, the merge's circuits × $2^{\text{step rank}}$ digits and the stages'. The device's statement is the exception: the wallet hands over a bare leaf without a fuse of its own, so its mask covers the leaf's $2^{\text{step rank}}$ digits alone, and the merge that folds the leaf is the host's. The hash-based zero-knowledge rows likewise put their masked prover's extra work in the fold, once per transaction; under recursion that is their only zero-knowledge cost, since the public merges and the decider's proof are of public data. Pedersen-IPA's blinding is free. This is total work, not latency: the zk note's §5.8 precomputes candidate masks in idle time and defers the decomposition to the aggregator, which leaves the device a sparse ring product and the transfer of the masked witness. An Orchard-style action has the recovery circuit's mix, with one path check and no BLAKE2b.

Tachyon transactions

The transaction options of 2, 4, 8 or 16 tachyactions, half spends and half outputs, keep Tachyon's own proof tree (tachyon-zcash/tachyon 73031f7, Stamp::prove). A spend is two steps, an output a seed and a step, each action after the first a merge, then the fee step and Ragu's rerandomization: 3s + 3o + 2 nodes for s spends and o outputs. The rerandomization is two fuses in Ragu as it is, each with the public bootstrap proof as the right child; Tachyon's pinned Ragu did it in one.

The page re-packs that tree for each step rank's step size: a node larger than a step takes whole steps and its remainder joins the rest, which fill steps first-fit decreasing, and each rerandomization fuse is its own step. So at step rank 13 over Pasta no two action circuits share a step and the merge rides with the fee step, 7 steps for two tachyactions against Tachyon's 8. At step rank 15 every circuit of two tachyactions fits one step beside the two rerandomization fuses.

The nodes' circuits were measured through Ragu's synthesis analysis on Tachyon's steps at its header size of 9: 1,630 and 1,649 gates for a spend's two steps, 1,467 and 1,351 for an output's, 17 for an action merge and 1,934 for the fee step, in Pasta gates with Poseidon, all within step rank 13's 2,048. In the other arithmetizations the transaction is Tachyon's circuits as they are, a pre-quantum protocol proven in a post-quantum proof system: the Poseidon permutations, 192 gates each in Ragu's gate model, are hashed under the page's policy; the fixed-base multiplications' window selections cost the bits' four monomials; and the window additions and the openings of staged values at $\zeta$ are Pallas multiplications. The remaining gates are the scalars' bit checks, the canonicity and range comparisons and the step's glue, priced as bit operations, one gate or committed bit each, which understates any among them that multiply field elements. So a node that is one step over Pasta is 6 to 8 steps of step rank 17 over Goldilocks with Poseidon and 5 to 7 Boolean steps, which is where the range of step counts comes from. Pricing the remaining gates as multiplications too would give 27 to 39 Goldilocks steps.

Per-note steps that run in the background are not counted; a Zoodles note's lineage step, their counterpart, is an application of its own. A Tachyon transaction also needs Tachyon's header of nine elements, so while one is selected every row is shown at that header. The recovery circuit's options do the same, since its primary input needs six or seven elements.

On one core at the default floor, Pedersen-IPA at its own 128‑bit floor, two tachyactions take: with Pedersen-IPA at the checked-in configuration 7 fuses, 10.2 s, where Tachyon's own tree of 8 fuses is 11.7 s, and at step rank 15 with 16 endoscalings per step 3 fuses, 6.33 s; Akita with Pasta claims over 64‑bit commitments 3.08 s at step rank 15 with Poseidon; BitZ 1.18 s at step rank 20; Kishu 0.0578 s in a step of $1.5 \times 2^{20}$ digits with Poseidon by the limbs route; Akita over Goldilocks 1.15 s in 36 steps with Poseidon; and the $\mathbb{F}_2$ family 9.97 s in 31. With zero knowledge Akita with Pasta claims takes 4.33 s and Kishu 0.0811 s, and BitZ is unchanged.

Sixteen tachyactions take: Pedersen-IPA 51.1 s in 35 steps at step rank 13, or 19.0 s in 9 steps at step rank 15; Akita with Pasta claims 5.58 s; BitZ 1.32 s; Kishu 0.111 s; Goldilocks 7.17 s.

For scale, Orchard's Halo 2 prover, measured on the same machine with assembly, takes 1.88 s for two actions and about 14 s for sixteen on one thread. So Tachyon on Pedersen-IPA is 3.6 to 5.4 times Orchard per action on one core, and about three times that again on all cores in the current implementation, which has had less attention to parallelization of the fuse.

How much to trust the Module-SIS parameters

Akita's advantage over the hash-based route rests on Module-SIS, where that route rests on a random oracle alone and Pedersen-IPA on the discrete logarithm. This section states what the parameters assume and where the uncertainty sits.

The sizing rule

Every matrix in Akita's catalog is sized so that the cheapest known attack on its Module-SIS instance costs at least $2^{128}$ operations under the quantum core-SVP model of [ADPS16]. That model charges $2^{0.265\beta}$ for lattice reduction with BKZ block size $\beta$ and ignores the polynomial factors.

The block size that reaches a given root Hermite factor is taken from the BKZ 2.0 analysis of [CN11]. Every instance in the catalog lands at block size 484 to 486, in lattices of dimension 2,000 to 4,800. The catalog's own security check reports 128.26 bits on each.

A cross-check with the maintained lattice estimator [LE] agrees on the ranks. The estimator is maintained; the paper that introduced it, [APS15], is not, and its authors say so in a 2019 note. The estimator costs BKZ as $8n$ calls to an SVP oracle of the block size, with sieving per [BDGL16] classically and per [LMvdP15] and [Laa16] quantumly. It follows [ADPS16] for the primal attack, as [AGVW17] established, and leaves out the dimensions for free of [Duc18] and [ADH+19]. The same block size under other models:

Where the uncertainty sits

Three places, in decreasing order of concern.

  • Proof-level slack, not the assumption. Lattice schemes have slipped before by sizing for honest norms rather than the extractor's. Akita sizes for the extractor's collision bound, which is why the masked variant needs a rank of 340 field elements against 143. But the knowledge-soundness proof in the quantum random-oracle model is still in progress. And the decoupled commitment modulus has no soundness argument yet beyond the LaBinius analogue. A gap of that kind moves parameters by a lot, not by twenty bits.
  • The instantiation is unusual. Ring dimension 64 with 128‑bit or 255‑bit moduli, compression rings of dimension 8 and 4, and norm bounds up to $2^{48}$ are far from the regime of the standardized schemes. No attack exploits module structure beyond constant factors. Module-SIS has a worst-case reduction [LS15], and the quantum attacks on ideal lattices [CDW17] need principal ideals, which modules of rank 64 and more do not present. But there is less cryptanalytic attention on this corner, and the $\ell_\infty$ bounds take their own estimator route.
  • The cost model. Core-SVP is already a lower-bound style model. Refinements since 2016, such as the dimensions for free of [Duc18], have moved concrete estimates by ten to twenty bits either way. The disputed dual-attack improvements of [MATZOV22], reassessed in [DP23], concern LWE rather than SIS, which is attacked through the primal route. A 147‑bit quantum floor is 162 classical bits, and the paranoid model still gives 115; at 128 bits, 141 and 100.

Against the other assumptions

The hash-based route's assumption is weaker in the good sense: a random oracle and collision resistance only. Its soundness risk is concentrated in the proximity-gap conjectures, which is exactly where the 2025 refutations landed. So every hash-based row here takes its query counts from the proven Johnson regime. The rows derived from BEEP rest on an analysis of November 2026 that no implementation uses yet, which is why they are ranked with the strongest assumption.

Pedersen-IPA rests on the discrete logarithm on Pasta, about 126 classical bits and nothing against a quantum adversary. So the fair statement is that Akita dominates on efficiency while adding one assumption whose concrete hardness is estimated rather than proven. That assumption has SIS studied since [Ajt96], Module-SIS since [LS15], and standardization behind it.

A margin

The 147‑bit floor, the page's default, puts the block size at 556 against the 128‑bit floor's 484 to 486. The security-floor option above re-plans Akita's catalog at the higher floor with Akita's own planner. Its single security policy is retargeted to 147 bits, its runtime width tables are regenerated by its estimator at that floor, and its six rank-one compression cells are re-sized from them. So the openings at 147 bits are planned, not scaled.

The floor is 147 rather than 150 because the compression chain is built from rank-one maps. At the catalog's geometry its first map, at ring dimension 16 and width 4,096, certifies at 147.3 bits. A 150-bit floor allows that map a width of 3,806, so the chain itself would have to change, as it does at the 165‑bit level.

The accumulation matrix agrees about where the floor's cost begins. Of the 574 lattice rows shown at both floors, 154 take a larger matrix at 147 bits than at 128: BLAKE3 rows of the Akita variants, whose merges' many circuits make wide accumulators, and 144 Kishu rows of $2^{23}$ digits or more without zero knowledge, the longest accumulators of all. At 150 bits 26 more would move, each an unmasked matrix from degree 512 to 1,024: those of the Pasta-claims rows with 64‑bit commitments, and 24 of Kishu's rows without zero knowledge, from a step of $1.375 \times 2^{23}$ digits. Kishu's masked matrices are the same at 147 and 150 bits.

The planner does not give the prover's rows term or the verifier's inner-image share. For those the model takes the weakest Module-SIS instance of each of the 115 schedules in the shipped catalog's security report and, for each, the smallest module rank that covers the instance's width at both floors. With the catalog's own mix of moduli the module ranks sum to 121 at 128 bits and to 150 at 147 bits, a factor of 1.24, with 29 instances moving up a module rank. Over a 64‑bit modulus, where the module ranks are higher to begin with, the sums are 185 and 211, a factor of 1.14, with 26 moving. Over Pallas the instances sit at the width cap at both floors but one: the sums are 117 and 118, a factor of 1.01, with 1 moving.

Where the floor binds, the maximum secure widths shrink several-fold. At 147 bits the median shrink, over the cells where neither width is at the estimator's search cap and the width falls, is a factor of 2 to 3 at ring dimensions up to 32, 4 to 10 at 64, 4 to 13 at 128 and 256, and 6 to 15 at 512 and above, with the 32-bit modulus at the low end of each range. The few cells at the largest dimensions whose 147-bit width is still finite shrink about 36-fold.

The hash-based rows at the higher floor

The floor moves the hash-based rows too, through $\lambda$ alone and independently of any lattice instance. WHIR's proofs, measured with the reference implementation at $2^{17}$ over both fields at rates 1/2 to 1/8, are 16 percent larger at 147 bits than at 128, and its verifier's hashes 12 percent more; ARC's openings per input, $2(\lambda - g)/\log_2(1/\rho)$, grow by 15 percent.
