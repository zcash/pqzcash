Post-Quantum Zcash

CAPSS

A third authorization method, which Zoodles does not adopt, is a SNARK-friendly post-quantum signature: one whose verification is cheap inside a proof because its hashing is a permutation that is cheap in the proof system at hand, rather than a bit-oriented hash. CAPSS [FR25] is a framework for such signatures, and the one suggested by Tachyon’s book. The signature is a zero-knowledge proof of knowledge of a preimage under a one-way function built by truncating the permutation, made with SmallWood [FR25a], a hash-based argument whose Merkle trees and transcript hashing use the same permutation. Its security rests on that permutation alone, modelled as ideal, plus the random-oracle heuristic for the sponge. The paper instantiates it with Anemoi, Griffin, Poseidon, and Rescue-Prime, but the framework takes any permutation; which one is a question for the proof system that verifies the signature.

The paper’s figures

Over a 256-bit field at 128 bits, the paper’s instances are between 9,504 and 25,824 bytes, with verification between 20,717 and 71,145 rank-1 constraints. Its headline instances are Anemoi’s, 12,640 bytes and 23,484 constraints at the default trade-off, with verification calling the permutation about 173 times and signing about 13,900. Over the 64-bit Goldilocks field, which is Kishu’s size, the implemented Anemoi instance signs in 101 ms and verifies in 1.4 ms on a desktop core at 9,715 bytes, against 2,460 ms of signing over a 254-bit field. The paper estimates the 64-bit verifier at 80,000 to 100,000 constraints, about 4 times the 256-bit field’s, since the smaller field needs more repetitions for the same soundness.

Anemoi is not the permutation to use. The algebraic FreeLunch attacks [BBL+24] and the Gröbner-basis cryptanalysis of Anemoi that followed [CR25] have cut its margin, and Griffin’s and Rescue-Prime’s with it, below the 128 bits the paper’s parameters claim. The paper says so and keeps its parameters for comparability. Its Poseidon instances, which the attacks do not reach, are the conservative ones: 19,616 bytes and 46,567 constraints at the default trade-off, with verification calling the permutation about 143 times at three widths and signing about 10,000.

An instance for Kishu

A SNARK-friendly signature’s advantage is in a prime-field arithmetization: there a CAPSS verifier is tens of thousands of multiplication gates, where a leanSPHINCS verifier’s 298 BLAKE3 or BLAKE2s compressions are millions. Kishu commits bits, which is what suits BLAKE2s to it, and a field wire costs it 22 digits, so the comparison has to be made in Kishu’s own units. The permutation that is cheap in Kishu is Poseidon over its 64-bit field, which the model already prices at 276 multiplications a permutation, each a committed wire. Taking the paper’s Poseidon call counts and its factor of 4 for the 64-bit field, a verifier is about 3.47 million digits, against 3.38 million for leanSPHINCS with BLAKE3 and 4.74 million with BLAKE2s. The paper’s own constraint estimate for the 64-bit field, at one wire a constraint, gives 2.2 million. So in Kishu the two are within a factor of two or so of each other, and nothing like the order of magnitude a prime-field circuit sees. These are estimates from the paper’s counts at the model’s per-permutation cost, not a row of the frontier page. A row would count the verifier’s calls at each width and add the field arithmetic of the PACS checks.

Signing is a SmallWood proof, about 40,000 permutations over the 64-bit field with Merkle trees of 2^10 to 2^14 leaves. At the page’s assumed rate for an MCU-class device that is about 1.1 s, and a few times that on a secure element. So a device can sign with it, where it could not make a Kishu leaf. The trees’ memory, 2^12 leaves of 32 bytes, is a secure element’s whole budget. The signature, about 20 kbytes, fits a QR channel at four or five times leanSPHINCS’s scanning time.

Against the other two methods

  • In the circuit. Parity with leanSPHINCS within a factor of two or so, on the estimates above, rather than a gain.
  • On the device. Seconds of signing at the MCU rate and tens of kilobytes of memory, against hashing alone and under a second of grinding for leanSPHINCS; still feasible, which a Kishu leaf is not.
  • The assumption. The hash preimage and leanSPHINCS rest on a bit-oriented hash. CAPSS rests on an arithmetization-oriented permutation, the class whose margins the algebraic attacks have cut and which the frontier page excludes from its default hash policy for future-proofing. Poseidon is the member with the most scrutiny, and a signature key can be rotated where a long-lived commitment cannot, which makes the policy’s case weaker here than for storage.
  • Ethereum. Ethereum’s post-quantum signature is leanSPHINCS, so the compatibility the second method may offer is not on this route.

So CAPSS is the method for a prime-field proof system, and for Kishu it is a second signature route with a different assumption and no clear gain over leanSPHINCS. It is not priced as a row; a row would be a day’s work if the comparison needed settling.
