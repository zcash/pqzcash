Post-Quantum Zcash

Hardware wallets

A hardware wallet can use either authorization method of this section on its own: the hash preimage as a Kishu leaf of the transaction’s proof, or a leanSPHINCS signature that the host verifies in its transaction. This page asks what the wallet has to compute and transfer for each, and what a channel allows. It was written for ZIP 2005’s Recovery Statement [ZIP-2005], whose Option A keeps qsk on a hardware wallet and has the networked host prove the rest (Recovery from Ironwood). It takes the device split from an analysis of FROST members recovering from a seed phrase. The costs are the same for a native Zoodles spend. The figures come from the PCS frontier page, which prices the wallet’s part as applications of its transaction measure.

The device split

The model to reason in makes the hardware wallets the only honest parties and treats every networked device as the adversary. A wallet holds the spending key sk, or with FROST its signing share and the common sk, and so qsk. It exports nk and qk and nothing else that is derived from the secret. ak is derived from sk and exported by a single-signer wallet; with FROST it is the output of the distributed key generation. rivk_ext and the incoming and outgoing viewing keys follow from those, so the host can scan, build transactions and produce the main Recovery proof, whose witness is viewing-key material plus the note and its path. ZIP 2005’s other path, use_qsk=false, has the Recovery Statement prove the key derivations from sk itself. The priced circuit takes either value; this page follows the use_qsk=true path, the one whose costs a hardware wallet is meant to optimize.

The wallet signs FROST shares and makes SoKqsk, each after verifying the transaction and asking the user, and the user’s confirmation is the adversary’s choice of message. Two adversary classes share one win condition: a consensus-valid recovery spend on an unapproved SigHash.

  • A pre-quantum adversary corrupts up to t−1 wallets. It is bounded by the unforgeability of re-randomized FROST.
  • A discrete-log-breaking adversary is given every host’s state and every network message, but no wallet. It is bounded by the unforgeability of SoKqsk as a signature scheme with key qk. That bound is 1-of-n, since every wallet holds the same qsk.

Option A is the statement that the second class learns nothing from a wallet but its interface’s outputs.

What the wallet can prove

Two shapes are priced. The wallet can prove the preimage statement as a leaf of the transaction’s own proof system, Kishu on the frontier page: one BLAKE3 compression, 11,200 committed digits, with the linking commitment of ZIP 2005’s hardware-wallet section left out for now. Or the wallet can sign with leanSPHINCS and leave the verification to the host, which proves the verifier inside its own transaction with the signature and the public key as witnesses, so the spends stay unlinkable.

A Kishu leaf

The handoff is the whole question. The host folds the wallet’s leaf into the transaction’s accumulator, and split accumulation needs the witness of whatever it folds. So the wallet hands over a masked witness: the response z=y+cs of the zero-knowledge note (the zk note), a Gaussian mask y added to the leaf’s digits s under a challenge c. Rejection sampling makes the accepted z independent of the secret. The mask covers the leaf’s 2rank digits alone, since the wallet hands over no accumulator of its own. The transaction measure charges the user’s handoff the host’s merge that folds the leaf, and a small merge of the handoff’s own: the challenge derivation, the commitment identity, and the claim fold. The decomposition and the range and norm proofs are deferred to the host (the zk note’s §5.8). For a one-compression leaf the host’s merge is most of the cost. The measure’s 0.0266 s at rank 16 is 88 percent merge and 12 percent fold, and the circuit itself is a few percent.

The wallet need not prove that fold. The zk note’s §4.2 lists the handoff merge’s witness as simulatable: the hiding commitment, the mask’s commitment, the challenge, the claims and the obligation transcripts. So the host can derive the challenge, prove the fold’s merge and the obligations (the zk note’s §5.8 deferral), and verify the leaf’s sum-checks in its own merge. The wallet keeps what touches the secret. It commits the leaf with a hiding padding at the masked rank. It proves its constraint and Booleanity sum-checks under Libra-style masking polynomials, since the host sees their transcripts: 112 committed elements of Fq​. Each is committed in the widest digits that keep the vector’s matrix at the padding sweep’s rows, judged by the vector’s exact ℓ2​ norm: 33 balanced dibits at the 147-bit floor, and 22 tribits at 128. It samples and commits the mask in idle time, and computes the response with the rejection test.

The padding is computational rather than the zk note’s leftover-hash one. Aρ for ρ of 4,800 uniform bits at the 147-bit floor, the page’s default, or 4,400 at 128, is pseudorandom if knapsack-LWE is hard at the commitment’s rows. By the Micciancio–Mol duality that is LWE of the padding’s bits less the rows, with the rows as samples and binary secret and error at the 64-bit modulus (the zk note’s §5.10 gives the argument). The lattice estimator puts the cheapest attack on the padding at the matrix’s 1,024 rows at 149 and 128 quantum bits at the two floors’ paddings, under the page’s cost model, a dual hybrid attack on the binary secret. The primal attacks are a hundred bits dearer, so a primal estimate alone would overstate the margin. The padding is the least on the sweep’s grid, 50 bits where the floors are crossed, that reaches the floor, so the hedge of the 147-bit floor applies to it too. At the zk note’s 1,385 rows 5,000 bits give 140 bits, and at 2,048 rows they would fall short of the 128-bit floor. The statistical padding would be 66 thousand bits, 5.9 times the statement.

So the wallet commits 19,700 digits. That is 0.223 ms of online work on one M4 core, under a tenth of the handoff fold that the measure charges the user, with 0.278 ms in idle time, and a response of 54 kbytes at 22 bits a coordinate. The device rates are assumptions, scaled from the hash rates that [Wam26]’s hardware-wallet benchmark measured on a Ledger Nano S+: 10 million word multiplications a second for an MCU-class device and 3 million for a secure element. At those rates the computation takes 0.067 and 0.22 s. The transfer of z at that device’s measured 21 kbytes a second over USB takes 2.6 s, or 0.93 s with a pipelined transport. No merge circuit and no accumulator sit on the device, and the witness is 2.9 kbytes packed at its digits’ widths. So a Kishu signature of knowledge is within reach of a high-end hardware wallet on a USB link, and the transfer is the larger part of it.

Over an animated QR code

An airgapped wallet of the Keystone kind exchanges data by animated QR codes, fountain-coded fragments read by a phone camera from the wallet’s screen. Keystone’s firmware sends fragments of 200 bytes at 5 frames a second, 1 kbyte a second. So the response takes 54 s with no frame missed, and the fountain code’s redundancy after missed frames can double or treble that. Denser codes would cut it: a fragment of 800 bytes at the same rate gives 14 s, and the format’s ceiling, a version-40 symbol holding about 2,100 bytes of fragment, 5.2 s. Whether a camera reads the denser codes reliably depends on the screen, the light, and the camera, and gets worse toward the ceiling. So the transfer is slow at today’s settings and within reach with denser codes on some devices. The device’s memory is the other constraint: it holds the mask, about 54 kbytes, over which the response is written in place, beside the witness. The response cannot be made much smaller, and the reason is not an encoding choice. The accepted z is a Gaussian of width σ on every coordinate of the leaf, about log2​(σ2πe​) bits of information each. It is secret-dependent, so it cannot be regenerated from a seed as the mask can. With no padding and no masking elements the leaf’s own 11,200 coordinates still make 29 kbytes. A succinct Kishu proof of the leaf in place of the response would be about 72 kbytes, whatever the rank from 14 up, since nearly all of it is the opening’s fixed parts, the compression levels and the terminal. A narrower mask saves two or three bits a coordinate at the price of idle-time attempts, and a lighter challenge set a fraction of a bit. The transfer cannot be avoided by moving the fold elsewhere: split accumulation needs the host to hold a witness of the wallet’s accumulator, and that witness is z. A wallet on today’s QR settings is better served by the signature of the next section.

A hash-based signature

leanSPHINCS fits the QR channel: a signature of 4,276 bytes with the pruned key, 6,332 with the layered forest key, or 7,740 with Ethereum’s provisional set [EIP-8288] [Lit26] is a few seconds of scanning. Its signing is hashing alone, measured on a Nano S+ by [Wam26]’s benchmark. The pruned key’s 16,384 grinding digests per signature take 2.8 s with Keccak on that device and 0.50 s with BLAKE2s; BLAKE3, with seven rounds to BLAKE2s’s ten, was not in the benchmark and should be no slower. The grind is the wallet’s: it is keyed, and the key cannot leave the wallet without revealing which leaves it kept. A full tree can take the host’s help instead. The wallet produces the leaf hashes at key generation, the host builds and stores the tree and supplies the authentication paths, and signing then has no grind.

leanSPHINCS is stateless and deterministic, which matters when every member’s wallet holds the same seed: a stateful scheme’s index would be reused across wallets, and the deterministic randomizer makes the members’ signatures identical. The verifier’s cost in the host’s transaction is on the leanSPHINCS page.
