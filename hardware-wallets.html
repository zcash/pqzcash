Post-Quantum Zcash

Hardware wallets and the recovery statement

ZIP 2005’s Recovery Statement needs two authorizations of a recovery spend [ZIP-2005]. One is a RedDSA signature under the randomized key, which FROST makes t-of-n. The other is SoKqsk: a signature of knowledge of the quantum spending key qsk on the transaction’s SigHash, with Hqk(qsk)=qk. The ZIP’s Option A keeps qsk on a hardware wallet, which makes SoKqsk there, and the networked host proves the rest of the statement. This page asks what the wallet can prove and at what cost, and how the signature of knowledge is argued. The figures come from the PCS frontier page, which prices the wallet’s part as applications of its transaction measure. The page was written on 2026-11-21 alongside an analysis of FROST members recovering from a seed phrase, from which it takes the device split.

1. The device split

The model to reason in makes the hardware wallets the only honest parties and treats every networked device as the adversary. A wallet holds the spending key sk, or with FROST its signing share and the common sk, and so qsk. It exports nk and qk and nothing else that is derived from the secret. ak is derived from sk and exported by a single-signer wallet; with FROST it is the output of the distributed key generation. rivk_ext and the incoming and outgoing viewing keys follow from those, so the host can scan, build transactions and produce the main Recovery proof, whose witness is viewing-key material plus the note and its path. ZIP 2005’s other path, use_qsk=false, has the Recovery Statement prove the key derivations from sk itself. It is not considered here, since use_qsk=true is the path whose costs a hardware wallet is meant to optimize.

The wallet signs FROST shares and makes SoKqsk, each after verifying the transaction and asking the user, and the user’s confirmation is the adversary’s choice of message. Two adversary classes share one win condition: a consensus-valid recovery spend on an unapproved SigHash.

  • A pre-quantum adversary corrupts up to t−1 wallets. It is bounded by the unforgeability of re-randomized FROST.
  • A discrete-log-breaking adversary is given every host’s state and every network message, but no wallet. It is bounded by the unforgeability of SoKqsk as a signature scheme with key qk. That bound is 1-of-n, since every wallet holds the same qsk.

Option A is the statement that the second class learns nothing from a wallet but its interface’s outputs.

2. What the wallet can prove

Two shapes of SoKqsk are priced. The wallet can prove its statement as a leaf of the transaction’s own proof system, Kishu on the frontier page. That statement is one BLAKE3 compression, 15,100 committed digits, with the linking commitment of ZIP 2005’s hardware-wallet section left out for now. Or the wallet can sign SigHash with a stateless hash-based signature and leave the verification to the host. The host then proves the verifier inside its own transaction, with the signature and the public key as witnesses, so recovery spends stay unlinkable.

2.1 A Kishu leaf

The handoff is the whole question. The host folds the wallet’s leaf into the transaction’s accumulator, and split accumulation needs the witness of whatever it folds. So the wallet hands over a masked witness: the response z=y+cs of the zero-knowledge note, a Gaussian mask y added to the leaf’s digits s under a challenge c. Rejection sampling makes the accepted z independent of the secret. The mask covers the leaf’s 2rank digits alone, since the wallet hands over no accumulator of its own. The transaction measure charges the user’s handoff the host’s merge that folds the leaf, and a small merge of the handoff’s own: the challenge derivation, the commitment identity, and the claim fold, the decomposition and the range and norm proofs being deferred to the host (the note’s §5.8). For a one-compression leaf the host’s merge is most of the cost. The measure’s 0.0456 s at rank 16 is 83 percent merge and 11 percent fold, and the circuit itself is a few percent.

The wallet need not prove that fold. The note’s §4.2 lists the handoff merge’s witness as simulatable: the hiding commitment, the mask’s commitment, the challenge, the claims and the obligation transcripts. So the host can derive the challenge, prove the fold’s merge and the obligations (the note’s §5.8 deferral), and verify the leaf’s sum-checks in its own merge. The wallet keeps what touches the secret. It commits the leaf with a hiding padding at the masked rank. It proves its constraint and Booleanity sum-checks under Libra-style masking polynomials, 120 committed elements, since the host sees their transcripts. It samples and commits the mask in idle time, and computes the response with the rejection test.

The padding is computational rather than the note’s leftover-hash one. Aρ for ρ of 4,800 uniform bits at the 147-bit floor, the page’s default, or 4,400 at 128, is pseudorandom if knapsack-LWE is hard at the commitment’s rows, which by the Micciancio–Mol duality is LWE of the padding’s bits less the rows, with the rows as samples and binary secret and error at the 64-bit modulus (the note’s §5.10 gives the argument). The lattice estimator puts the cheapest attack on the padding at the matrix’s 1,024 rows at 149 and 128 quantum bits at the two floors’ paddings, under the page’s cost model, a dual hybrid attack on the binary secret; the primal attacks are a hundred bits dearer, so a primal estimate alone would overstate the margin. The padding is the least on the sweep’s grid, 50 bits where the floors are crossed, that reaches the floor, so the hedge of the 147-bit floor applies to it too. At the zero-knowledge note’s 1,385 rows 5,000 bits give 140 bits, and at 2,048 rows they would fall short of the 128-bit floor. The statistical padding would be 66 thousand bits, 4.4 times the statement.

So the wallet commits 27,600 digits. That is 0.256 ms of online work on one M4 core, under a tenth of the handoff fold that the measure charges the user, with 0.389 ms in idle time, and a response of 76 kbytes at 22 bits a coordinate. The device rates are assumptions, scaled from the hash rates that [Wam26]’s hardware-wallet benchmark measured on a Ledger Nano S+: 10 million word multiplications a second for an MCU-class device and 3 million for a secure element. At those rates the computation takes 0.077 and 0.26 s. The transfer of z at that device’s measured 21 kbytes a second over USB takes 3.6 s, or 1.3 s with a pipelined transport. No merge circuit and no accumulator sit on the device, and the witness is 13 kbytes as bits. So a Kishu signature of knowledge is within reach of a high-end hardware wallet on a USB link, and the transfer is the larger part of it.

2.1.1 Why this is not feasible for an animated QR code

An airgapped wallet of the Keystone kind exchanges data by animated QR codes: fountain-coded fragments of a few hundred bytes a frame, at a few frames a second, read by a phone camera from the wallet’s screen. That is a few kilobytes a second at best. The response does not fit that channel, and the reason is not an encoding choice. The accepted z is a Gaussian of width σ on every coordinate of the leaf, about log2​(σ2πe​) bits of information each. It is secret-dependent, so it cannot be regenerated from a seed as the mask can. With no padding and no masking elements the leaf’s own 15,100 coordinates still make 40 kbytes. A succinct Kishu proof of the leaf in place of the response would be about 72 kbytes, whatever the rank from 14 up, since nearly all of it is the opening’s fixed parts, the compression levels and the terminal. A narrower mask saves two or three bits a coordinate at the price of idle-time attempts, and a lighter challenge set a fraction of a bit. The transfer cannot be avoided by moving the fold elsewhere: split accumulation needs the host to hold a witness of the wallet’s accumulator, and that witness is z. So a Kishu-style proof cannot be made small enough for a QR channel. A wallet on such a channel needs the signature of §2.2.

2.2 A hash-based signature

leanSPHINCS, the stateless hash-based signature of post-quantum Ethereum [EIP-8288] [Lit26], fits the QR channel. A signature is 4,276 bytes with [Wam26]’s pruned key of lifetime 230, the configuration priced here, 6,332 with its layered forest key, and 7,740 with Ethereum’s provisional set: a few seconds of scanning. Its signing is hashing alone, measured on a Nano S+ by [Wam26]’s benchmark. The pruned key adds 16,384 grinding digests per signature, 2.8 s with Keccak on that device and 0.50 s with BLAKE2s, and its proved lifetime is 126,900 signatures, one per recovery transaction. The grind is the wallet’s: it is keyed, and the key cannot leave the wallet without revealing which leaves it kept. A full tree can take the host’s help instead. The wallet produces the leaf hashes at key generation, the host builds and stores the tree and supplies the authentication paths, and signing then has no grind.

leanSPHINCS is stateless and deterministic, which matters when every member’s wallet holds the same seed: a stateful scheme’s index would be reused across wallets, and the deterministic randomizer makes the members’ signatures identical.

The host proves the verifier inside its transaction: 298 BLAKE2s compressions or 262 Keccak-f permutations for the pruned key, against 462 and 408 for the layered one and 515 permutations for Ethereum’s. XOR is not free in a digit arithmetization, so in Kishu a Keccak-f permutation is 154,000 committed bits against 21,300 for a BLAKE2s compression. The verifier is therefore 6.39 million digits with BLAKE2s and 40.3 million with Keccak, 6.3 times as many, which is Ethereum’s open hash question seen from the circuit. In a Boolean circuit with XOR free the ratio is 3.3. With zero knowledge Kishu proves the BLAKE2s verifier in 0.297 s on one M4 core at rank 21 and the Keccak one in 1.37 s at rank 22, beside 0.370 s for the recovery circuit itself. Ethereum’s draft fixes Keccak-256 as the scheme’s only primitive; the circuit and the device both argue for BLAKE2s.

What this asks of ZIP 2005 is less than it first seems. Its qk=Hqk(qsk) hashes the secret, and a hash-based signature needs qk to commit to the signature’s public key instead. Deriving that key from the seed inside the Recovery Statement would cost the top layer’s key generation, out of the question. But the derivation is domain-separated. A signature route would define its own derivation of qk from the public key under another context, and the Recovery Statement, which is not yet specified, would accept it as one more arm of its tagged union beside the preimage proof and SoKsk. The arms cannot satisfy each other’s rule, since that would be a collision across two domain-separated hashes. So nothing has to be decided before the scheme is known. The one consequence is that a key derived before the signature route exists is a preimage-route key. A wallet that wants the signature route later needs a new key, which means new addresses and moved funds.

3. The signature of knowledge

A proof of knowledge of a preimage of qk is not enough. It would verify under any transaction, so it could be replayed; the statement has to be signed. The reason goes deeper than replay. The reduction that proves SoKqsk unforgeable, as a signature scheme with key qk, has to answer signing queries without qsk. So it simulates them, and it must then extract a preimage from a forger who has seen simulated proofs. That is simulation extractability, which plain knowledge soundness does not give. A signature of knowledge is by definition a simulation-extractable proof with the message as its label [CL06].

3.1 The folklore technique, and where it is proved

The technique is to bind the message into the Fiat–Shamir transcript from the start. The instance and the message go into the hash before the first challenge, and the chain of challenges binds everything after. Classically this is proved. For sigma protocols it is [FKMV12]. For multi-round sum-check protocols it is recent. [DG23] shows Spartan and Bulletproofs simulation-extractable with no change. [FFKRZ23] and [KPT23] prove it for Fiat–Shamir of a polynomial IOP with a binding commitment in general, under the conditions one expects: the instance hashed first, and a unique-response or state-restoration property of the protocol. The Frozen Heart vulnerabilities of 2022 [TOB22], forgeries against implementations that left the instance out of the hash, are what omitting it costs. The technique is free.

What is missing is the quantum random-oracle model, which the Recovery Protocol needs. Fiat–Shamir of sigma protocols is settled there, by [DFMS19] and [LZ19]. Knowledge soundness of Fiat–Shamir on an IOP is settled with polynomial loss, by [CMS19]. But the multi-round measure-and-reprogram bound [DFM20] loses a factor exponential in the number of rounds, which for thirty sum-check rounds says nothing. And the simulation-extractability proofs above rewind, which the quantum setting does not allow. We know of no simulation-extractability result for a sum-check-based succinct argument in the quantum random-oracle model with usable bounds. In the trust-boundary style of the Ironwood formalization, the signature-of-knowledge property would have to be a named hypothesis.

3.2 A route that is available: the non-injectivity of the hash

A classical argument avoids simulation extractability altogether, and it fits this statement unusually well. Hqk maps 256 bits to 256 bits, so a uniformly random qsk has a second preimage with probability about 1−1/e, 63 percent. The reduction to the collision resistance of Hqk picks qsk itself, answers every signing query with the real prover, and extracts a preimage from the forgery. A zero-knowledge proof is witness indistinguishable [FS90]. So when a second preimage exists, the forger’s view is the same whichever preimage the reduction holds, and the extracted one differs from the reduction’s with probability about a half. Then the reduction holds a collision. The loss is a factor of about 3.2. This is the two-witness form that [KW03] used for tight signature reductions, with the hash’s own non-injectivity standing in for the second key, so the circuit does not change. It needs zero knowledge and knowledge soundness with auxiliary input, the honest signatures on other messages being that input. Both are available in the quantum random-oracle model for the proof systems in play, and the message is still bound into the instance before the first challenge. Nothing in the protocol changes; only the proof does. This is the proof to write for the Kishu route. The hash-based signature route does not raise the question. leanSPHINCS is a signature scheme with its own strong unforgeability proof, 127 classical bits in the random-oracle model [Wam26], and the message enters its digest with the randomizer.
