Post-Quantum Zcash

Kishu’s arithmetization

Kishu is a proof-carrying-data scheme in Ragu’s shape. An application is a tree of nodes. Each node is a circuit that takes the public headers of its input nodes, checks whatever the application needs, and publishes a header of its own. The scheme attests that every node in the tree was satisfied, so that a verifier checks one proof for the whole tree. What differs from Ragu is what a node’s circuit is written over. This page describes a node’s circuit as its writer sees it, then the pieces Kishu is built from, the costs the frontier page prices it by, and the differences from Ragu’s arithmetization. Every figure is the model’s (Regenerating the Study’s Numbers).

Kishu as a Proof-Carrying-Data scheme

A node’s circuit has:

  • a witness, the values its prover knows;
  • a header, the values the node publishes, which the scheme carries to the node above; and
  • the headers of its input nodes, which the scheme attests.

The circuit’s writer states constraints over these three, and the scheme guarantees that a proof of the node exists only if the constraints hold and the input nodes were proved. A transaction is formed from a tree of nodes, as in Tachyon: a node for each spend and each output, nodes that combine them, and a node that fixes the fee, with the root’s proof the transaction’s. (For efficiency we distinguish nodes in the PCD graph from steps of the accumulation scheme, discussed below: a step is what the prover commits at once, and several nodes can share one.)

The arithmetization

Let q be a fixed prime; q=264−59 for the modelled parameters. Write ⟨x,y⟩=xTy for the inner product of two column vectors over the field Fq​. A matrix in Fqr×c​ has r rows and c columns, so that premultiplying by it takes a column vector in Fqc​ to one in Fqr​.

Consider a Kishu circuit over Fq​ with m constraints. The public inputs, padded to length m with zeros, form the instance vector v∈Fqm​. A witness is a vector of N small integers, w∈ZN, read as elements of Fq​. The circuit fixes each position’s alphabet: a bit, in {0,1}; a dibit, in {−2,−1,0,1}; or a tribit, in {−4,…,3}, Akita’s own balanced base-8 digit. The entries are called digits.

The circuit is defined by, for each constraint index j,

  • fixed (typically sparse) coefficient matrices Aj​,Bj​∈Fqtj​×N​, where tj​≥0; and
  • a fixed coefficient row vector cj​∈Fq1×N​.

The constraint at index j requires this equality in Fq​:

⟨Aj​w,Bj​w⟩+cj​w=vj​.

In words: each row of Aj​ and the matching row of Bj​ give two linear combinations of the digits in Fq​, which are multiplied. These products are added up over the tj​ rows, together with one more linear combination of the digits given by cj​. The total must equal the constraint’s entry of v. So a constraint is a polynomial of degree at most two in the digits, with no constant term, set equal to zero or to a public input. Leaving out the constant term loses no generality: if one public input is the constant 1 and one witness bit is constrained to equal it, a constant term in any constraint is a multiple of that bit.

That every entry of w lies in its alphabet is enforced for the whole witness at once by a range check, not by a constraint per digit. For the bit positions it is the Booleanity check.

Relation to R1CS

This is a generalization of R1CS over Fq​ with several products allowed in one constraint. The digits’ range constraints are implicit.

Relation to CCS

CCS [STW23] writes a whole constraint system as one vector equation over z=(w,1,x), the witness, the constant 1, and the public inputs:

i∑​ci​⋅◯j∈Si​​Mj​z=0.

The Mj​ are fixed matrices, each Si​ is a multiset of their indices of size at most the degree d, ◯ is the Hadamard (entrywise) product between vectors, and the ci​ are constants.

Read row by row at degree two, that is the form above. Each Si​ then names one or two matrices. For a pair, their rows at a constraint, the first scaled by ci​, are a row of Aj​ and the matching row of Bj​. The single matrices, scaled, sum to cj​. A tj​ that varies with j is the same as padding Aj​ and Bj​ with zero rows up to a fixed number.

CCS puts the constant 1 and the public inputs inside z, where they can enter the products. Here the witness is small digits and the public inputs stand on the right-hand side. A constant or an input that a product needs is first copied into witness digits by a linear constraint. CCS allows degrees above two; here the degree is two.

Relation to Universal Constraint Systems

Universal Constraint Systems [AGK+26] are the constraint systems of Zinc+, over polynomial rings in general. Over the integers they are the constraint systems of Zinc [GWHD25]: CCS on a witness of bounded integers, with lifted moduli. The relations are proved by projecting them to a random prime field for the PIOP, so that one constraint system serves any moduli. That is what makes them universal. BitZ [BGK+26] proves integer polynomial constraints the same way.

Kishu keeps the shape of the constraints and the small integers as witness, but evaluates the constraints in Fq​ itself. Its fingerprint route is Zinc’s method, the relations checked in instances over random primes; its limb route is not, holding the relations over the integers by keeping the left-hand side of each constraint within a range no larger than q.

Relation to Ragu

Ragu’s arithmetization is the constraint system of Bootle et al. [BCC+16], over the Pallas base field and with a fourth wire added to each gate. A circuit of n gates has four wires a gate, each gate enforcing ai​bi​=ci​ and ci​di​=0, and 4n linear constraints, each a fixed linear combination of all the wires equal to an instance value (Ragu’s arithmetization chapter).

Kishu’s wires are digits of one to three bits rather than field elements, so a boolean value needs no gate. Constraints are over the 64-bit Fq​, not a Pallas-sized field. Its constraints are not gates of a fixed shape with a fixed number of linear constraints, but quadratic constraints with any number of products each, as above. In both Ragu and Kishu, the public inputs enter as instance values.

Writing circuits

The writer pays per digit of witness, per product, and a little per nonzero coefficient — less for a small one, such as the powers of two that assemble a word from its digits. A sum-check could take higher degrees at a cost per degree, but the model charges degree two. Fusing Keccak’s steps into cubic constraints was found not to pay; a term of higher degree costs an intermediate witness value instead.

A digit’s width is the writer’s choice, position by position. A value that is a factor of a product needs the width the product needs: a bit, in the hashes. A value that enters constraints only through linear combinations is written in tribits. That is any integer word: a limb, a quotient, a carry, an amount, or an element of Fq​. A dibit or a bit takes the remainder if the width is not a multiple of three. The commitment, the fold, and the mask cost the same per digit at any of these widths. The range check costs one product a bit, two a dibit, and four a tribit, by Akita’s paired predicate. So a tribit holds three bits for a little over a third of their cost.

A word of ℓ<64 bits is a linear combination of its digits plus a constant, which costs nothing to form. The constant offsets the balanced digits. Bit vectors of any fixed length can be represented directly, as can elements of Fq​ (non-canonically, in 22 digits). A range check is free: a value held in digits of ℓ bits in all is below 2ℓ by construction.

Since a word is a linear combination in certified digits, a relation holds over the integers whenever it holds in Fq​, provided that its two sides, as integers, both lie in a range no larger than q, so that they cannot differ by a multiple of q. That is how integer arithmetic is written. Adding a value in [0,u] to one in [0,v] is safe whenever u+v<q, for example. A 32-bit addition is a+b=s+232c with the sum and the carry committed, and a comparison is a subtraction’s carry. A multiplication of two words costs one product term; a constant costs by its nonzeros.

Arithmetic in a prime field other than Fq​, such as Pasta’s base field, is a relation with a committed quotient. Each multiplication carries a quotient and carries, which Kishu commits as tribits. An R1CS emulation of the same arithmetic pays mostly for the boolean constraints on the bits of those values, which Kishu’s digits do not need, so in Kishu’s arithmetization it is expensive but not prohibitively so.

Bit operations are a term each: XOR is z=x+y−2xy and AND is z=xy; rotations and shifts are wiring and cost nothing. A result needs committing only where it is a factor of a later product. A result used linearly, as an addend or as a hash’s output, stays an expression inside the constraint that uses it. In a chain of XORs every other word is committed, each certified against both XORs’ inputs at once at two terms a bit. A third XOR in a chain would need degree three. Bitwise operations keep bits. A dibit XOR is degree two only with a committed quotient digit, which is pinned only by committing the reduced result. Tribits have no degree-two XOR at all. So a BLAKE3 compression is 11,344 bits of witness and 11,120 terms, a multiplication in Fq​ is one term over 64-bit operands, and a Pasta multiplication is 170 or 374 digits by one of two routes. The circuits that suit Kishu are the hash-heavy ones.

A step is one or more circuits of 2rank digits, the rank chosen for the application between 13 and 24. The merge’s digits take the first part of the step, and the application’s nodes fill the rest, as many as fit, as the Zoodles page’s packing shows. Zero knowledge asks nothing of the circuit’s writer: the scheme hides the witness, and no randomizer or blinding wire is needed. Not available: lookup arguments, which the study does not model.

What Kishu is made of

Kishu is an arithmetization in BitZ’s style over Akita’s commitment scheme.

Akita is a lattice-based polynomial commitment scheme, secure under the Module-SIS assumption: it commits to a vector of small integers, its digits, by an Ajtai hash over a ring with a 64-bit modulus (its own digits are balanced base-8 digits, a few bits each), and it accumulates many such commitments into one by folding, so that a proof-carrying-data scheme can defer the expensive opening to the end.

BitZ is a hash-based commitment scheme whose witnesses are bit-decomposed: it commits the bits and proves an evaluation claim over any ring by lifting it to the integers.

Kishu takes BitZ’s view of the witness as small integers and the constraint style of Zinc+’s universal constraint systems. It commits the witness with Akita: bits where the circuit needs bits as factors, and Akita’s own base-8 digits for integer words. So the committed vector is the witness itself, the range check certifies it, and Akita’s accumulation replaces BitZ’s hashing-heavy proofs. A digit is therefore the unit in which this study counts a circuit’s size. The two arithmetizations are not the same. BitZ states integer relations and proves them by projecting to random prime fields. Kishu evaluates its constraints in Fq​, Akita’s commitment modulus. It reaches the integers either by keeping each constraint’s sides within a range no larger than q, the limb route, or by random-prime instances as BitZ does, the fingerprint route.

The piece that such a combination needs is a range check over the lattice’s own prime field, to certify that every digit lies in its alphabet. For the wider digits that is Akita’s own paired predicate, a sum-check of degree b/2 for an alphabet of b values. For the bits it is a Booleanity check, and an internal research draft of September 2026, provisional and unreviewed, supplies one. The committed table’s bits are certified by one unweighted quadratic identity plus an integer norm bound, which a structured random projection supplies by revealing 160 coordinates. So BitZ’s exponent-lift GKR, 80% of its prover, is not needed. The draft measures the reduction alone, single-threaded at 227 bits over 264−59. The projection prover takes 113 ms against 438 ms for the optimized conventional Booleanity sum-check, with verifiers of 0.85 and 0.023 ms and payloads of 2.6 kbytes and 1.7 kbytes. Neither includes the commitment opening or hiding. The projection form reveals coordinates of the committed table, so the rows that use it have no zero knowledge; the zero-knowledge rows use the sum-check form. The draft’s certificates are conditional and not yet formalized; Kishu’s Soundness Obligations states what has to be proved.

Beneath the step interface, Kishu accumulates as Ragu does: each step’s proof is folded into an accumulator rather than verified, and a step’s circuit is accompanied by a circuit that verifies the fold of its input proofs’ accumulators, the merge, written in the same arithmetization. The sections below price the arithmetization’s units, the merge, and the application circuits in them.

Digits and constraints

Kishu commits its digits over Akita’s 64-bit modulus, plus the range check. For a bit that is the Booleanity check at 2.5 or 9.8 word multiplications. A dibit or a tribit costs one or three products more at the same rate, charged with the application’s constraints. A step commits 2rank digits. Its constraints are charged as well as its digits: the constraint PIOP, a sum-check over the committed table, costs about 9.8 word multiplications per product term of a constraint, the measured rate of the Booleanity sum-check. A nonzero coefficient of a linear combination costs 2 for a small constant or 4 for a field constant, the combinations being over word entries. This is charged for the application’s constraints and the merge’s alike, and the PIOP’s transcript is absorbed and its verifier run in the merge above.

Its claims live in the 64-bit field itself, Akita’s commitment modulus, and the verifier recomposes each Pasta value from 32-bit limbs proved as linear claims in the same sum-check. So Kishu does not use the decoupled commitment, and its soundness rests on Akita’s own at the 64-bit profile and on the draft’s Booleanity certificate. Its opening proof and decider are priced on the Goldilocks variant’s plan and measurement, the same profile, at Kishu’s digit count.

Pasta multiplication by two routes

Akita’s claims are in one fixed field, so a Pasta multiplication a⋅b=k⋅p+r over committed digits cannot be checked modulo that field alone: the prover could choose r. Kishu’s rows therefore come in two routes, each a row. By the limb route the relation is checked coefficient by coefficient over 29-bit limbs, which are linear in the digits and cost nothing to commit. That is 17 constraints of up to nine product terms each, bounded below half the modulus so that they hold over the integers, with a committed carry of 34 bits each. A multiplication is then 374 digits and 98 product terms. By the fingerprint route the relation is one constraint in each of 3 PIOP instances over random 52-bit primes, as BitZ checks it, at 170 digits. The price is per step that has multiplications, so a step of hashes alone pays nothing and the routes tie on the path applications. Each instance’s evaluation claim becomes about 2rank​ integer column sums by BitZ’s tensor split, each exact as one claim in the 64-bit field and committed as a word of 61 bits, 21 digits. That is 91,287 digits a step at rank 21, with three more transcripts for the merge above. So the recovery circuit is 6.1 million digits by the limb route and 3.2 million by the fingerprint route. Its constraint PIOPs take 0.0135 and 0.00556 s on one core. The range checks of its tribits take 0.0509 and 0.0244 s more, beyond the one product a digit that every digit pays.

The merge

Kishu’s merge is Akita’s, costed in digits. A committed bit is one digit, so a BLAKE3 compression of the transcript is 11,216 digits and the images’ reductions one per bit. The BLAKE3 accounting is its seven rounds of eight G functions. Every word of a G function is used once as a factor, in an XOR, and once linearly, in an addition, and only a factor needs committing:

  • each 32-bit addition is one relation a+b=s+232c over word entries, with the 32 sum bits and the carry committed, two bits of carry where there are three addends;
  • of each chain of two XORs in a G function, the first’s word is left uncommitted: its bits are the expressions x+y−2xy inside the addition that takes the word, 32 terms there;
  • the second’s word is committed, each bit certified against both XORs’ inputs at two terms;
  • rotations are free, being wiring; and
  • the finalisation XORs two state words into each of the eight output words, and what is committed depends on what consumes them.

Where the output is the next compression’s chaining value, within one message, four of its words are that compression’s factors in its first round and are committed at one term a bit. The other four enter its first additions as expressions. That is 11,216 digits a compression.

Where the output is data, all eight words are committed at one term a bit. A Merkle node’s output and a hash call’s are message words of the next compression, addends in each of its seven rounds, and a transcript’s last output is a challenge. That is 128 digits more, 11,344. Either way a compression is 11,120 constraints, with 37,760 nonzeros where the output is data.

BLAKE3 splits a message into chunks of 1,024 bytes, each a chain of compressions over its 64-byte blocks, and joins the chunks’ outputs by a binary tree of parent compressions, one fewer than the chunks. Over a whole transcript the parents would be 6.25% more compressions, and that would compound, since the merge absorbs each step circuit’s claim and a dearer merge needs more circuits.

So the transcript is a Merkle–Damgård chain at the application level. Each link is a keyed BLAKE3 call on one 64-byte block of the transcript, whose key is the previous call’s output. Every call is BLAKE3 as specified, in its keyed mode, on a single block, so it is one compression, and the transcript takes exactly one compression a block. The key is the compression’s chaining value, so each output but the last is consumed as one, and the last is data.

BLAKE2b has the same G function at 64 bits, each addition in two halves since a 64-bit sum can wrap modulo q. Its finalisation differs: it XORs the old chaining word in with the two state words, a chain of two XORs. So all eight output words are committed, each bit certified at two terms. That gives BLAKE2b 38,528 digits and 38,272 constraints.

Under BLAKE3 every transcript element the prover supplies is first checked to be below the modulus, since a 64-bit decomposition is canonical only there, about 22 digits a word.

The merge’s arithmetic is over the 64-bit field and its quadratic extension, which is the PIOP’s own field. As in BitZ, the constraint PIOP runs over the ring itself, and only the final linear claim is proved on the bits. So a multiplication there is one constraint, with no bit decomposition and no quotient. But every wire is committed as tribits: 22 digits for a field element and 44 for a product in the extension. So the sum-check’s arithmetic is 400 such products, each circuit’s claim 4 and the Booleanity sum-check’s verifier about 3,000; the projection verifier scans 3.1 million sampled coefficients, a digit each. The transcript’s elements and each circuit’s claim are elements of the extension, 16 bytes, and the Booleanity sum-check’s own transcript is absorbed with them.

Under the Poseidon option the transcript is hashed by Poseidon over the 64-bit field at width 12 and rate 8 with the x3 S-box: 276 multiplications a permutation at 22 digits each. That is 54% of BLAKE3’s committed digits per absorbed byte, fewer because Poseidon’s wires are tribits where BLAKE3’s are bits. A permutation is 276 constraints with 13,800 nonzeros of field constants, where a BLAKE3 compression is 11,120 with 37,760 of small ones, and these are charged at the rates above. Per 64 bytes absorbed, commitments and constraints together, BLAKE3 costs 430,000 word multiplications and Poseidon 350,000, within 21% of each other, which many modelling choices could sway either way. So both hashes are kept as variants of Kishu on the frontier page, each a family of its own in the list and the plot. The BLAKE3 rows appear under either hash option and the Poseidon rows under the arithmetic-hash option, and the frontier decides between them.

The recovery circuit

The Ironwood recovery circuit itself is hash-heavy: its expensive parts are seven BLAKE2b-512 compressions (the cost section of [ZIP-2005]; beside two Sinsemilla commitments, two full-width Pallas scalar multiplications, a Merkle path, and a BLAKE3 compression in the quantum-key signature). In Kishu a bit-oriented hash is one bit per wire against many Pasta gates per round in the others, and the integer arithmetic simulates Fp​ without a bit decomposition per operation, with range checks free. A Sinsemilla chunk selects one of 1,024 generators by ten bits, each coordinate a fixed linear form in the subset products of the bits. Only a product that is a factor of another is committed: the 52 subsets within each half of the bits. The 961 subsets spanning both halves are terms of the two coordinate constraints. So a chunk is 222 digits with its coordinates. Costed component by component in this arithmetization, with a committed bit at one digit and a multiplication at 374 digits by the limb route or 170 by the fingerprint route, the recovery circuit is 6.1 million or 3.2 million digits. In Pasta arithmetic it is 570,000 gates. The frontier page’s transaction measure uses those counts together with the constraint PIOP’s work.

Differences from Ragu

Relation to Ragu above gives the differences in the arithmetization itself. Beyond it, Ragu commits a step’s trace as one structured polynomial of degree below 4n, by Pedersen, and checks the gates and constraints together by one revdot identity at random points, sound by Schwartz–Zippel in the field. A step of rank r commits 2r coefficients, so 2r−2 gates: at rank 13, Ragu’s production rank, 2,048 gates, which every one of Tachyon’s circuits fits. Kishu differs in these ways:

  • A value’s digits cost less than a coefficient. Ragu’s gate costs about one Pallas multiplication of Pedersen work per wire, about 5,600 word multiplications a coefficient at rank 13. Kishu’s digit costs 11.0 to commit, so a Pasta value’s 85 tribits cost 935, well under one coefficient.
  • Constraints are a sum-check, not a gate identity. Ragu’s constraints are the fixed gate shape and linear combinations, checked by one polynomial identity over the field. Kishu’s are a sum-check PIOP over the committed table, as in BitZ and Zinc+’s universal constraint systems, with integer relations projected to the 64-bit field or to random primes. A constraint is charged by its product terms and nonzeros rather than by gates.
  • Hashes are cheap and Pasta arithmetic is dear. In Ragu a Poseidon permutation over Pallas is about 192 gates and a Pasta multiplication one gate, while a bit-oriented hash would cost a gate per bit operation. In Kishu a BLAKE3 compression is 11,344 digits, one per bit, and a Pasta multiplication 170 or 374 digits by relation, since the field is not Pasta’s. That is why the recovery circuit is 3.2 million digits against 570,000 Pasta gates, and why Zoodles, with no Pasta arithmetic, suits it. Kishu is not specialized to Pasta at all. Given that, it does well on Pasta in prover time, as the frontier page’s transaction measure shows, and it would do as well on any large-field arithmetic.
  • The claim field is small. Ragu’s claims are Pallas elements; Kishu’s are elements of the 64-bit field’s quadratic extension, 16 bytes, so a Pasta value is proved through limbs or a fingerprint.
  • The step is larger and its size is a knob. Ragu’s step is fixed at 213 coefficients; Kishu’s is 2rank digits over a range of ranks, and one Kishu step holds several of Tachyon’s circuits, as the Zoodles page’s packing shows.
  • The merge verifies a lattice fold. Ragu’s merge circuits verify Pedersen-IPA accumulation with Poseidon transcripts over Pallas (the frontier page’s circuit budget); Kishu’s verify Akita’s fold, its sum-checks and images, with a BLAKE3 or Poseidon transcript in digits, as above.
  • Zero knowledge by masking. Ragu rerandomizes its proofs in two steps of their own; Kishu’s accumulation is made zero-knowledge by a masking fold with rejection sampling, paid once per transaction, as the zero-knowledge note describes.
