Post-Quantum Zcash

Akita proof sizes at a Pasta modulus: what can be computed now, and how

Written 2026-10-13. Companion to Zero knowledge for a lattice accumulation step. The question: what would an Akita opening proof cost if the base field were the Pallas base field (255 bits), as Tachyon and the Ironwood recovery circuit want, and what about Goldilocks. Akita’s planner cannot plan a schedule for a modulus that it does not know, so this note does two things. It re-prices every shipped schedule at the other field’s widths with Akita’s own byte formula. And it generates the SIS width tables that a real Pasta profile would need. The remaining work for a real profile is listed at the end.

1. What was built

All code lives in the scratch clone of LayerZero-Labs/akita at commit 60b82bc30f4f8f1130ccd71fe10b0b9bed909ab9 (2026-10-09, Apache-2.0 or MIT), under work/akita-src. The diff is small and self-contained:

  • akita-sis-estimator: two estimator-only moduli, pallas (the Pallas base field) and goldilocks (264−232+1), selectable in every example by name. They do not have any params profile, so the table generators emit CSV for them and the ℓ∞​ generator gives them the canonical role cells of the checked-in profiles as explicit origins. A small example, pasta_rank, binary-searches the least row count for a Euclidean bound at each modulus.
  • akita-params: NonterminalLevelLayout::with_field_widths, which re-prices a level’s atoms at other base-field and challenge-field widths without changing their counts.
  • akita-schedules: expanded_schedule_proof_breakdown, which splits a schedule’s proof estimate into non-terminal payloads, offloaded setup sum-checks, the terminal extension-opening reduction, the terminal response, and grinding nonces, at the schedule’s own widths or at a FieldWidthOverride. With no override its total equals the planner’s own estimate, and the example checks that on every row.
  • akita-schedules and akita-planner: a process-wide pricing width override, set_pricing_width_override, consulted by every byte estimate that the planner derives from a policy, and read by the artifact generator from AKITA_PRICING_WIDTHS. With it set, the planner selects schedules by their cost at the override widths (§3.1).
  • akita-planner: the example catalog_reprice, which loads every shipped schedule artifact, or those in a directory given by --artifacts, and prints the breakdown three times. Once at the schedule’s own modulus, once at Pallas widths (255-bit base and challenge field, no extension), and once at Goldilocks widths (64-bit base field, degree-2 challenge extension, as the fp64 presets use).
  • work/akita-pasta-estimates.sh: builds, runs the re-pricing and the rank ladder, then generates the full Euclidean and ℓ∞​ width tables for both new moduli as CSV. Outputs land in work/akita-pasta-out, one log per step and a summary with timings.

Reproduce the re-pricing alone with:

cd work/akita-src
RUSTUP_TOOLCHAIN=1.96.0 cargo run --release -p akita-planner --features catalog-gen --example catalog_reprice

2. What re-pricing means

The planner’s proof estimate is a byte formula over a schedule’s geometry (crates/akita-params/src/proof_size.rs and crates/akita-schedules/src/runtime.rs). Per non-terminal level it sums opening payloads and compressed images in base-field atoms, the range-tree, norm, and fused sum-checks in challenge-field atoms, and the next level’s compressed payload. The terminal adds the clear partials in base-field atoms and the digit-valued folded response at a variable width, and grinding nonces are priced separately.

Re-pricing keeps everything about the schedule fixed: fold levels, witness lengths, digit bases, sum-check shapes, terminal shape, nonces. It changes only the width of a base-field atom and of a challenge-field atom, and the claim extension degree used for the extension-opening reduction. So it prices “this schedule, serialized over that field”. It is not the schedule that the planner would choose for that field. Two effects go the other way:

  • Pallas has 85 base-8 digits per coefficient against 43 for the 128-bit field, so a natively planned Pasta witness is longer at every level, which adds sum-check rounds and possibly a level. This pushes the real figure up.
  • The planner would re-optimize levels, block counts, and the terminal shape for 32-byte atoms. This can push it down, and §3.1 measures it: about 6%.

The first effect cannot be quantified without a Pasta profile, which is the remaining work of §6. The re-pricing is the part of the answer that follows from the byte formula alone.

3.1 Letting the planner re-plan at Pasta widths

The second native-planning effect of §2 can be measured without a Pasta profile. The planner selects schedules by their estimated bytes, and every byte estimate that it derives from a policy passes through a handful of functions in akita-schedules. A process-wide pricing override, set by AKITA_PRICING_WIDTHS=255:255:1 on the artifact generator, makes those functions price at Pasta widths while geometry, SIS sizing, digit bases, and validation stay the fp128 policy’s. The planner then chooses the schedule that is cheapest at 32-byte atoms. This was run over all seventeen fp128 families, 79 rows at about five seconds a row. A no-override run first confirmed that the generator reproduces the shipped catalog byte for byte.

The planner’s move is one fold level fewer, not one more. At 214 it goes from five levels to four, which cuts the non-terminal payloads from 27.4 kbytes to 17.7 kbytes and accepts a terminal of 83.4 kbytes instead of 79.6 kbytes. With sum-check messages as wide as partials, a level of sum-checks no longer pays for the partials it removes. Savings across the fp128 rows run from 1.3% to 7.5%, and the small one-hot rows with two levels do not change at all. So re-planning takes back about 6 kbytes of the 42, and a Ragu-sized opening stays around 100 to 106 kbytes on this accounting.

The earlier estimate of 15 kbytes for the move to a 255-bit field accounted for the terminal partials at a 64-bit base field and nothing else. From the 128-bit field, the partials alone add 28.7 kbytes, and the sum-checks add about the same again, so the same-geometry figure is about 42 kbytes above fp128, roughly 1.65 times. A Ragu-sized opening, 214 to 216 dense coefficients, is therefore around 105 to 115 kbytes on this accounting, before the two native-planning effects of §2.

Goldilocks does not need any new work for proof sizes. Re-pricing the fp64 rows at Goldilocks widths reproduces the fp64 figures to the byte, because the widths and the extension degree are the same. The SIS estimator gives the same ranks as the 64-bit representative at every bound tried, since the two moduli differ by one part in 232 in log2​q. What Goldilocks changes is runtime only: its 2-adicity makes Xd+1 split completely, the same challenge-set question that Pasta raises in §5.4 of the zero-knowledge note.

4. SIS tables for the new moduli

The 2026-10-14 run of work/akita-pasta-estimates.sh completed every step; its outputs are in estimates/ beside this note, with summary.txt giving each step’s status and duration. The Euclidean width tables for both moduli, 16,800 rows, took under a second. The ℓ∞​ tables, 9,480 rows each over the canonical role cells, took 2.4 hours for Pallas and 1.1 for Goldilocks. At Pallas 8,963 of the 9,480 cells are secure up to the search cap, against 7,243 at Goldilocks, because a single ring row at 255 bits already carries most of the bits that a cell needs. Three readings from the tables:

  • Per ℓ∞​ cell, the least rank that is secure at any width carries about the same number of bits at both moduli, as §5.2 of the zero-knowledge note predicts, with Pallas coarser. At ring dimension 128 and coefficient bound 7, Pallas needs rank 1, 128 rows and 32.5K bits, where Goldilocks needs rank 4, 512 rows and 32.8K bits. At ring dimension 64 and the same bound, Pallas needs rank 2 (32.5K bits) where Goldilocks needs rank 7 (28.7K bits). The whole-row granularity costs Pallas 13% there, so a native Pasta profile would prefer the smaller ring dimensions.

  • At a Euclidean collision bound of 222 and ring dimension 64, one ring row at Pallas (64 rows, 16,256 bits) secures widths up to 14 ring elements, and rank 2 secures any width. Goldilocks needs rank 3 at that bound. Same bits, as expected.

  • For the ℓ∞​ bound 7 at ring dimension 64, rank 1 at Pallas secures any practical width, where Goldilocks rank 1 stops at width 1,380. Pallas commitments therefore come in coarser steps: one ring row is 2 kbytes at dimension 64, and the planner would pick smaller ring dimensions to get finer steps.

The same run also re-planned all seventeen fp128 families under the decoupled pricing of §6.3: across the 79 rows the planner improves 49 and leaves the rest, saving 0 to 1.7 kbytes per row and 0.7 kbytes on average. The shipped schedules are already close to the planner’s choice under that pricing, so the §6.3 figures stand. The shipped catalog’s own security check passed as the last step.

5. What this does not tell us

  • The native Pasta schedule’s reaction to the doubled digit count, the first effect of §2. The planner’s reaction to the atom widths is measured in §3.1 and is small, so my expectation is a modest increase over the re-planned figure.
  • Verifier or prover time. The formula counts bytes only.
  • The per-merge cost in Ragu, which the planner does not model; §8 uses one of Akita’s own fold levels as a proxy.

6. The tail, and decoupling the commitment modulus

6.1 What the terminal reveals

Akita’s terminal is already LaBRADOR’s folded tail (paper §8.2). The last committed fold binds, as its outgoing message, the inner images ti​=Aterm​si​ of the terminal’s B witness blocks. The terminal then reveals the partial evaluations ei​, grinds a nonce, and reveals the folded response z=∑i​ci​si​. The verifier checks three relations directly: Aterm​z=∑i​ci​ti​ in the ring, ∑i​ci​ei​=a⊤Gz for fold consistency, and the trace relation that ties the ei​ to the claimed value. One block of t and of e is recomputed from the others and checked against a digest. No outer commitment, range proof, or relation sum-check is run.

So the clear message per block is (nA​+1)dA​ field elements: nA​dA​ coordinates of ti​ and dA​ of ei​. In the shipped 214 dense schedule that is 1,344 coordinates of t against 448 of e (seven blocks, rank 3, ring dimension 64), plus 16,384 digit-valued response coordinates at about 1.4 bytes each. The inner images, not the partials, are three quarters of the clear field elements. Folding them away means one more committed level, which the planner priced and rejected. At Pasta a field element costs 32 bytes revealed, or about 85 three-bit digits carried, and folding compresses those digits by only the block count. Revealing wins, which is why the planner stops where it does.

6.2 Inner images are SIS-sized, not field-sized

The ti​ are commitment coordinates. Their size in bits is set by Module-SIS, and §5.5 showed that the bits are independent of the modulus. A native Pasta plan therefore needs about half the rows at the terminal for the same bound, and the t bytes stay near their fp128 size instead of doubling. The re-pricing of §3 doubled them. Pricing the inner images and the compressed payloads at 128 bits, with partials and sum-check messages at 255, approximates a native plan’s t without changing the geometry:

6.3 Decoupling the commitment modulus from the claim field

The same observation goes further. Nothing in the commitments needs the modulus to be the claim field. Binding needs Module-SIS modulo some qc​, and the ring relations modulo qc​ can be checked over Fp​ with an explicit quotient. The identity Az−∑i​ci​ti​=qc​k holds over the integers, with ∣k∣ around 231 for Ragu-sized witnesses. An Fp​ identity among integers of size 286 cannot wrap, so checking it modulo p checks it over the integers. The quotient k joins the witness as eleven base-8 digits per relation coordinate, a few thousand digits per level, in the same range check as everything else. Akita already carries quotient witnesses for the reduction modulo Xd+1, so this is the same kind of object.

With qc​=264−59, Akita’s own 64-bit modulus:

  • inner images are revealed at 8 bytes and carried as 22 digits plus the quotient, instead of 32 bytes and 85 digits;
  • compressed images, outer commitments, and compression chains are the shipped 64-bit objects, with the shipped SIS tables, compression plans, ring dimensions, and challenge families, which also removes the fully-splitting-ring question for the challenge sets;
  • partials, sum-check messages, and claim values stay at 32 bytes, since they are Fp​ values;
  • the response z is unchanged;
  • the merge circuit absorbs 64-bit commitment coordinates, three to a Pasta element.

Priced with partials and sum-checks at 255 bits and commitment coordinates at 64, on the shipped geometry and after re-planning under that pricing (AKITA_PRICING_WIDTHS=255:255:1:64, which also routes the commitment width into the planner’s terminal pricing):

One-hot polynomials of 212 to 215 entries come to 40,375 bytes, slightly below their fp128 figure, because their proof is almost entirely inner images and response. The remaining gap to fp128 on dense polynomials is the sum-check messages and the partials, which cannot be narrowed without leaving Fp​. The re-pricing still keeps the fp128 digit counts for t. A native plan would carry 33 digits per t coordinate instead of 85, which shrinks the recursive witness by roughly a sixth per level and lets the planner fold deeper.

This is a protocol change, not a parameter: the relation rows gain the quotient terms, and the knowledge-soundness proof has to be redone with the no-wrap step. LaBinius (ePrint 2026/2103) is an implemented lattice commitment built on exactly this decoupling, a composite NTT-friendly commitment modulus under claims in another field, with an extraction that avoids reduction modulo the commitment modulus by working over the number field; its claim field is binary rather than Pasta, but its soundness argument is the one to adapt. But it is a far smaller change than a 255-bit profile, since every lattice object reuses the 64-bit profile, and it keeps Tachyon’s circuits on Pasta. It gets most of what the consensus-layer two-field split would get, without the second proof system.

6.4 What a tail redesign would still have to beat

Within this structure the only tail quantities are t, e, and z. Decoupling makes t cheap. The partials cost dA​ claim-field elements per block, so a smaller terminal ring dimension cuts them directly, at the cost of a larger challenge-coefficient range for the same soundness. The response is digit-valued already. A LaBRADOR-style change that commits to the partials and folds once more would trade dA​⋅32 bytes per block for the partials’ digits folded into the response, plus a range check. At 85 digits per partial coordinate that trade does not pay. A genuinely smaller tail at a 255-bit claim field would need a way to reveal the block evaluations in fewer than dA​ coordinates, which is a question about the opening representation rather than about folding.

7. What a real Pasta profile needs

In order of effort, from the survey of the code:

  1. A modulus profile in akita-params. SisModulusProfileId::modulus() returns u128, and field_bits() is derived from it, so a 255-bit modulus needs the representation widened first. That touches about fifteen call sites, among them the trivial collision bound in sis/coverage.rs, the physical-ℓ2​ norm route, the challenge-field order used by transcript grinding, and the NTT cache. The enum itself is matched in about fifty non-test files across the workspace, most of them one-line arms.
  2. Generated tables: the CSVs in estimates/, converted to the rust-split module format that generated_sis_table and generated_l2_sis_table consume, plus the table digests.
  3. Compression chain plans for 32-byte coefficients: ring dimensions 8 and 4 for the two maps, so that an 8192-byte image compresses to 256 bytes and then 128, matching the pattern 64/32, 32/16, 16/8 of the shipped profiles.
  4. A preset in akita-config, modeled on proof_optimized/fp128.rs, with the base field as its own challenge field, a 255-bit field type for the offline path, and ring-challenge configurations for each ring dimension. The challenge families and their operator-norm certificates have to be redone for a fully splitting prime.
  5. Response-model calibration for the optimized later-fold schedules needs an implementation to measure; the rigorous bounds of the paper’s Appendix G do not, and give an upper bound.

With 1 to 4 done, gen_schedule_artifacts plans the schedule and the same breakdown function prices it exactly.

8. Conclusions for Ragu with Akita as a split-accumulation PCD scheme

Take Ragu with Akita in place of Pedersen and IPA as a split-accumulation PCD scheme, with zero knowledge as an option provided by folding with a random claim (the zero-knowledge note). The scheme has four kinds of object, and the figures above price each of them. Ragu’s circuits are about 212 gates with committed vectors of 214 coefficients over Pasta, and every figure here is flat from that size upward, so the application’s circuit size is not where the cost comes from.

Accumulator instance. Today’s compressed instance, counted from Ragu’s structs at the checked-in production rank, lists 50 native and 45 nested commitment components. With the registry and accumulator points on each side that is 100 points of 32 bytes, plus 19 scalars and the two headers: 3,812 bytes plus 64 per header element. Of the 95 components, 54 are endoscaling steps and 4 are challenge binders, which exist only because of the curve cycle, and several more are the bridge and nested stages. A lattice instance does not have any of those. It would list on the order of 15 to 20 commitments, compressed to 128 bytes each, about 2.5 kbytes, plus the folded claims and the final claims of the range and norm obligations. With the zero-knowledge option there is one more claim group.

Accumulation proof, one per fold. Akita’s planner does not model Ragu’s accumulation step, but one of Akita’s own non-terminal levels is a fair proxy, since it sends the same kinds of object: compressed commitments and the sum-check transcripts of the range and norm checks. In the re-planned 214 schedule a level costs about 4.4 kbytes at Pasta widths, so a fold’s messages are about 5 kbytes on top of the instance. Today’s edge carries the step’s instance, about 4 kbytes, and no sum-checks. So the per-edge succinct material goes from about 4 kbytes to about 7.5 kbytes, and it is also what each merge circuit absorbs into its transcript.

Accumulator witness. The digit vector that the holder of an accumulator keeps and passes to whoever folds it next: N≈222 digits, about 1.5 Mbytes, for the unmasked vectors. With the zero-knowledge option the first fold masks the witness by rejection sampling, which makes it about 9 times longer, 14 Mbytes, and raises the Module-SIS rank of the accumulation matrix, under which every accumulator is committed, by about 2.4 times (§5 of the zero-knowledge note). This is the one cost that the zero-knowledge option changes materially, and it is paid between provers, not in any proof. The length belongs to the masked accumulator alone: the aggregator’s first fold on it returns to N digits, so the accumulators above the zero-knowledge layer are the unmasked size, and the rank is what every fold pays, in the prover’s commitment work; Akita’s internal levels and terminal keep their own bounds, so the decider’s opening is nearly unchanged.

Decider proof. The Akita opening of the final accumulator, which is what Ragu’s compression produces for a third party. At 214 to 216 coefficients:

commitment modulusopening proof
128-bit field, or Goldilocks for both field and commitments65 to 67 kbytes
Pasta claims, 64-bit commitments (§6.3)71 to 74 kbytes
Pasta for everything (§6.2)83 to 87 kbytes

Today’s compressed proof, counted from the structs at rank 13, is 7,652 bytes plus 64 per header element, 7,908 with a header of four elements. That is the instance above, with the six online openings and two online polynomial commitments that §8.1 describes, and on each curve a revdot reduction of 4 points and 9 scalars, a batch of 1 point and 11 scalars, and an IPA of 13 rounds, 1,696 bytes per curve. Rank 14 adds 128 bytes. The model is ragu-size-model.py beside this note. An independent estimate for a 212 configuration with the endoscaling steps rebalanced gives about 5.9 kbytes. Before the online polynomials, the model gave exactly 5,892 bytes at rank 14 with sixteen endoscalings per step on both sides, one binder, and a two-element header, so that is presumably the configuration found; it is 6,372 bytes now. The extra rank costs 128 bytes, one IPA round per curve. Going from four to eight endoscalings per step removes 37 committed components, 1,184 bytes, and the doubled circuit is what makes room for them; the step from eight to sixteen saves a further 320 bytes, and a third doubling of the circuit would cost another 128 bytes for less than that. Its floor at rank 14 is about 6.2 kbytes, because 4.6 kbytes is independent of every parameter: the 19 instance scalars, the online openings and commitments, both curves’ reduction and batch messages, and both IPAs. Below 5.5 kbytes needs changes to the compression protocol itself, such as fewer opened polynomials, rather than parameter choices. The model also prices the circuit budget (python ragu-size-model.py budget), from the internal circuits’ gate counts pinned in Ragu’s tests: a rank-14 circuit holds eight endoscalings beside a step’s overhead, not sixteen, and the 8 challenges need 2 binders there, so the 6,372-byte configuration does not fit. The smallest proof that fits is 6,468 bytes at rank 14, with the 6 fixed native circuits packed into 3 and the 3 nested into 1, or 6,244 bytes at rank 15 with 16 endoscalings per step, one binder, and the fixed circuits packed into 2 and 1. Rank 16 gains nothing further. Fewer components is also less prover commitment work: rank 14 packed needs fewer word multiplications than today’s rank 13 as well as fewer bytes. Ragu’s several committed vectors open in one Akita batch at no visible cost: the catalog prices two polynomials of 215 within two bytes of one of 216, and a separately committed group adds 0.6 to 2 kbytes. The opening grows by about 0.4 kbytes per doubling of the final witness length, so the masked witness of the zero-knowledge option adds a few kilobytes. A compressed lattice PCD proof is the opening plus the 2.5 kbytes instance: 68 to 90 kbytes, or 9 to 11 times today’s.

Reading the table. The decider proof is 9 to 11 times today’s, and the field width moves it by a quarter at most. The per-edge material is about twice today’s. So the decoupled commitment modulus of §6.3 matters more for the per-edge objects, where it halves the width of every commitment coordinate that the merge circuit absorbs, than for the decider, where it saves 12 kbytes. How often each object is produced is the application’s business.

8.1 Measured Pedersen-IPA size, the group dependence, and prover commitment work in word multiplications

Measured. Ragu’s wire format (crates/ragu_pcd/src/wire/, since its PR #875) encodes a compressed proof field by field at fixed widths behind a 28-byte versioned envelope, and CompressedProof::encoded_len::<R, HEADER_SIZE>() gives the exact length from the rank, the header size and the profile’s component counts. At tachyon-zcash/ragu 8109a41c (2026-11-13) it gives 7,936 bytes at rank 13 with a four-element header, so 7,908 of payload, and 7,808 with a two-element header. Rank 14 adds 128 bytes, one IPA round per curve, so rank 14 with two header elements and rank 13 with four have the same length. The payload is 448 bytes more than the 7,460 counted from the structs at the fork of 2026-10-04, because every proof now carries two online polynomials. Those are their two commitments and the six openings, a point and a value for each slot of the left, right and output proofs. The component counts are otherwise unchanged, 50 native and 45 nested commitments.

Online polynomials are Ragu’s polynomial oracles. A step’s application circuit can open each of the six slots once at the challenge ζ, drawn after the polynomials and the staged values are committed, and so prove an identity between committed polynomials by Schwartz–Zippel without their coefficients entering the circuit. Tachyon uses them for its tachygram set polynomials, whose roots are the set’s members: a step proves that its output accumulator is the product of its children’s, or of its input’s and the factors X−t for the tachygrams it adds, with one opening per operand. Every step pays for all six openings whether its headers use them or not, and so does every compressed proof.

Groups. The planner was run for one final group of 214 plus K separately committed groups of 214 under the decoupled pricing (estimates/ does not include them; they are in work/akita-pasta-out/groups/). Opening bytes at Pasta claims with 64-bit commitments: K=0: 70,725; 1: 74,212; 2: 75,562; 4: 76,804; 8: 77,445; 12: 78,580. The first extra group costs 3.5 kbytes, each further one about 0.4 kbytes. At Pasta for everything the first extra group costs 14 kbytes, because every partial is a 32-byte atom. So how Ragu’s stage polynomials are grouped is the one Ragu parameter that reaches into Akita, and it matters little under decoupling.

So at a 255-bit commitment modulus the Ajtai prover does more wide multiplications per coefficient than Pedersen, because the digit count outweighs the per-point cost. The decoupled modulus is what makes the lattice prover cheaper: about seven times fewer word multiplications per coefficient. And the step commits its circuits as one batch beside five stages, instead of about 100 separate polynomials. Folding and sum-check work is not in these figures; the page’s accumulation prover measure adds the fold’s two sum-checks over the accumulator, at about five field multiplications per digit a pass, to the merge’s commitments. The model and the interactive page are pareto-model.py, pareto-template.html, and pareto.html beside this note. The page plots bytes against two measures chosen from the same seven, the accumulation prover and a whole transaction’s prover by default, or the step’s prover alone, merge gates, absorbed atoms, the decider’s verification, or the accumulator’s memory, for every configuration of every family, with the hashes in circuits selectable between BLAKE3 which is the default, and Poseidon 1.1. It marks the frontier of each class, with native or relation Pasta arithmetic as a requirement in the dominance test, and its Knobs and Circuit budget sections state every assumption behind the numbers.

Circuit budget and rank. The gate counts of the internal circuits, pinned in Ragu’s tests at rank 13, bound the parameters at each rank. A rank-13 circuit of 2,048 gates holds 4 endoscalings per step and binds 2 challenges per binder. Rank 14 holds 8 and 5; rank 15 holds 17 and all 8, so one binder; rank 16 holds 35. Under Pedersen-IPA this makes the merge the main consumer of the rank. Every endoscaling step and binder is its own commitment, so a larger circuit removes components faster than the IPA adds its 128 bytes per rank. The smallest proof that fits is 6,468 bytes at rank 14 with the fixed circuits merged, or 6,244 bytes at rank 15, against 7,780 today, all with two-element headers. Rank 14 also needs fewer word multiplications than today. The application and the merge therefore compete for the rank. Under Akita the merge verifier is spread over as many step circuits of the rank’s size as it needs, and their witnesses open as one batch. Its commitments are therefore nearly flat in rank, while the fold’s sum-checks over the accumulator, K groups of 2^rank coefficients, grow with it; the page’s accumulation prover measure counts both. From rank 13 to 16 the per-circuit evaluation claims save about 160 bytes and a dozen absorbed atoms, against a few percent of prover commitment work spent padding the last circuit. The rank then follows the largest application step. The limits are the padding of every polynomial to 2rank coefficients, the opening’s growth of 0.5 to 2 kbytes per doubling of the batched witness, and the prover’s memory per circuit. The Pareto page shows all of this per configuration, and marks each one as needing parameter changes only, code changes within the protocol, or design changes.

Hash-based comparison. The Pareto page also prices WHIR (ePrint 2024/1586) with ARC accumulation (ePrint 2024/1731) over Pasta’s own field, which is FFT friendly. The proof sizes come from WHIR’s reference implementation at the batched witness size over a 256-bit field, at 128 bits of security in the proven Johnson regime. The conjectured sizes rest on capacity conjectures that were refuted over large fields in late 2025 (ePrint 2026/1367 §5.4), so they do not apply. The measured proofs are 250 kbytes, 158 kbytes and 126 kbytes at rates 1/2, 1/4 and 1/8, with 2.7k, 2.0k and 1.7k verifier hashes. A hash commitment is not homomorphic, so split accumulation does not apply. ARC’s verifier checks 2λ/log2​(1/ρ) Merkle openings per input, 256, 128 and 86 at those rates, each a path through the accumulator’s tree. At one Poseidon permutation per node that makes a merge of 1.3 to 3.7 million gates, 50 times Akita’s estimate and 10 to 30 times today’s. Ragu’s step circuits hold 2,048 gates each at rank 13, so that merge verifier has to be spread over hundreds of them. Committing their witnesses in Poseidon Merkle trees then costs about a hundred times Pedersen-IPA’s prover commitment work. WARP (ePrint 2025/753) has the same opening count for Reed–Solomon codes; its linear-time prover needs linear-time codes and does not help, since hashing rather than encoding is the cost. Basefold is subsumed by WHIR over Reed–Solomon codes, and Zook (ePrint 2026/391) makes zero knowledge free at the IOP level. Moving everything to Goldilocks, with the claims in its cubic extension, cuts the prover by about 5 times and the proof by a sixth, to 315 kbytes to 787 kbytes at rank 13. It grows the merge, because a Goldilocks Poseidon costs more gates per node. A hybrid with a Pasta witness committed as four 64-bit limbs gains nothing, unlike Akita’s decoupled commitment modulus. The Pasta merge circuit forces Pasta’s hash on the trees, and four limbs per coefficient is more hashing, not less. So the hash-based route is dominated by Akita on every measure here. What it offers is a native Pasta field and a mature deployment base. To compete it would need a design with far larger step circuits and a cheaper hash. The binary-field systems of 2026, Flock (arXiv 2607.27491) and Binius64, show what that design looks like. They are Boolean circuits over F2​ with claims in F2128​. Flock proves 82,000 BLAKE3 compressions per second on one core, with proofs of 205 to 395 kbytes at 100 bits. At that rate the Merkle hashes of an ARC merge are about 44 ms of proving, against 276 ms per node today. The price falls on the application: Pasta arithmetic, which the Ironwood recovery circuit is made of, then pays the embedding and simulation overhead that the small-field survey (ePrint 2026/1371, §5) describes. The Pareto page prices the binary-field route in its own terms, with FRI-Binius’s measured commitment proofs at the merge’s witness size and Flock’s measured throughput as prover time. At rate 1/8 it comes to 214 kbytes and the time of 5.8e8 word multiplications per merge. Akita over Goldilocks with Poseidon still dominates it on both, at 73 kbytes and 2.4e7. The merge’s Boolean size, about 33 million AND gates, is not comparable with Pasta gates. LaBinius (ePrint 2026/2103) is a lattice scheme over a binary claim field. It reaches proofs under 103 kbytes with LaBRADOR compression and a prover within twice the hash-based ones, and §6.3 says why it matters here.

Each scheme’s own recursion, and BitZ. At Quang Dao’s suggestion the page also prices every hash-based scheme with full recursion in place of ARC: the merge circuit verifies its two input proofs, each step emits a full proof, and the decider verifies one. The two rows of a scheme differ only in the accumulation. For WHIR the recursion is about a wash at rate 1/8: the verifier’s 2,600 hashes per input match ARC’s 86 openings through 26 levels. At rate 1/2 it saves a fifth of the merge and half of the prover, since the verifier’s queries fall with the rate while ARC’s openings grow with it. FRI’s verifier has twice WHIR’s hashes, so its recursion costs about twice ARC. Ligerito’s verifier, which Flock and BitZ use, opens 491 columns over its five levels at 100 bits, against ARC’s 86 openings at rate 1/8. So its recursion is two to seven times ARC’s hashing, and ARC is the better accumulation for those two. BitZ (ePrint 2026/2141) is a hash-based commitment for multilinears over any finitely generated ring. The witness is bit-decomposed and committed packed over F2128​ with Flock’s Ligerito. A claim over a ring R is lifted to the integers, through a random prime where R is large, and proved in the exponent of F2128​ by batched GKR grand products, at a cost per committed bit that does not depend on R. With the Universal Constraint Systems of Zinc+ (ePrint 2026/855) the application’s Pasta arithmetic is integer relations, integer witnesses with ideal membership modulo p. So the recovery circuit stays Pasta arithmetic at 510 committed bits per gate (a 255-bit value and its quotient). The merge is a hybrid circuit: its hashing is Boolean, and its GKR and PIOP verifiers are native over the binary extension and the projection field. The 128-bit and rate scalings come from running the paper’s own benchmark: at 128 bits its implementation works in the unique-decoding regime with fold grinding, and the Ligerito part of the proof is 1.8 times the 100-bit one. Rate 1/8 is its proof-size minimum: at rate 1/16, the lowest the implementation allows, the proof is 3% larger again and the prover 27% slower. At the Johnson bound the implementation certifies only 118 bits, its out-of-domain binding term with one sample; there the proof is 217 kbytes at 225 and rate 1/2 against 304 kbytes in the unique-decoding regime at 128 bits, and since the Ligerito part grows linearly with the bits, a Johnson-regime BitZ at 128 bits, which analysis in progress may reach, would be about 231 kbytes, a quarter less. The figures below are the certified ones. Priced from the paper’s Table 10 with those scalings and Zinc+‘s PIOP added, BitZ with ARC at rank 13 and rate 1/8 comes to 563 kbytes, 2.4e8 word multiplications, a 33 million bit merge and a 8.30 ms decider. Its prover is the grand-product GKR, 63 of Table 10’s 80 ms at 225 bits on one thread; as Quang Dao puts it, the prover with grand-product GKR is just really expensive. It is on the frontier, because its Pasta arithmetic is one relation per multiplication and no other hash-based family’s row with native or relation Pasta arithmetic has both a smaller proof and a cheaper prover; Kishu, described below, beats it on both. Akita’s Pasta variants beat it on proof size, and its merge is a Boolean witness of the F2​ family’s size. BitZ does not support zero knowledge yet. Quang Dao notes that BitZ’s arithmetization might in future be combined with a lattice backend such as Akita, which would keep its free range checks and claims over any ring while replacing the hash-based commitment. An internal research draft of September 2026 (provisional) supplies the piece that needs: Booleanity of a committed bit table certified over the lattice’s prime field by one quadratic identity plus an integer norm bound from a structured random projection, so that the exponent-lift GKR, 79% of BitZ’s prover, is not needed. Its single-threaded reduction at 227 bits over 264−59 takes 113 ms to prove against 438 ms for the optimized conventional Booleanity sumcheck, with verifiers of 0.85 and 0.023 ms. The page prices it as 255 one-bit digits per Pasta value over Akita’s 64-bit modulus. Its claims are in that modulus’s field, and a Pasta value is recomposed from 32-bit limbs proved as linear claims in the same sum-check, so it does not use the decoupled commitment. Its merge is Akita’s, costed in digits: a bit gate is one digit, and its arithmetic is native over the 64-bit field and its quadratic extension, each product’s output committed as its bits, 128 digits. At rank 17 with BLAKE3 it comes to a proof of 74 kbytes, a merge prover of 4.1e7 word multiplications and a merge of 1.9 million digits in the sum-check form; with Poseidon 74 kbytes, 4.6e7 and 2.2 million digits. The projection form’s verifier scans 3.1 million public coefficients, which recursion cannot afford. So against BitZ at rank 17 its proof is 7.6 times smaller and its merge prover 5.2 to 5.9 times. Against the 64-bit Akita variant, which commits every wire of a hash gate as a 255-bit value, its merge prover under BLAKE3 is 140 times smaller, so under conventional hashes it dominates that variant on every measure but the merge’s count, whose units differ. Quang Dao thinks the prover is likely overestimated here: the commitment rows are sized by the loose security analysis, with its grinding, and a tighter analysis would cut them, so the figure is an upper bound. The page’s measures are the recursion’s, the step’s commitments, the accumulator’s opening and the merge circuit, which every design pays and which can be estimated, whereas which application circuit to benchmark is hard to agree on, since it depends on the application; so the application enters only through the rank. The recovery circuit itself is hash-heavy: its expensive parts are seven BLAKE2b-512 compressions by ZIP 2005’s cost section, beside two Sinsemilla commitments, two full-width Pallas scalar multiplications, a Merkle path, and a BLAKE3 compression in the quantum-key signature. In Kishu a Boolean hash costs one bit per wire, and the integer arithmetic simulates Fp​ without a bit decomposition per operation, with range checks free. Costed component by component in this arithmetization, the recovery circuit is 17 million digits by the limb route or 8.5 million by the fingerprint route, since a Pasta multiplication cannot be checked modulo one fixed field alone, so either its limbs’ carries are committed or BitZ’s random-prime fingerprint runs in three instances whose column sums are committed per step, against 570,000 Pasta gates, which the page’s transaction measure uses.

What is not modeled. Verifier and prover time, which the byte formula does not touch. The accumulation step’s exact message count, for which the fold-level proxy stands in. And of the two native-planning effects of §2, the planner’s own reaction is measured (§3.1, §6.3) and the doubled digit count is not.

9. How much to trust the Module-SIS parameters

Akita’s advantage over the hash-based route rests on Module-SIS, where that route rests on a random oracle alone and Pedersen-IPA on the discrete logarithm. This section states what the parameters assume and where the uncertainty sits.

The sizing rule. Every matrix in Akita’s catalog is sized so that the cheapest known attack on its Module-SIS instance costs at least 2128 operations under the quantum core-SVP model of [ADPS16]. That model charges 20.265β for lattice reduction with BKZ block size β and ignores the polynomial factors. The block size that reaches a given root Hermite factor is taken from the BKZ 2.0 analysis of [CN11]. Every instance in the catalog lands at block size 484 to 486, in lattices of dimension 2,000 to 4,800. The catalog’s own security check reports 128.26 bits on each. A cross-check with the maintained lattice estimator [LE] agrees on the ranks (§5.5 of the zero-knowledge note). The estimator is maintained; the paper that introduced it, [APS15], is not, and its authors say so in a 2019 note. The estimator costs BKZ as 8·n calls to an SVP oracle of the block size, with sieving per [BDGL16] classically and per [LMvdP15] and [Laa16] quantumly. It follows [ADPS16] for the primal attack, as [AGVW17] established, and leaves out the dimensions for free of [Duc18] and [ADH+19]. The same block size under other models:

Cost modelBits at β=485Source
classical sieving, 20.292β142[BDGL16]
quantum sieving, 20.265β, the floor used128[Laa16], as adopted in [ADPS16]
“paranoid” lower bound, 20.2075β101[ADPS16]

For calibration, ML-DSA [FIPS204], the standardized Dilithium, sizes its SIS instances at block sizes of about 423, 638 and 909 for its three security levels [Dil21]. These parameters therefore sit between its levels 2 and 3 on the same model.

Where the uncertainty sits. Three places, in decreasing order of concern.

  • Proof-level slack, not the assumption. Lattice schemes have slipped before by sizing for honest norms rather than the extractor’s. Akita sizes for the extractor’s collision bound, which is why the masked variant needs 340 scalar rows against 143 on the zero-knowledge note’s Euclidean route, and rank 2 against rank 1 at the 64-bit modulus on the ℓ∞​ route the frontier page takes. But the knowledge-soundness proof in the quantum random-oracle model is still in progress. And the decoupled commitment modulus of §6.3 has no soundness argument yet beyond the LaBinius analogue. A gap of that kind moves parameters by a lot, not by twenty bits.
  • The instantiation is unusual. Ring dimension 64 with 128-bit or 255-bit moduli, compression rings of dimension 8 and 4, and norm bounds up to 248 are far from the regime of the standardized schemes. No attack exploits module structure beyond constant factors. Module-SIS has a worst-case reduction [LS15], and the quantum attacks on ideal lattices [CDW17] need principal ideals, which modules of rank 64 and more do not present. But there is less cryptanalytic attention on this corner, and the ℓ∞​ bounds take their own estimator route (§4).
  • The cost model. Core-SVP is already a lower-bound style model. Refinements since 2016, such as the dimensions for free of [Duc18], have moved concrete estimates by ten to twenty bits either way. The disputed dual-attack improvements of [MATZOV22], reassessed in [DP23], concern LWE rather than SIS, which is attacked through the primal route. A 128-bit quantum floor is 142 classical bits, and the paranoid model still gives 101.

Against the other assumptions. The hash-based route’s assumption is weaker in the good sense: a random oracle and collision resistance only. Its soundness risk is concentrated in the proximity-gap conjectures, which is exactly where the 2025 refutations landed (§8.1). The proven Johnson regime is therefore where that route is priced here. Pedersen-IPA rests on the discrete logarithm on Pasta, about 126 classical bits and nothing against a quantum adversary. So the fair statement is that Akita dominates on efficiency while adding one assumption whose concrete hardness is estimated rather than proven. That assumption has SIS studied since [Ajt96], Module-SIS since [LS15], and standardization behind it.

A margin. The Pareto page’s higher floor is 147 quantum bits, which moves the block size from 484 to about 556. It was to be 150. The width tables were generated at 150 bits first (work/akita-150-tables.sh; tables in work/akita-150-out/). There the Euclidean rank ladder alone needs 12 percent more rows (work/akita-pasta-rank-150.log), and the coefficient-L∞ tables, which size the catalog’s matrices, move more. Where the 147-bit floor binds, the median shrink of the maximum secure width, over the cells where neither width is at the estimator’s search cap and the width falls, is a factor of 2 to 3 at ring dimensions up to 32, 4 to 10 at 64, 4 to 13 at 128 and 256, and 6 to 15 at 512 and above, the 32-bit modulus at the low end of each range. The exception is the largest dimension of each table, where it is about 60. The q64 and Goldilocks tables each have 83 small cells with no secure width at all. But a re-plan at 150 bits cannot run. Akita’s compression chain is built from rank-one maps, and at the catalog’s geometry its first map, at ring dimension 16 and width 4,096, certifies at 147.3 bits; the 150-bit cell allows width 3,806. Raising the maps to rank two, or splitting them, would double the compressed images at every level. So the floor is 147 bits, the most the rank-one maps allow. At that floor Akita’s catalog is re-planned by its own planner, in a checkout whose single policy, runtime width tables and compression cells are retargeted to 147 bits (2026-11-12). The page’s openings there are therefore planned rather than scaled. The planner does not give the prover’s rows term or the verifier’s inner-image share. For those the page’s model takes the 115 weakest Module-SIS instances in the shipped catalog’s security report (work/akita-pasta-out/catalog-security.tsv) and, for each, the smallest module rank that covers the instance’s width at both floors. With the catalog’s own mix of moduli the ranks sum to 121 at 128 bits and to 150 at 147 bits, a factor of 1.24, with 29 instances moving up a rank; at 150 bits the sum was 154, a factor of 1.27. Over a 64-bit modulus, where the ranks are higher to begin with, the sums are 185 and 211, a factor of 1.14, with 26 moving; at 150 bits 215, a factor of 1.16. The q64 and Goldilocks tables are identical for these cells. Over Pallas the instances sit at the width cap at both floors but one: the sums are 117 and 118, a factor of 1.01. WHIR’s proofs, measured with the reference implementation over both fields, are 16 percent larger at 147 bits than at 128 and its verifier’s hashes 12 percent more, through λ alone; ARC’s openings grow by 15 percent. Pedersen-IPA over Pasta rests on the discrete logarithm in a group of order near 2^254: about 126 classical bits by Pollard rho, and nothing against a quantum adversary. The page shows it at the 128-bit floor as the conventional pre-quantum baseline. It drops out at 147 bits, which it does not reach even classically.
